\abstract{Gene-set and pathway analyses can improve interpretability and power in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) by aggregating many modest genetic effects, but valid inference is challenging because gene- and pathway-level statistics are strongly correlated due to linkage disequilibrium (LD), shared annotations, and overlapping pathways. We introduce CATFISH, a correlation-aware pathway analysis framework using LD-adjusted MAGMA gene-level summary statistics ($p$-values and/or Z-scores). CATFISH computes multiple pathway-level tests—ACAT, Fisher’s combination test, adaptive soft TFisher, minP/Tippett’s method, and Stouffer’s method—and combines them via an omnibus operator that maintains sensitivity across diverse enrichment patterns. To control false positives under dependence across genes and across tests, CATFISH calibrates the omnibus statistic against a pathway-specific, LD-informed multivariate normal (MVN) null built from MAGMA-based gene–gene correlation matrices. For $p$-value–based components, CATFISH uses a Gaussian-copula transform to embed $p$-values in the MVN framework. Simulations across LD structures and pathway sizes show that standard independence-based calibrations can be anti-conservative for several component tests and naïve omnibus schemes. In contrast, MVN-based calibration achieves near-nominal type I error while retaining strong power for sparse, diffuse, and heterogeneous enrichment architectures. In a sorghum GWAS of stem volume, CATFISH identifies pathways passing Bonferroni and FDR thresholds and supports multi-layer candidate gene prioritization by integrating SNP-, gene-, and pathway-level evidence. CATFISH thus provides a dependence-calibrated, architecture-robust framework for pathway discovery and biological interpretation in GWAS.}

\keywords{genome-wide association study, pathway analysis, gene-set test, $p$-value combination, LD-aware calibration, ACAT, MAGMA}

\boxedtext{Key Messages}{
\begin{itemize}
\item CATFISH integrates multiple complementary pathway-level tests (ACAT, Fisher’s method, TFisher, minimum $p$-value, Stouffer’s Z) to maintain power across sparse, diffuse, and heterogeneous enrichment architectures.
\item To address correlation among gene-level signals and pathway statistics induced by LD, CATFISH uses LD-aware multivariate normal (MVN) calibration with MAGMA-derived gene–gene correlation matrices to obtain well-calibrated omnibus $p$-values.
\item Simulations and stress tests show that analytic calibrations assuming independence become anti-conservative under correlated nulls, whereas MVN-based recalibration maintains type I error near nominal levels.
\item In a sorghum stem-volume GWAS, CATFISH identifies interpretable pathways and supports multilayer candidate gene prioritization by integrating SNP-, gene-, and pathway-level evidence.
\end{itemize}}

\section{1.1 Introduction}

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have transformed complex trait research by identifying thousands of loci linked to disease, development, and quantitative phenotypes. Yet most variants explain only a small fraction of phenotypic variance and often fall in noncoding regions, limiting single-marker mechanistic interpretation \citep{Manolio2009,Boyle2017}. Complex traits are often highly polygenic, with relevant signals distributed across many modest-effect loci rather than concentrated in a few genome-wide significant variants. Thus, gene-set analysis, including a pathway-based approach, is a key post-GWAS strategy for translating summary statistics into biological hypotheses. By aggregating evidence across predefined groups of functionally related genes, pathway-based methods enhance interpretability, reduce the multiple-testing burden, and reveal coordinated pathway disruptions that single-locus analyses may miss \citep{Mooney2015,deLeeuw2015}.


A plethora of pathway analysis tools has been developed for GWAS. Classical over-representation methods test whether predefined pathways are enriched among significant genes, as implemented in DAVID, g:Profiler, and WebGestalt \citep{Sherman2022,Kolberg2023,Liao2019}. Ranking-based methods such as Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) assess whether pathway genes are non-randomly concentrated at the top or bottom of a ranked gene list \citep{Subramanian2005}. GWAS-focused extensions, including MAGENTA, INRICH, and MAGMA, add features such as SNP-to-gene mapping, pathway-level modeling, and adjustment for linkage disequilibrium (LD) or gene size \citep{Segre2010,Lee2012,deLeeuw2015}. Several widely used post-GWAS methods already account for LD through model-based or reference-based dependence estimates rather than assuming independence. For example, VEGAS uses multivariate normal simulation, Pascal uses LD from a reference population to compute gene and pathway scores, and MAGMA models gene-level dependence through an approximated correlation structure \citep{Liu2010,deLeeuw2015,Lamparter2016,Watanabe2017}. Together, these methods have made pathway analysis a standard element of post-GWAS interpretation. In practice, pathway tests are often applied to correlated gene-level summaries and then compared or combined without a dependence-aware calibration step, which can inflate false positives under LD. CATFISH addresses this by calibrating the entire multi-test workflow under a pathway-specific dependence model.

Despite these advances, major challenges still remain. Existing methods differ substantially in their assumptions about SNP-to-gene mapping, null hypothesis specification, LD modeling and adjustment, and their vulnerability to biases from pathway size, gene density, and annotation quality \citep{Mooney2015,Debrabant2017}. Self-contained and competitive testing frameworks also address fundamentally different biological questions, yet their results are often treated as directly comparable. In addition, pathway databases are incomplete and inconsistently curated, and genes are frequently assigned to multiple overlapping pathways, creating redundancy and complicating interpretation. Benchmarking studies show marked performance variability across enrichment methods, indicating that no single approach is optimal for all genetic architectures \citep{Geistlinger2021}. Together, these issues create a practical gap in GWAS pathway analysis. Real datasets can show pathway signals that vary widely in sparsity, concentration, and overall enrichment, whereas many existing methods perform well only for a limited subset of these patterns.

We developed CATFISH (Combining \textbf{C}auchy combination test (ACAT), \textbf{A}daptive \textbf{T}Fisher (soft) test, \textbf{F}isher's test, m\textbf{I}n-P, and \textbf{S}touffer's method for \textbf{H}olistic pathway analysis), a correlation-aware pathway analysis framework that integrates multiple complementary gene-set statistics in a unified omnibus strategy. Different pathway tests are sensitive to different genetic architectures. Some are best at detecting broad, distributed enrichment, whereas others are more sensitive to sparse strong signals or heterogeneous evidence. Instead of relying on a single summary statistic and signal, CATFISH evaluates each pathway with several complementary tests and combines them in an omnibus framework. This improves robustness when the true signal configuration is unknown while preserving the interpretability of the component tests, providing a flexible, broadly sensitive alternative for pathway discovery in GWAS-based studies. Here, we use pathway and network interchangeably.

\section*{1.2 Pathway signal archetypes}

CATFISH was developed to capture diverse pathway signal patterns, from single-gene drivers to weak, broadly distributed enrichment. It offers a unified framework that summarizes these heterogeneous patterns and produces robust pathway-level results without requiring prior knowledge of the signal architecture. To systematize these distinct behaviors, we define five archetypal patterns of pathway signal and then use simulation studies to demonstrate that the component tests within CATFISH exhibit distinct and interpretable response profiles for each archetype.

\subsection*{1.2.1 Statistical signatures and biological intuition}

We divide pathway signals into a set of archetypes that describe different ways a pathway can be enriched in a biological sense, as explained in detail below. We then use a combination of statistical tests chosen to be representative of each of these behaviors and provide a biological example for each archetype (Figure \ref{fig:supp_archetypes_rankcurves}).

\subsubsection{1.2.1.1 Archetype I --- Sparse Driver Architecture (SDA)}

Sparse Driver Architecture (SDA) describes pathway patterns in which the overall signal is dominated by a small subset of strongly associated genes, while the majority of genes within the pathway exhibit behavior consistent with the null hypothesis. Formally, under SDA there exists a subset of genes with extremely small ordered $p$-values, $p_{(1)}, \dots, p_{(K)} \ll \alpha$, followed by a large collection of genes whose $p$-values, $p_{(K+1)}, \dots, p_{(G)}$, are approximately distributed as $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$. The ranked gene-level $p$-value curve therefore shows a pronounced elbow (Fig 1A) that suggests the association is driven by a small number of genes rather than by widespread enrichment across the entire pathway.

From a biological perspective, SDA is expected when the behavior of a pathway is governed predominantly by one or a small number of bottleneck genes, such as a committed-step enzyme, a rate-limiting regulatory factor, or an essential transporter, such that genetic effects are concentrated at a limited set of key control points \citep{MorenoSanchez2008, Naz2023, Tanner2018, Song2020}. A canonical example is aspartokinase in the aspartate-derived amino acid biosynthetic pathway. This pathway converts aspartate into the essential amino acids lysine, threonine, methionine, and isoleucine \citep{Galili2011}. Its initial reaction is catalyzed by aspartokinase, which phosphorylates aspartate to generate aspartyl-phosphate \citep{Hasenoehrl2019}. As this enzyme functions at the entry point of a branched pathway, variation in its activity can influence flux across the entire network, whereas the effects of many downstream enzymes are more distributed or buffered. This network architecture is therefore expected to produce a sparse association pattern in which one or a few genes exhibit very strong signals, while the majority of genes remain close to null. Within CATFISH, pathways of the SDA type are most efficiently detected by ACAT and minP.

\subsubsection{1.2.1.2 Archetype II --- Coordinated Moderate Enrichment (CME)}

Coordinated Moderate Enrichment (CME) refers to architectures in which a large number of genes exhibit modest yet non-negligible statistical associations, without any single gene exerting a disproportionately strong influence on the overall signal. Here, we assume a substantial proportion of genes attain moderately small $p$-values, typically on the order of $10^{-3}$ to $0.05$, and the most highly ranked genes are not several orders of magnitude more significant than the bulk of the distribution. Consequently, the ordered gene-level $p$-value profile is characterized by a broad shoulder, indicating a diffuse enrichment pattern.

From a biological standpoint, CME is most likely in pathways whose output reflects the collective behavior of many interacting components rather than a single dominant effector. Cytokine-driven immune signaling is a canonical example. Cytokine activity is shaped by redundancy and pleiotropy, with related ligands often sharing receptors, downstream signaling modules, and overlapping transcriptional programs, while the overall output is further shaped by cell type--specific context and multilayered feedback (Jiang et al., 2021; Megha et al., 2021). A representative case is TNF$\alpha$/IL-1$\beta$--to--NF-$\kappa$B signaling, where responses propagate through sequential receptor engagement, adaptor recruitment, kinase cascade activation, and transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation, rather than a single determinative node (Verstrepen et al., 2008; van Loo and Beyaert, 2011). In these pathways, inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-$\alpha$ and IL-1$\beta$ signal via their receptors to activate intracellular modules including NF-$\kappa$B and MAPK, whose downstream kinases act across multiple regulatory tiers, including chromatin remodeling, mRNA stability, and translational control (Cargnello and Roux, 2011). Consequently, genetic variation in such pathways is more likely to produce many modest perturbations distributed across the network than a single large-effect change. Within CATFISH, this architecture should favor statistical procedures that aggregate consistent moderate signals across many genes, particularly Fisher's and Stouffer's methods, with soft TFisher also performing well when enrichment is concentrated in a moderately sized subset of pathway genes.

\subsubsection*{1.2.1.3 Archetype III --- Diffuse Polygenic Enrichment (DPE)}

Diffuse Polygenic Enrichment (DPE) denotes architectures in which genes exhibit, on average, slightly stronger association evidence than the genome-wide background, even though few or none individually surpass conventional significance thresholds. In such pathways, most gene-level $p$-values remain greater than 0.05, yet the aggregate evidence shows a subtle but systematic departure from the null distribution. This pattern is reflected as a mild excess of modestly small $p$-values and a gentle downward deflection in the ranked $p$-value curve relative to its null expectation. DPE therefore represents a broadly distributed, weak pathway-wide enrichment pattern across many genes.

From a biological perspective, DPE corresponds to canonical polygenic architectures in which pathway-level signals emerge from the cumulative contribution of many small effects. Human height is a classic example, as it is influenced by thousands of variants across many loci, while most individual variant or gene effects are small (Guo et al., 2018; Yengo et al., 2022). Instead, biologically coherent pathways, such as TGF-$\beta$ signaling, Hedgehog signaling, and genes involved in the growth-plate extracellular matrix, may exhibit enrichment only at the level of cumulative pathway-wide association (Lango Allen et al., 2010; Guo et al., 2018). Under this regime, methods that are sensitive to distributed enrichment across many genes, such as Stouffer’s method and Fisher’s method, are more congruent with the underlying biological architecture (de Leeuw et al., 2015).

\subsubsection{1.2.1.4 Archetype IV --- Hybrid Driver--Support (HDS)}

Hybrid Driver–Support (HDS) denotes architectures in which a limited number of genes exhibit strong or very strong association signals, while a broader set of additional genes provides more moderate evidence of association. The ranked gene-level profile combines features of both sparse and coordinated patterns. One or a few top genes show very strong significance, several others show moderate signals, and the rest are essentially null. This results in a pronounced peak at the top of the ranking, accompanied by supporting signal, positioning HDS, on statistical grounds, between the SDA and the CME models.

From a biological standpoint, HDS is expected to arise in pathways with a few core regulatory elements that have large, pathway-defining effects, embedded in a broader network of auxiliary components that provide secondary modulation. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol metabolism exemplifies this structure. Rare, high-effect variants in genes such as \textit{LDLR}, \textit{APOB}, and \textit{PCSK9} are primary determinants of monogenic familial hypercholesterolemia, whereas population-level variation in LDL-C is further shaped by a polygenic background of common alleles with small to moderate effects across many LDL-modulating genes (Umlai et al., 2025; Peloso et al., 2022). Consistent with this, GWAS of lipoprotein traits implicate a broader set of genes involved in lipoprotein metabolism and remodeling, including \textit{ABCA1}, \textit{LIPC}, \textit{CETP}, \textit{PLTP}, \textit{ANGPTL3}, and \textit{SORT1}. These observations suggest that pathway-level association signals often reflect a few dominant loci superimposed on more distributed supporting effects (Chasman et al., 2009). Within CATFISH, this mixed genetic architecture is expected to be well captured by methods that accommodate both strong, localized signals and more widespread moderate enrichment, particularly adaptive soft TFisher and Fisher’s method.

\subsubsection{1.2.1.5 Archetype V --- Single-Gene Proxy Pathway (SGP)}

The Single-Gene Proxy (SGP) pattern describes pathways that achieve statistical significance predominantly due to the presence of a single, highly associated gene, while the remaining genes are essentially null. In such cases, the leading gene exhibits an extremely small \(p\)-value, whereas the rest of the pathway shows negligible enrichment. Accordingly, the apparent pathway-level association largely disappears once this single gene is excluded from the analysis. From a statistical perspective, this configuration can be viewed as a boundary case of SDA. However, its substantive interpretation differs, as the pathway-level finding provides little additional information beyond what is already captured by the underlying gene-level association.

From a biological perspective, SGP is expected when a pathway is dominated by a single major driver gene, particularly when the annotation is small or when overlapping pathway definitions repeatedly capture the same gene. Phenylalanine metabolism provides a representative example. \textit{PAH} encodes phenylalanine hydroxylase, the enzyme that catalyzes the rate-limiting conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine, and pathogenic variation in \textit{PAH} is the principal cause of phenylketonuria (PKU) and most cases of PAH deficiency \citep{Flydal2013PAH,Elhawary2022PKU}. Although broader phenylalanine-metabolism annotations may also include genes involved in tetrahydrobiopterin synthesis, recycling, chaperoning, or related metabolic processes, these represent secondary or rarer contributors relative to the dominant role of \textit{PAH} in classic hyperphenylalaninemia. Consequently, pathway-level significance in such settings may largely reflect the influence of a single major driver rather than coordinated signal distributed across the pathway. Within CATFISH, this architecture is therefore expected to be captured most readily by minP and ACAT. However, such findings should be interpreted cautiously as primarily gene-centric associations, rather than as strong evidence that the pathway as a whole is jointly implicated.

\section{2 Methods}

\subsection{2.1 Archetype-recovery and effect-size sensitivity simulations}

We conducted  simulation studies to assess whether CATFISH component tests recover the proposed pathway signal archetypes and to evaluate how these response profiles change with pathway size and effect magnitude. CATFISH uses non-directional MAGMA gene-level inputs in the present study. Thus, all simulated alternatives were defined in terms of the magnitude and distribution of pathway enrichment rather than signed positive versus negative gene effects. In principle, other pathway-level combination statistics based on gene-level $p$-values could also be incorporated within the same framework, provided they are recomputed under the same LD-aware multivariate normal (MVN) null and calibrated empirically in the same manner as the current CATFISH components.

\subsection{2.1.1 Archetype-recovery}

We conducted a targeted simulation study with correlated gene-level associations to assess whether CATFISH component tests can statistically identify the proposed pathway signal archetypes. Our aims were to determine if each archetype preferentially improves its corresponding component test and whether these method-specific performance profiles remain interpretable across pathway sizes. CATFISH uses non-directional MAGMA gene-level inputs in our study. Thus, all simulated alternatives are defined in terms of the magnitude and distribution of pathway enrichment rather than signed positive versus negative gene effects. In principle, other pathway-level combination statistics based on gene-level $p$-values could also be incorporated within the same framework, provided they are recomputed under the same LD-aware multivariate normal (MVN) null and calibrated empirically in the same manner as the current CATFISH components \citep{Liu2019ACAT}.

\subsubsection*{2.1.1.1 Simulation design}

We performed a correlated MVN power/ranking simulation under
\[
Z \sim N(\mu,\Sigma),
\]
where $\Sigma$ is an exchangeable correlation matrix with pairwise correlation \(\rho = 0.20\), and \(\mu\) specifies the archetype-specific enrichment profile on the simulated gene-level \(Z\)-score scale. In this setting, departures from zero represent stronger aggregate association evidence rather than signed positive versus negative biological effects. In these simulations, we considered pathway sizes $m \in \{5, 20, 50\}$ and generated 500 independent replicates per archetype for each $m$. For each replicate, we applied the five CATFISH component tests (ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, and Stouffer) together with the omnibus test. Performance was assessed using (i) power at $\alpha = 0.05$, (ii) the mean within-replicate rank of each method’s pathway-level $p$-value (lower is better), and (iii) the top-method probability, defined as the probability that a method yields the smallest pathway-level $p$-value among all methods within a replicate (Supp.~Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_blockA_archetype_recovery_by_m}). To preserve each archetype’s qualitative structure across sizes, we scaled signal layers proportionally with \(m\) rather than fixing the absolute number of affected genes.

\subsubsection*{2.1.2 Effect-size sensitivity simulation design}

To characterize effect-size sensitivity within each pathway signal family, we repeated the correlated MVN simulations across the same five archetypes (SDA, CME, DPE, HDS, SGP) and pathway sizes \(m \in \{5,20,50\}\), using effect-size multipliers \{0.5,\,0.8,\,1.0,\,1.2,\,1.5,\,1.8\} applied to the baseline archetype-specific mean-shift layers. For each \((m,\text{archetype},\text{effect})\) setting, we generated 500 replicates and computed ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, Stouffer, and omnibus \(p\)-values using MVN null calibration at \(\alpha=0.05\). In addition to empirical power, we summarized omnibus performance using power regret,
\[
\max_j \mathrm{Power}_j - \mathrm{Power}_{\mathrm{Omnibus}},
\]
computed within each setting, where \(j\) indexes the component tests.

\subsection{2.2 Gene-level inputs from MAGMA}

GWAS summary statistics were aggregated into gene-level association metrics using MAGMA’s LD-aware SNP-wise gene model, employing an external reference panel to characterize local LD structure \citep{deLeeuw2015MAGMA}. SNPs were mapped to genes based on gene boundaries, optionally extended by flanking regions, resulting in per-gene outputs that included a gene-level $p$-value, $p_g$, and an association-strength $Z$ statistic, $Z_g$. To mitigate residual confounding attributable to gene length and SNP density, gene-level $Z$ statistics were regressed on $\log(L_g)$ and $\log(S_g)$, where $L_g$ denotes gene length and $S_g$ the number of mapped SNPs. The residuals from this regression were then transformed back into adjusted gene-level $p$-values (Appendix A2.3). The adjusted gene-level association evidence was subsequently used as input to CATFISH.

\subsection{2.3 Overview of component tests}

For a pathway $S$ containing $G = |S|$ genes, let $p_g$ denote the gene-level association $p$-value for gene $g \in S$, and let $Z_g$ denote the corresponding gene-level $Z$-statistic when available. We define $\mathbf{p}_S = (p_1, \dots, p_G)$ and $\mathbf{Z}_S = (Z_1, \dots, Z_G)$ as the vectors of gene-level evidence within pathway $S$. These quantities constitute the common inputs for all CATFISH component tests as well as for the omnibus calibration procedure. An overview of the CATFISH analysis workflow is provided in Fig.~\ref{fig:catfish_workflow}, illustrating the generation of gene-level inputs, component pathway tests, and dependence-aware omnibus calibration under the LD-aware MVN null model. A detailed description of the model is provided in Appendix A2.

Each pathway in CATFISH is evaluated using a panel of complementary component tests, comprising ACAT, Fisher’s method, adaptive soft TFisher, minP, and Stouffer’s method. These five tests were chosen because together they span the main pathway signal archetypes considered in this study, while remaining simple, interpretable, and directly applicable to gene-level $p$-values or $Z$-scores without requiring external weights or pathway-specific tuning parameters. Specifically, ACAT and minP are most powerful for sparse architectures dominated by one or a small number of highly significant genes, whereas Fisher’s and Stouffer’s methods are better suited to detecting broader enrichment patterns involving many modestly associated genes. The adaptive soft TFisher test provides an intermediate, tail-adaptive compromise between these extremes. Formal mathematical definitions of all component statistics are given in Appendix A2.4.

\subsection{2.4 Dependence structure and motivation for null calibration}

CATFISH component tests are not statistically independent for two main reasons. First, each component statistic is a deterministic function of the same within-pathway gene-level evidence, which induces dependence across methods, even if the genes are mutually independent. Second, gene-level evidence within a pathway is often correlated due to LD, overlapping SNP-to-gene mappings, and shared local genomic architecture, further strengthening dependence among component tests and any cross-method selection procedures (see Appendix 2.5 for details).

To address this, CATFISH interprets analytically derived omnibus statistics that aggregate across methods and derives final $p$-values using a null calibration procedure that explicitly preserves dependence. CATFISH provides two such dependence-aware null calibration strategies. The primary inferential approach is LD-aware MVN calibration, which, by construction, preserves pathway-specific gene–gene dependence as well as cross-method dependence. MAGMA gene-set analysis is explicitly built on the idea that genes are dependent due to LD, and it addresses this by modeling gene-level residuals as multivariate normal with correlation set to a gene–gene correlation matrix computed during gene analysis \citep{deLeeuw2015MAGMA}. This provides a principled motivation for using an MVN-based null generator when you subsequently apply alternative gene-set statistics to MAGMA gene outputs. In addition, CATFISH offers a global gene-set resampling mode, which maintains the empirical genome-wide distribution of gene-level test statistics and cross-method dependence but does not explicitly retain the within-pathway LD structure. As the MVN-based approach more directly captures pathway-specific dependence, we recommend it as the default calibration strategy while presenting the global resampling approach in Appendix A2.7.1 as an auxiliary option for organisms with no LD. 

\subsection{2.5 Assumptions and LD-aware MVN omnibus calibration}

Following MAGMA, CATFISH adopts the MAGMA-derived pathway gene--gene correlation matrix $R_S$ as the working null covariance for pathway-level gene statistics. This matrix is used to approximate within-pathway dependence under the null and to calibrate the omnibus statistic using dependence-preserving MVN resampling. Similar LD-informed covariance modeling is widely used in GWAS gene- and pathway-level methods \citep{deLeeuw2015MAGMA}. Accordingly, the validity of the CATFISH MVN calibration depends on the following assumptions.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Approximate MVN assumption at the gene level.}  
    Under the null, the pathway-level vector of gene statistics is treated as approximately multivariate normal, that is,
    \[
    Z^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_S).
    \]
    This provides the basis for simulating null gene-level score vectors while preserving pathway-specific dependence.

    \item \textbf{Correlation-matrix adequacy assumption.}  
    The pathway-specific matrix $R_S$, based on MAGMA gene--gene correlations, serves as an LD-informed working approximation to within-pathway dependence under the null. Since the exact gene-level covariance is not directly observed, $R_S$ is not treated as exact. Rather, its practical adequacy is evaluated indirectly through downstream calibration and robustness analyses.

    \item \textbf{Residual-confounding assumption.}  
    CATFISH assumes that the gene-level inputs have already been adjusted so that pathway signals are not driven primarily by structural confounders such as gene size or SNP density.
\end{enumerate}

To account simultaneously for correlation among genes within a pathway and dependence among the component pathway tests, CATFISH calibrates the final omnibus statistic under this pathway-specific MVN null. For a pathway $S=\{g_1,\dots,g_d\}$, let $R_S$ denote the corresponding gene--gene correlation matrix \citep{deLeeuw2015MAGMA}. Under the LD-aware null, CATFISH generates replicate null gene-level score vectors as
\[
Z^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_S), \qquad b=1,\dots,B.
\]

For $p$-based component statistics, null gene-level $p$-values are obtained from the same latent MVN draw via a Gaussian-copula mapping. Under the default two-sided convention,
\[
U_g^{(b)}=\Phi\!\left(Z_g^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
p_g^{(b)} = 2\min\{U_g^{(b)},\,1-U_g^{(b)}\},
\]
where $\Phi(\cdot)$ denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function. This ensures that both $Z$-based and $p$-based component tests are recomputed from a common dependence-preserving null realization.

For each replicate, CATFISH recomputes all component pathway tests from the same simulated pathway and forms the replicate component vector
\[
\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S)
=
\Big(
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Fisher}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{TFisher}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{minP}}^{(b)}(S)
\Big),
\]
with the observed component vector $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)$ defined analogously from the real data. CATFISH then applies a prespecified omnibus operator $\mathcal{O}(\cdot)$, such as ACAT across methods or a minP-type across method operator, to obtain
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S)\right),
\qquad
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)\right).
\]

The final MVN-calibrated omnibus $p$-value is estimated empirically as
\[
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)\le p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}.
\]

This calibration targets the null distribution of the complete pathway-analysis procedure rather than the marginal null distribution of each component test considered in isolation. Since all component statistics are recomputed from the same simulated pathway in each replicate, the MVN procedure preserves both within-pathway gene dependence and cross-method dependence by construction. In practice, CATFISH fixes the pathway-specific correlation matrix $R_S$ and repeatedly simulates correlated null gene-score vectors from this matrix. Details on the construction, stabilization, and factorization of $R_S$ are provided in Appendix~A2.7.2, and the full calibration procedure is summarized in Algorithm~1.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{LD-aware MVN calibration for pathway $S$}
\label{alg:mvn_calibration}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Pathway $S=\{g_1,\dots,g_d\}$, pairwise gene-correlation input, number of replicates $B$, omnibus operator $f_{\mathrm{omni}}$
\Ensure Calibrated omnibus $p$-value $\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)$

\State Construct the pathway-specific correlation matrix $R_S$
\State Set diagonal entries to 1
\State Fill off-diagonal entries with available pairwise correlations $r_{ij}$
\State Set missing pairs to 0
\State Clip extreme correlations if needed (e.g.\ $|r_{ij}|\le 0.999$)
\State Enforce positive-definiteness of $R_S$ if needed

\State Compute the observed component pathway $p$-values for $S$
\State Compute the observed omnibus value
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
=
f_{\mathrm{omni}}\!\left(\{p_j^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)\}\right)
\]

\For{$b=1,\dots,B$}
    \State Simulate correlated null gene-level scores
    \[
    Z^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,R_S)
    \]
    \State Map the same latent draw to null gene-level $p$-values using a Gaussian copula
    \[
    U_g^{(b)}=\Phi\!\left(Z_g^{(b)}\right),
    \qquad
    p_g^{(b)} = 2\min\{U_g^{(b)},\,1-U_g^{(b)}\}
    \]
    \State Recompute all $p$-based component tests from $\{p_g^{(b)}\}$
    \State Recompute Stouffer from $\{Z_g^{(b)}\}$
    \State Combine replicate component $p$-values using the same omnibus rule:
    \[
    p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)
    =
    f_{\mathrm{omni}}\!\left(\{p_j^{(b)}(S)\}\right)
    \]
\EndFor

\State Estimate the calibrated omnibus $p$-value as
\[
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)\le p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}
\]

\State \Return $\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{2.6 Multiple testing correction}

Across all pathways, the final omnibus $p$-values were adjusted using the BH false discovery rate procedure. Since the final omnibus $p$-value is calibrated with respect to the entire multi-test selection and combination scheme, we report a single pathway-level $p$-value for each pathway. Subsequently, multiple corrections across pathways are performed using the BH-FDR procedure.

\subsection{2.7 Omnibus pathway combination across methods}

Let
\[
\mathcal{P}(S)=
\left\{
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Fisher}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{TFisher}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{minP}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S)
\right\}
\]
denote the available component pathway $p$-values for pathway $S$, with $K=|\mathcal{P}(S)| \le 5$. CATFISH provides two omnibus combination rules: (i) ACAT-O, which aggregates method-specific $p$-values via a Cauchy-based transformation, and (ii) minP-O, which takes the minimum $p$-value across methods. These analytic omnibus values define the within-pathway cross-method summary statistic to be calibrated, but they are not used directly for final inference because the component tests are dependent. Instead, CATFISH recomputes the same omnibus operator under the LD-aware MVN null and uses the resulting empirical omnibus $p$-value as the final pathway-level inferential quantity. Multiple testing correction is then applied across pathways to these final calibrated omnibus $p$-values using the BH FDR procedure. Analytic omnibus values may still be reported as descriptive summaries or exploratory rankings, but inferential conclusions are based on the MVN-calibrated omnibus $p$-values. Details are provided in Appendix A2.7.2.6.

\subsection{2.8 Candidate-gene prioritization}

To prioritize genes beyond top-SNP, MAGMA, or top-pathway evidence alone, we integrated support across these three complementary layers. For each gene, we summarized multi-layer support using a support-count plus strength score that gives primary weight to agreement across layers and secondary weight to continuous evidence strength within each layer. Genes supported by at least two layers were prioritized for downstream interpretation. The exact scoring rule is given in Appendix~A3.

\section{3 Results and discussion}


\subsection*{3.1 Archetype-specific response profiles across pathway size and effect magnitude}

Figures~\ref{fig:supp_blockA_archetype_recovery_by_m}, \ref{fig:blockH_power}, and \ref{fig:blockH_regret} report two complementary analyses. First, fixed-parameter archetype recovery (Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_blockA_archetype_recovery_by_m}) is assessed using: (i) empirical power at \(\alpha = 0.05\); (ii) mean within-replicate rank (lower is better); and (iii) the “top-method” probability, i.e., the chance that a method attains the smallest pathway-level \(p\)-value in a replicate. Second, effect-size sensitivity (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_power}) is evaluated by varying the archetype-specific mean-shift scale across pathway sizes (\(m = 5, 20, 50\)), characterizing how performance changes with signal strength. 
Overall robustness of the omnibus procedure is summarized via power regret (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_regret}), defined as
\[
\max_{j \in \mathcal{M}} \mathrm{Power}_j - \mathrm{Power}_{\mathrm{Omnibus}},
\]
where
\[
\mathcal{M}=\{\mathrm{ACAT}, \mathrm{Fisher}, \mathrm{TFisher}, \mathrm{minP}, \mathrm{Stouffer}\}
\]
indexes the component tests. Here, smaller values indicate that the omnibus more closely matches the best-performing component method.

\subsubsection{3.1.1 Sparse architectures remain the most stable and consistently favor tail-sensitive methods.}

We first summarize the fixed-parameter archetype-recovery results (Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_blockA_archetype_recovery_by_m}) using the three metrics explained above. The clearest, most consistent patterns occur under SDA and SGP. Across pathway sizes, SDA is dominated by minP and ACAT. For \(m=5\), minP has the highest power (0.762), with ACAT close behind (0.720), and minP most often ranks first (top-method probability 0.504). For \(m=20\), minP and ACAT again have the highest power (0.920 and 0.900), but ACAT most often yields the smallest \(p\)-value (0.710). For \(m=50\), ACAT and minP have nearly identical power (0.958 and 0.964), yet ACAT overwhelmingly dominates top-method probability (0.882 vs.\ 0.048). Thus, under the SDA, extreme-tail--sensitive procedures consistently dominate, with minP maximizing rejection probability and ACAT increasingly dominating top-method probability as pathway size grows. Although the omnibus is not the leading SDA method, it is still well powered. The results show a specialization pattern. ACAT/minP usually gives the smallest pathway-level \(p\)-values, while the omnibus is slightly less powerful and becomes clearly less competitive only in harder sparse settings (notably larger pathways with moderate effect sizes).

This pattern is even stronger under SGP. For \(m=5\), SGP is detected almost exclusively by ACAT, minP, and TFisher, with minP having the highest power (0.958) and ACAT the highest top-method probability (0.772). For \(m=20\), minP again has the highest power (0.908), while ACAT most often produces the smallest \(p\)-value (0.688). For \(m=50\), minP remains the most powerful (0.938), and ACAT continues to dominate winner frequency (0.786). Across all pathway sizes, the omnibus procedure has relatively high power under SGP (0.926, 0.704, 0.632 for \(m=5,20,50\)), yet it rarely attains the highest top-method probability, consistent with SGP being a highly concentrated gene--proxy architecture where procedures targeting extreme-tail signals are inherently favored.

The effect-size analysis yields concordant conclusions (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_power}). In the SDA setting, across 18 pathway-size\(\times\)effect-size configurations, minP has the highest power in 8 configurations, ACAT in 7, TFisher in 2, and Fisher in 1, demonstrating the dominance of tail-focused methods. In SGP, this pattern intensifies as minP is optimal in 11 configurations, ACAT in 6, TFisher in 1. The omnibus procedure retains reasonably high power but is not top-ranked in these sparse scenarios. Its mean power declines with pathway size in both architectures (SDA: 0.887 \(\rightarrow\) 0.717 \(\rightarrow\) 0.561; SGP: 0.835 \(\rightarrow\) 0.671 \(\rightarrow\) 0.465 for \(m=5,20,50\)). The regret analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_regret}) aligns with this pattern. Sparse regimes yield the largest regret for the omnibus procedure, especially under SGP (mean 0.148, median 0.078, maximum 0.500; within 0.05 of the best method in 38.9\% of configurations) and SDA (mean 0.088, median 0.020, maximum 0.380; within 0.05 in 55.6\%). Overall, sparse architectures are best captured by ACAT and minP, whereas the omnibus procedure is better interpreted as a robustness-oriented compromise than as an optimal method for sparse settings.


\subsubsection{3.1.2 DPE remains a diffuse-enrichment regime best captured by Stouffer-like behavior.}
In the archetype recovery results, within the DPE archetype, absolute power differences across pathway sizes are modest, but rank-based and top-method probability summaries are highly stable. At \(m=5\), Stouffer's method has the highest power (0.208), lowest mean rank (2.863), and largest top-method probability (0.442). At \(m=20\), Fisher's method, Stouffer's method, and the omnibus procedure have nearly identical power (0.264, 0.266, 0.264), yet Stouffer again has the best mean rank (2.922) and highest top-method probability (0.384), indicating closer agreement with the diffuse weak-enrichment alternative. At \(m=50\), Stouffer still gives the greatest power (0.270), best mean rank (3.110), and highest top-method probability (0.296), while the omnibus retains similar power (0.268) but is rarely ranked first (0.062). Overall, these results support DPE as a genuine pathway-wide weak-enrichment regime. Stouffer's method aligns most closely with the archetype, Fisher's method remains competitive, and the omnibus procedure shows intermediate performance.

This ordering largely persists across effect sizes (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_power}). Stouffer’s method most often achieves the highest power in DPE settings (11 of 18 pathway-size\(\times\)effect-size combinations), while other procedures rarely dominate (TFisher 3, ACAT 2, Omnibus 1, minP 1). For \(m=5\) and \(m=20\), Stouffer’s method typically has higher power than the omnibus procedure across most effect multipliers. For \(m=50\), their power curves are closer, and the advantage diminishes, with the omnibus method sometimes matching or slightly exceeding Stouffer’s average power. The regret analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_regret}) shows that DPE is the regime where the omnibus procedure is most consistently near-optimal. The mean regret is 0.024 (the lowest among the archetypes), the maximum regret is 0.060 (it lies within 0.05 of the best method in 88.9\% of configurations and within 0.10 in all configurations). Thus, although Stouffer’s method is the most archetype-aligned specialist for DPE, the omnibus procedure tracks the leading power curve more closely here than in the sparse architectures.


\subsubsection{3.1.3 CME remains intermediate, with mixed leadership across both pathway size and effect magnitude.}
CME remains the least clearly resolved archetype in the fixed-parameter results, although method performance becomes more structured as \(m\) increases. At \(m=5\), Fisher's and Stouffer's methods have the highest power (0.330 and 0.326), Stouffer's has the highest top-method probability (0.352), and the omnibus shows only moderate power (0.308). At \(m=20\), TFisher is most powerful (0.486), Fisher's has the best mean rank (2.808), and the omnibus is competitive but not dominant (power 0.448; mean rank 3.281). At \(m=50\), TFisher strengthens further (power 0.552; top-method probability 0.340), Fisher's still has the best mean rank (2.795), and the omnibus remains mid-range (power 0.478; mean rank 3.203; top-method probability 0.140). Overall, CME is the archetype most sensitive to pathway size.

The effect-size sensitivity analysis confirms CME as a genuinely intermediate regime with mixed leadership rather than a single dominant detector (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_power}). Across the 18 pathway-size\(\times\)effect-size settings, Stouffer and TFisher each lead in 5 settings, Fisher and minP in 3 each, and ACAT in 2, with no uniformly optimal method. The omnibus remains competitive but is not top in this CME grid. Its mean power falls with pathway size (from \(0.576\) at \(m=5\) to \(0.510\) at \(m=20\) and \(0.289\) at \(m=50\)), indicating stronger sensitivity to signal dilution in larger pathways. Regret is moderate (mean \(0.050\), median \(0.028\), max \(0.172\)), and the omnibus is within 0.05 of the best method in 61.1\% of settings and within 0.10 in 83.3\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_regret}). Thus, CME is a setting where multi-component integration is most useful, since the best-performing component test is not predictable in advance and varies with effect size and pathway size.


\subsubsection{3.1.4 HDS becomes increasingly favorable to balanced procedures as pathway size increases and remains mixed across effect sizes.}

Among the non-sparse archetypes, HDS depends most strongly on pathway size in the archetype-recovery. At \(m=5\), Fisher's method has the highest power (0.566), Stouffer's has the highest top-method probability (0.374), and the omnibus is competitive in power (0.536) but is never top-ranked, suggesting that very small pathways compress the driver-plus-support structure into a few moderate signals. At \(m=20\), the expected HDS pattern emerges. TFisher has the highest power (0.606), Fisher's has the best mean rank (2.765), and the omnibus remains close in power (0.568) but with a low top-method probability (0.040). At \(m=50\), the HDS structure is clearer. TFisher has the highest power (0.718), the omnibus attains the best mean rank (2.899), and Fisher's and TFisher both retain substantial top-method probabilities (0.196 and 0.282). Thus, as pathway size grows, HDS becomes more statistically identifiable, and the omnibus becomes more competitive once both driver and support layers are well represented.

Across effect sizes, HDS shows heterogeneous leadership rather than a single dominant component (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_power}). Over the 18 pathway-size\(\times\)effect-size settings, TFisher is most often optimal (6), followed by Stouffer (5), ACAT (4), Fisher (2), and minP (1), consistent with HDS acting as a genuinely hybrid architecture. Omnibus power is high overall but declines with pathway size (mean \(0.719\) at \(m=5\), \(0.619\) at \(m=20\), \(0.385\) at \(m=50\)), indicating increasing divergence from the best specialized component in larger pathways. Regret is moderate (mean \(0.047\), median \(0.028\), maximum \(0.168\)), and the omnibus method is within 0.05 of the best method in 72.2\% of settings and within 0.10 in 83.3\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_regret}). Thus, as in the CME setting, HDS highlights the practical value of multi-component integration for mixed architectures when the best specialized method is not known in advance.


\subsubsection{3.1.5 The omnibus is rarely the winner, but often remains near-competitive.}

Across pathway sizes in the fixed-parameter archetype-recovery analysis, the omnibus procedure is rarely the most archetype-aligned method under top-method probability summaries, but it also seldom falls to the lowest tier except in highly sparse settings. Under SDA and SGP, omnibus remains detectably powered but is not the preferred specialist. ACAT/minP are more architecture-matched and therefore achieve better rank and top-method probability. In DPS, omnibus power is typically close to the leading methods, though Stouffer more often attains the smallest within-replicate \(p\)-value. In CME and HDS, omnibus usually occupies a middle-to-upper position and often tracks the leading method without systematically dominating.

The effect-size sensitivity curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockH_power}) show the same pattern. In non-sparse/intermediate regimes, omnibus frequently stays close to the best component. For example, at \(m=20\), effect multiplier \(=1.2\), omnibus power is 0.580 in CME versus 0.592 (best, TFisher), and 0.740 in HDS versus 0.752 (best, TFisher). In DPS at the same setting, omnibus reaches 0.288 versus 0.332 (best, Stouffer) and remains close at higher signal (\(m=20\), multiplier \(=1.8\): 0.496 versus 0.516). In contrast, sparse regimes show larger gaps, especially at larger pathway size and low-to-moderate effect (\(m=50\), multiplier \(=0.8\): SDA 0.184 vs 0.564 best; SGP 0.104 vs 0.604 best).

Moreover, figure~\ref{fig:blockH_regret} also quantifies this directly. Regret is smallest for DPS (mean 0.024), intermediate for CME/HDS (0.050/0.047), and largest in sparse regimes, especially SGP/SDA (0.148/0.088). Consistent with this, omnibus regret increases with pathway size overall (mean regret \(m=5: 0.019\), \(m=20: 0.051\), \(m=50: 0.145\)). Thus, CATFISH components are complementary rather than redundant. Sparse architectures favor ACAT/minP, diffuse architectures favor Stouffer/Fisher-type aggregation, and hybrid architectures have mixed leaders. The omnibus is therefore best interpreted as a robustness-oriented hedge, not an architecture-specific optimum.

\subsection{3.2 Calibration, robustness, and diagnostic evaluation of the CATFISH framework under LD}

To assess whether CATFISH yields valid and stable inference under realistic pathway dependence, we performed MVN-based simulations and diagnostic analyses of calibration, robustness, and sensitivity for the full framework. We systematically varied signal architecture, pathway size, effect magnitude, within-pathway LD (gene–gene correlation), and calibration quality, including incomplete or zero-imputed correlation matrices and structured component-misspecification stress tests. We also evaluated adaptive calibration procedures and leave-one-out diagnostics to assess the stability of pathway-level inference. Full simulation designs and parameter settings appear in Appendix~1 and key results are summarized below.

\subsubsection{3.2.1 Null calibration under LD and robustness to missing correlations}

We simulated MVN gene-level \(Z\)-scores under three LD scenarios: (i) LD\_independent (\(\rho=0\)), (ii) LD\_moderate (\(\rho=0.3\)), and (iii) LD\_strong (\(\rho=0.7\)), across pathway sizes \(m\in\{5,25,50\}\) (used throughout the null-calibration analyses unless otherwise noted). For each LD\(\times m\) condition, we generated 500 null replicates, computed ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, Stouffer, and omnibus \(p\)-values, and summarized calibration using genomic-control inflation \(\lambda\) and empirical type I error at \(\alpha=0.05\). For a well-calibrated test under the null, \textit{λ} should be close to 1 and the empirical type I error should be close to 0.05. MVN calibration was based on condition-specific empirical null distributions precomputed from 1000 MVN null draws per condition. We report both analytic calibration (independence-based reference distributions; Omnibus\_Analytical) and MVN calibration (empirical null calibration from LD-matched MVN resampling; Omnibus\_MVN). Thus, all methods are evaluated on the same MVN-generated data but with different $p$-value calibration schemes (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockB_null_calibration}).

\paragraph{3.2.1.1 Null calibration under LD.}

Figure~\ref{fig:blockB_null_calibration} shows that calibration is acceptable under LD\_independent for most methods, but it deteriorates under LD\_moderate and LD\_strong when analytic assumptions ignore correlation. In the LD\_independent setting, empirical type I error remains near nominal for ACAT (mean 0.045), Fisher (0.051), Stouffer (0.055), minP (0.043), and Omnibus\_MVN (0.038), with only mild drift for Omnibus\_Analytical (0.056). The main outlier is TFisher, which shows low $\lambda$ (mean 0.234) despite only moderate type I deviation (mean 0.065), highlighting that $\lambda$ and tail error are not always aligned (Table).

Under LD\_moderate and LD\_strong, several analytically calibrated methods become clearly anti-conservative. The mean type I error increases to 0.174 and 0.222 for Fisher, 0.214 and 0.297 for Stouffer, 0.103 and 0.102 for TFisher, and 0.180 and 0.243 for Omnibus\_Analytical (Table). ACAT is comparatively stable but still mildly inflated (0.067 and 0.061). ACAT was the least anti-conservative component test overall under correlated nulls. In contrast, Omnibus\_MVN remains close to the nominal level across LD settings, with a type I error of 0.060 under LD\_moderate and 0.053 under LD\_strong (ranges across pathway sizes: 0.052--0.066 and 0.044--0.066, respectively). For example, at LD\_moderate with $m=50$, type I error is 0.052 for Omnibus\_MVN versus 0.072 for ACAT and 0.224 for Omnibus\_Analytical. At LD\_strong with $m=50$, OMNIBUS\_MVN is slightly conservative (0.044), while OMNIBUS\_Analytical is strongly inflated (0.326). Overall, these results support Omnibus\_MVN as the most reliable default for controlling false positives under LD while remaining well-calibrated under LD\_independent.

\paragraph{3.2.1.2 Robustness to missing correlations.} 
We next evaluated whether missing gene--gene correlations affect the omnibus statistic. Empirical analyses in real biological data often yield only partial estimates of gene--gene correlations, so we evaluated omnibus calibration under incomplete correlation matrices (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockC_missing_corr}). Null data were generated from a true block-correlation matrix (\(\rho=0.5\)) for pathway sizes \(m\in\{5,25,50\}\). We then removed a fraction of off-diagonal entries (\(0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7\)) by setting them to zero, symmetrized the matrix, and projected it to the nearest positive-definite correlation matrix before MVN null resampling. We compared three omnibus strategies: (i) analytic fallback (ignores correlation), (ii) MVN calibration with the full true correlation matrix (true correlation), and (iii) MVN calibration with the damaged/zero-imputed correlation matrix.

Two patterns were consistent. First, the analytic fallback was strongly miscalibrated across all settings, with a mean type I error \(0.201\) (max \(0.284\)) and mean \(\lambda=0.293\). Second, MVN calibration substantially improved calibration. The true correlation MVN gave a mean type I error \(0.054\) (max \(0.078\)) and mean \(\lambda=0.944\), while zero-imputed MVN gave a mean type I error \(0.076\) (max \(0.146\)) and mean \(\lambda=0.900\). Thus, zero-imputed MVN was clearly better than the analytic fallback, but generally less accurate than the true correlation MVN, especially at larger pathway sizes and higher missingness. For example, at a missing fraction \(0.7\) and \(m=50\), the type I error was \(0.284\) (analytic fallback), \(0.146\) (zero-imputed MVN), and \(0.052\) (MVN true correlation).

Overall, these results support using MVN calibration with the best available correlation estimate as the default. With incomplete correlations, zero-imputed MVN is still much safer than independence-based assumptions but should be treated as an approximation, not a fully calibrated replacement for a complete pathway correlation matrix. Thus, calibration error rose with pathway size and the fraction of missing entries, showing that incomplete matrices can leave residual anti-conservatism in large or poorly observed pathways.

\subsubsection{3.2.2 Stress test: omnibus robustness under component misspecification}

To evaluate robustness under component misspecification, defined here as anti-conservative component behavior (empirical type I error above the nominal level), we conducted a structured component-breakage stress test under the global null. For each LD setting (LD\(_\text{independent}\), LD\(_\text{moderate}\), LD\(_\text{strong}\)) and pathway size (\(m=5,25,50\)), we selected \(k=1,2,3,4\) of the five component tests (ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, Stouffer) and applied anti-conservative breakage to those selected components (broken \(p\)-values transformed as \(p^\gamma\), \(\gamma=0.7\)). For each \((m,\mathrm{LD},k)\), we evaluated all \(\binom{5}{k}\) breakage combinations, generated 500 null replicates per combination, and summarized metrics after averaging across combinations. In each replicate and breakage pattern, the omnibus statistic was recomputed from the broken component set and calibrated using a breakage-matched MVN null. Null component replicates were subjected to the same breakage pattern before constructing the omnibus reference distribution (\(B=1000\) null draws per condition; Fig.~\ref{fig:blockD_breakage}).

As the extent of breakage increased (larger $k$), individual components became increasingly unstable, especially under $\mathrm{LD}_{\mathrm{moderate}}$ and $\mathrm{LD}_{\mathrm{strong}}$. For example, at $k=4$, $\mathrm{LD}_{\mathrm{strong}}$, and $m=25$, type~I error reached 0.370 for Stouffer and 0.273 for Fisher, whereas the MVN-recalibrated omnibus remained at 0.058. A similar pattern appeared under $\mathrm{LD}_{\mathrm{moderate}}$, with $m=50$ and $k=4$, where type~I error reached 0.326 for Stouffer and 0.294 for Fisher, while the omnibus was 0.072. Even when inflation was milder, component trajectories generally worsened as $k$ increased, whereas the omnibus remained near the nominal target. This pattern was observed across LD regimes and pathway sizes.

Across all stress-test settings, omnibus (MVN) had a mean type I error of 0.0595, a mean absolute deviation from 0.05 of 0.0113, and a maximum type I error of 0.0736, making it the closest method to nominal control overall. In contrast, the mean type I error was 0.220 for Stouffer, 0.178 for Fisher, 0.108 for ACAT, 0.104 for TFisher, and 0.0768 for minP. The corresponding \(\lambda\) trends showed the same instability pattern for several components (e.g., Stouffer up to \(\lambda\approx 2.21\)), whereas the omnibus \(\lambda\) remained comparatively flatter and closer to 1.

These results show that robustness requires calibration matched to both the LD structure and the component-failure pattern. In this stress setting, breakage-matched MVN recalibration preserves near-nominal error control for the omnibus test as misspecification increases.

\subsubsection{3.2.3 Adaptive calibration and leave-one-out diagnostics}

Finally, we evaluated two robustness-oriented diagnostics for the omnibus framework, namely (i) adaptive component filtering and (ii) leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockE_adaptive_loo}). In the adaptive procedure, component tests are first screened using training null simulations, and components showing poor calibration are excluded before recomputing the omnibus. We compared four omnibus variants
(i) \textbf{Analytical}, the omnibus computed from all five components using analytic calibration
(ii) \textbf{MVN}, the omnibus computed from all five components with MVN calibration
(iii) \textbf{Adaptive+Analytical}, the omnibus recomputed after filtering miscalibrated components and then analytically calibrated
(iv) \textbf{Adaptive+MVN}, the omnibus recomputed after filtering miscalibrated components and then MVN-calibrated.
For each $(m,\rho)$ setting ($m\in\{5,25,50\}$, $\rho\in\{0,0.3,0.7\}$), adaptive filtering used $n_{\text{train}}=200$ null replicates to screen components and $n_{\text{test}}=300$ null replicates for evaluation. The LOO analysis used $n=500$ null replicates per setting. MVN calibration used $B=1000$ null draws per condition.

In the averaged summary across pathway sizes and LD settings, the uncalibrated analytical omnibus remained strongly anti-conservative (type I error $=0.156$) despite a conservative $\lambda$ estimate ($\lambda=0.439$), illustrating that $\lambda$ alone can be misleading for calibration assessment. By contrast, both MVN-based variants were near nominal type I control (MVN $=0.041$, Adaptive+MVN $=0.053$) with $\lambda$ close to 1 (0.990 and 0.971, respectively). Adaptive+Analytical was also near nominal in this averaged view (0.047), but it remained highly conservative in $\lambda$ (0.454), indicating that analytic $p$-value construction is less stable than LD-aware MVN calibration. Both are well calibrated and similar in performance, so MVN is the simpler default, and Adaptive+MVN is best treated as an optional robustness check rather than a clear improvement.

The LOO analysis provides a complementary sensitivity check by recomputing the MVN-calibrated omnibus after excluding each component in turn ($-$ACAT, $-$Fisher, $-$TFisher, $-$minP, $-$Stouffer) and comparing it with the full set. Across all exclusions, calibration remained stable. Type I error ranged from 0.051 to 0.058 and $\lambda$ ranged from 1.03 to 1.08, with no single exclusion producing a qualitative breakdown. This supports the interpretation that no individual component is solely driving omnibus calibration behavior and that MVN-based calibration provides a robust inferential layer for CATFISH under component perturbation.

\subsection{3.3 From SNP associations to pathway-level aggregation}

We performed a GWAS for stem volume in the sorghum association panel (SAP) \citep{Boatwright2022}. The SNP scan identified multiple genome-wide significant signals, including a prominent peak on chromosome 9 (Fig. \ref{fig:GWAS_MAGMA_CATFISH_sorghum}A). Using a genome-wide threshold of $-\log_{10}(p) > 7$, we mapped significant SNPs to 423 genes. Most strong associations occurred in a single interval on chromosome 9. Thirty four of the 36 top candidate genes fell within a $\sim$2.8~Mb region (56.1--58.9~Mb), indicating a major QTL for stem volume in this region.

We then aggregated SNP-level associations to genes using MAGMA, testing 33,967 annotated sorghum genes and assigning SNPs within gene boundaries plus $\pm$25~kb flanks. After BH FDR correction, 861 genes were significant at FDR $< 0.05$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}B). The top gene-level association was SORBI\_3009G206700 ($-\log_{10}(p) = 14.64$). Gene-level signals were similarly enriched on chromosome 9, mirroring the SNP-level peak and supporting a concentrated locus with multiple associated genes.

To assess pathway-level organization, we applied CATFISH pathway analysis to 410 sorghum PlantCyc metabolic pathways \citep{Hawkins2025}. CATFISH evaluated pathway enrichment using five component tests (ACAT, Fisher's method, adaptive soft TFisher, minP, and Stouffer's method) and an omnibus test combining them. Statistical inference used MVN resampling, with gene--gene correlation matrices from MAGMA to account for LD. We identified 102 pathways at FDR $< 0.05$, of which 73 remained significant after Bonferroni correction ($p < 0.05/410$). Fig.~\ref{fig:GWAS_MAGMA_CATFISH_sorghum}C shows the top 20 pathways and their $p$-value profiles across component and omnibus tests. The strongest pathway-level signals were detected for gluconeogenesis I (GLUCONEO-PWY), L-glutamate biosynthesis IV (GLUGLNSYN-PWY), L-glutamine degradation I (GLUTAMINDEG-PWY), phospholipases (LIPASYN-PWY), and aerobic respiration I (PWY-3781). Together, these pathways provide a coherent framework for interpreting variation in stem volume. Overall, these results show a clear progression from SNP-level associations to gene-level aggregation and then to pathway-level interpretation under LD-aware calibration (Fig.~\ref{fig:GWAS_MAGMA_CATFISH_sorghum}A--C).

\subsection{3.4 Evaluation of Pathway Enrichment Methods for Sorghum Stem Volume}

To assess consistency in pathway enrichment across $p$-value combination methods, we compared significant pathways from each CATFISH component test (ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, Stouffer) and the omnibus test using Bonferroni correction ($p < 0.05/410$). Figure~\ref{fig:suppfig}A) shows substantial but incomplete overlap. TFisher detected the most significant pathways (84), followed by Omnibus (73), Fisher (48), ACAT (47), minP (47), and Stouffer (24). Nineteen pathways were significant in all six methods, indicating highly robust signals. ACAT and minP had identical significant pathway sets (Jaccard similarity = 1.0), consistent with their shared sensitivity to sparse, strong signals.
                                                                                              
Furthermore, we characterized the omnibus $p$-value distribution of observed versus expected \(-\log_{10}(p)\) values across all pathways (\(n=410\);     
Fig.~\ref{fig:suppfig}B). Pathways were color-coded by significance as Bonferroni-significant 
(red), Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) FDR \(<0.05\) but not Bonferroni-significant (blue), and       
non-significant (gray). Most non-significant pathways align with the diagonal, indicating     
little global inflation of test statistics, whereas deviation in the upper tail (blue/red)    
suggests genuine pathway-level enrichment. Using \(B=1{,}000{,}000\) MVN resamples with GPD   
tail extrapolation, we identified 75 Bonferroni-significant pathways, with the most           
significant pathway achieving \(p=5.96\times10^{-35}\) (\(-\log_{10}(p)=34.2\)), and 104      
pathways significant at BH FDR \(<0.05\). The GPD extrapolation enables precise $p$-value     
estimation well beyond the empirical permutation floor, providing substantially improved      
resolution for ranking highly significant pathways.  

Next, the pairwise relationship among methods was assessed using the Spearman correlation of $-\log_{10}(p)$ values (upper triangle) and the Jaccard similarity of significant pathway sets (lower triangle) (Figure~\ref{fig:suppfig}C). All methods showed high pairwise correlations ($\rho = 0.71$--$0.97$), indicating consistent pathway ranking despite differences in significant set membership. The highest correlations were observed between ACAT and minP ($\rho = 0.97$), ACAT and TFisher ($\rho = 0.95$), and Omnibus with all component tests ($\rho = 0.84$--$0.97$). Stouffer showed the lowest correlations with other methods ($\rho = 0.71$--$0.90$), consistent with its distinct weighting scheme that is more robust to outliers. Jaccard similarities of significant sets ranged from 0.29 (Stouffer vs.\ TFisher) to 1.00 (ACAT vs.\ minP). The Omnibus test showed the highest Jaccard overlap with TFisher (0.87), reflecting TFisher's dominant contribution to the omnibus statistic for pathways with hybrid signal patterns. The lower Jaccard values for Stouffer (0.29--0.50) reflect its greater selectivity for pathways with coordinated, diffuse signals.

Finally, the distribution of $-\log_{10}(p)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:suppfig}D) revealed method-specific characteristics. TFisher produced the highest median $-\log_{10}(p)$, consistent with its sensitivity to pathways containing both strong driver genes and moderate supporting signals. ACAT and minP showed similar distributions with heavy right tails, reflecting their power for sparse signal detection. Fisher and Stouffer showed more symmetric distributions centered at lower $-\log_{10}(p)$ values. The Omnibus distribution combined features of all component methods, providing balanced sensitivity across signal patterns.

Together, these comparisons show that the individual CATFISH component tests capture complementary pathway-level enrichment patterns for sorghum stem volume. ACAT and minP behaved similarly, mainly detecting sparse, driver-like association signals, whereas Stouffer highlighted a distinct set of pathways consistent with coordinated, polygenic enrichment. Despite high rank-based concordance, the pathways exceeding significance thresholds overlapped only partially, indicating that thresholded discoveries are strongly method-dependent. The omnibus test reduces this dependence by combining evidence across component tests while retaining substantial power across different signal architectures. Its quantile–quantile behavior under MVN resampling also suggests adequate global calibration, with upper-tail deviations more consistent with true biological enrichment than with general statistical inflation.

\subsection{3.5 Integrated candidate gene analysis identifies high-confidence targets for stem volume and sucrose metabolism}

To prioritize genes supported by multiple modalities, we combined evidence from SNP-level GWAS, MAGMA gene-based tests, and CATFISH pathway enrichment (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}). Significance thresholds were defined as follows: GWAS $-\log_{10}(p) > 7$; MAGMA FDR $< 0.05$; and pathway support as membership in a Bonferroni-significant pathway with $p < 0.05$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}A). In total, 2,016 genes met at least one criterion, and 36 were supported by all three layers, forming the highest-confidence set. We then tested whether pathway membership was associated with stronger gene-level signals. Genes in significant pathways showed more extreme GWAS and MAGMA statistics than non-pathway genes (Wilcoxon $p = 6.34 \times 10^{-4}$ for GWAS; $p = 7.35 \times 10^{-3}$ for MAGMA; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}C), indicating that pathway-level signals preferentially highlight genes with stronger trait-relevant associations rather than random pathway members. The correspondence between SNP- and gene-level association signals is shown in the GWAS versus MAGMA scatter plot (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}D). The 36 genes supported by all three analytic layers cluster in the upper-right quadrant (red), indicating a strong single-variant and aggregated gene-level association. This cross-layer concordance suggests a robust biological signal rather than method-specific artifacts.

These 36 triply supported genes constitute our primary set for downstream functional interpretation and experimental validation (Table~\ref{tab:top9_genes}). Although the stem-volume GWAS identified 423 significant genes and MAGMA identified 861, prioritizing biologically meaningful candidates from either layer alone is difficult because many loci are supported by only one framework. By integrating SNP-level associations, gene-level aggregation, and pathway-level enrichment, our multi-layer scoring strategy narrows this broad candidate space to a tractable subset of genes that pass all three filters. This prioritization is biologically informative rather than a collection of isolated statistical hits, and it helps us narrow down the list of candidate genes. The top-ranked genes converge on functions directly implicated in stem growth, biomass accumulation, and carbon-nitrogen homeostasis

Among these, SORBI\_3009G225700 is annotated for glutamate biosynthesis, glutamine turnover, and organic nitrogen assimilation. This is notable because the GS/GOGAT cycle is the central ammonium assimilation route in plants, converting glutamine to glutamate and supporting nitrogen remobilization to developing sink tissues (\cite{Tamura2011} Tabuchi et al., 2007; Tamura et al., 2011). In rice, disruption of NADH-GOGAT2 reduces biomass and grain yield, directly linking glutamate-mediated nitrogen reutilization to biomass production (Tamura et al., 2011). SORBI\_3009G231100, associated with CMP-KDO biosynthesis, implicates rhamnogalacturonan II (RG-II) assembly and primary cell wall integrity. In Arabidopsis, CMP-KDO synthetase activates KDO for RG-II incorporation, and loss of this activity impairs pollen tube elongation, showing that a fully assembled RG-II complex is required to maintain cell wall integrity in rapidly expanding tissues (Kobayashi et al., 2011). Together, these findings support a direct connection between the genes and stem expansion through cell wall architecture and growth biomechanics.

Additional top-ranked candidates further support a stem-focused signal. SORBI\_3009G233200, implicated in sucrose biosynthesis, is consistent with an SPS-related role in stem carbohydrate metabolism. In rice, SPS8 is the major stem-expressed sucrose phosphate synthase during grain filling. Its disruption reduces stem SPS activity, impairs the remobilization of non-structural carbohydrates, increases stem starch, and decreases grain yield (Ma et al., 2024). SORBI\_3009G225100, annotated in phosphatidylserine/phosphatidylethanolamine biosynthesis, is consistent with a SUI-family phosphatidylserine synthase. In rice, SUI-family genes regulate internode elongation and panicle rachis expansion, linking phospholipid metabolism to stem development and cell expansion (Yin et al., 2013). SORBI\_3009G229300 is associated with xylan biosynthesis, a key determinant of grass secondary cell wall architecture. In rice, GT43 genes are required for xylan backbone elongation, and complementation studies showed that appropriate GT43 members restore stem strength, vessel morphology, and xylan-related cell wall properties, underscoring the role of xylan biosynthesis in structural stem integrity (Lee et al., 2014).

Overall, these results indicate that our integrative framework effectively refines broad GWAS-, MAGMA-, and pathway-derived candidate sets into a smaller, high-confidence subset of genes enriched for stem-relevant biological functions, including nitrogen assimilation, carbohydrate remobilization, membrane lipid homeostasis, and cell wall biogenesis. Concordant support across these three levels strengthens confidence in these candidates and underscores the value of multi-layer integration for prioritizing genes. This framework also has broader translational relevance, as stem sugars and structural biomass are principal feedstocks for lignocellulosic bioethanol production \citep{Saini2015}. Accordingly, the prioritized candidates may inform future efforts to improve both biomass accumulation and fermentable carbon availability.

\subsection*{3.6 Pathway-level comparison reveals distinct enrichment profiles}

To compare CATFISH with established pathway analysis methods, we compared it with MAGMA gene-set analysis for sorghum stem volume. The methods produced distinct pathway signatures (Fig. \ref{fig:GWAS_MAGMA_CATFISH_sorghum}). Among each method’s top 10 pathways, none overlapped (0/10 shared), indicating that CATFISH and MAGMA prioritize fundamentally different components of the genetic architecture of stem volume. CATFISH identified 75 pathways surpassing the Bonferroni threshold ($P < 1.22 \times 10^{-4}$), whereas MAGMA detected none at this stringency (Table~1). The Spearman rank correlation between pathway rankings was low ($\rho = 0.233$), reinforcing their divergent prioritization of biological processes.

CATFISH’s most enriched pathway was aerobic respiration I (cytochrome c; $P = 5.96 \times 10^{-35}$; 298 genes). Other top CATFISH pathways were xylan biosynthesis ($P = 1.27 \times 10^{-21}$), geranylgeranyldiphosphate biosynthesis I via the mevalonate pathway ($P = 1.23 \times 10^{-32}$), and phosphatidylcholine biosynthesis III ($P = 1.22 \times 10^{-29}$). Most top genes from both methods clustered on chromosome 9, aligning with a major QTL for stem volume in this region.

The discrepancy between CATFISH and MAGMA results reflects differences in their statistical frameworks. MAGMA uses a competitive gene set test that asks whether genes in a pathway show stronger associations than genes outside of it \citep{deLeeuw2015MAGMA}. In contrast, CATFISH combines multiple gene‑level \(P\)-value aggregation methods (ACAT, Fisher’s method, adaptive TFisher, minP, and Stouffer’s method) in an omnibus framework, increasing power across diverse signal architectures. Furthermore, The pathways highlighted by CATFISH are biologically plausible for stem volume and vegetative growth. Aerobic respiration supplies ATP and reducing power for biomass accumulation and stem elongation. Xylan biosynthesis directly supports secondary cell wall formation, which shapes stem mechanics and lignocellulosic biomass in grasses \citep{Rennie2014}. Geranylgeranyldiphosphate is a key precursor of gibberellins, chlorophylls, and carotenoids, which regulate stem elongation, growth, and photosynthetic capacity \citep{RuizSola2012}. In contrast, the top MAGMA pathways—D-galactose detoxification, putrescine biosynthesis, and D-mannose degradation lack clear links to stem volume. Their prominence likely reflects statistical artifacts of the competitive testing framework rather than true biological enrichment. 

CATFISH and MAGMA produced different pathway-level inferences for stem volume. CATFISH identified pathways related to energy production, cell wall biosynthesis, and core growth metabolism, whereas MAGMA highlighted a different, seemingly less directly relevant set of metabolic pathways. Pathway rankings showed little concordance and no overlap among the top pathways, indicating that the two methods emphasize different aspects of the genetic architecture. At the gene level, however, they agreed moderately, both focusing on top candidates within the chromosome 9 QTL, which is consistent with a shared causal locus but differs in how gene level evidence is aggregated into pathway-level signals.


\subsection{3.7 Computational scaling of Monte Carlo omnibus calibration}

We assessed the computational scaling of the CATFISH omnibus null-calibration workflow as a function of the Monte Carlo sample size ($B$), pathway size, number of pathways, and thread count. For each benchmark, we simulated null $Z$-scores under a multivariate normal model with pairwise correlation $\rho = 0.20$, converted them to one-sided $p$-values, and computed ACAT and Fisher combination statistics for each null draw. Runtime was measured as wall-clock time, and memory usage as approximate peak resident set size.

We varied pathway size $m \in \{10, 20, 50\}$, null draws per pathway $B \in \{50{,}000,\ 100{,}000,\ 250{,}000,\ 500{,}000,\ 1{,}000{,}000\}$, pathways $P \in \{1, 10, 100\}$, and compared 1 vs 4 threads, with $n_{\mathrm{reps}} = 1$ per condition. Benchmarks were run for $P \in \{1, 10\}$, and values for $P = 100$ were extrapolated from trends at matched $(m,B,\mathrm{threads})$.

Runtime increased approximately linearly with $B$ in all conditions (median $R^2 = 0.9993$, range $0.9952$–$0.999996$). Using 4 threads consistently reduced runtime relative to 1 thread, with speedups from $1.08\times$ to $1.72\times$ (median $1.38\times$). At $B = 10^6$ and $P = 10$, runtime fell from 64.5 to 41.0 s for $m = 10$, from 80.0 to 55.6 s for $m = 20$, and from 128.6 to 98.1 s for $m = 50$ when increasing threads from 1 to 4. For the extrapolated $P = 100$ case at $B = 10^6$, runtime decreased from 642.4 to 406.7 s for $m = 10$, from 795.6 to 557.8 s for $m = 20$, and from 1280.2 to 977.8 s under the same change in thread count.

\section{4 Conclusion}

CATFISH is a correlation-aware pathway analysis framework for genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in settings where pathway enrichment architecture is unknown and gene-level statistics are strongly correlated due to linkage disequilibrium (LD) and shared functional annotations. By computing multiple complementary gene-set statistics and combining them into an omnibus score, CATFISH provides an interpretable, portfolio-like summary of pathway evidence instead of relying on a single test optimized for a narrow class of alternatives. Its main innovation is an LD-aware multivariate normal (MVN) calibration of the entire multi-test procedure, using MAGMA-derived gene–gene correlation estimates and a shared latent MVN draw to recompute both Z-based and p value–based components. This calibration directly targets the null distribution of the full pipeline and mitigates anti-conservative behavior from analytic or independence assumptions applied to correlated gene sets. In simulations spanning varying LD strengths, pathway sizes, missing-correlation scenarios, and stress tests with component misspecification, MVN calibration yields robust type I error control, while the omnibus procedure maintains competitive power across sparse, diffuse, and hybrid enrichment models. In a sorghum stem-volume GWAS, CATFISH detects significant metabolic pathways and aids candidate gene prioritization by integrating SNP-, gene-, and pathway-level evidence. A practical limitation of the LD-aware MVN framework is that its calibration quality depends on the completeness and accuracy of the pathway-specific gene--gene correlation matrix. Although zero-imputation of missing correlations provides a useful fallback and performs substantially better than independence-based calibration, incomplete matrices can still introduce residual calibration error, particularly for larger pathways and under high missingness. Other limitations include reliance on MAGMA-based gene–gene correlation estimates to capture pathway-specific dependence, finite Monte Carlo resolution for extremely small p values, and sensitivity to gene definitions, reference panels, and pathway redundancy. Future work should benchmark CATFISH against established gene-set tools (including MAGMA’s competitive test and dependence-adjusted p value combination methods), extend validation to more GWAS traits and species. CATFISH is released as a fully reproducible software R package available for download from http://github.com/nirwan1265/CATFISH.

\section{A1 Bias warning}

Pathway enrichment analyses are susceptible to biases driven by gene size, pathway size, SNP coverage, and LD, which must be addressed explicitly \citep{White2019}. CATFISH mitigates these effects in three steps. First, SNP-to-gene aggregation is performed with MAGMA's LD-aware multi-SNP model so that gene-level statistics account for local LD structure and SNP density. Second, MAGMA gene $Z$-scores are regressed on $\log(\text{gene length})$ and $\log(\text{number of SNPs})$, and the resulting residual-based $p_{\mathrm{adj}}$ is used for downstream pathway analysis to reduce residual dependence on gene size and SNP density. Third, pathway-level inference is based on LD-aware, gene-label permutations that preserve each gene's adjusted $p$-value and the observed distribution of pathway sizes, producing enrichment $p$-values that are robust to these established sources of bias.

Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6391732/

\section{A2 Mathematical details of CATFISH}\label{app:catfish_math}

\subsection{A2.1 Extended notation}

For a pathway (gene set) $S$, let $G = |S|$ denote the number of genes in the pathway. For each gene $g \in S$, let $p_g$ denote the gene-level association $p$-value and let $Z_g$ denote the corresponding gene-level $Z$ statistic when available. We collect these into pathway-level vectors
\[
\mathbf{p}_S = (p_1, p_2, \dots, p_G),
\qquad
\mathbf{Z}_S = (Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_G).
\]

When gene-level adjustment is applied, we denote the adjusted quantities by
\[
\mathbf{p}_{S,\mathrm{adj}} = (p_{1,\mathrm{adj}}, \dots, p_{G,\mathrm{adj}}),
\qquad
\mathbf{Z}_{S,\mathrm{adj}} = (Z_{1,\mathrm{adj}}, \dots, Z_{G,\mathrm{adj}}).
\]

Unless otherwise stated, definitions below are written using the unadjusted inputs.

\subsection{A2.2 Gene-level association statistics}

Gene-level association evidence was obtained with MAGMA, which aggregates SNP-level signals within each gene while accounting for local LD via an external reference panel. We used MAGMA's SNP-wise gene model, which combines mean-like and top-SNP-like within-gene architectures into a single gene-level association summary. The resulting gene-level $p$-values and Z statistics from MAGMA were used as the common input for all downstream CATFISH pathway analyses.

\subsection{A2.3 Gene-level adjustment for gene size and SNP density}

To reduce the residual dependence of gene-level signals on gene size and SNP density, we optionally regressed the MAGMA gene-level $Z$ statistic on gene-level covariates. Let $L_g$ denote gene length and $S_g$ denote the number of SNPs mapped to gene $g$. We fit
\[
Z_g = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(L_g) + \beta_2 \log(S_g) + \varepsilon_g.
\]

We then define the adjusted residual score as
\[
Z_g^{\mathrm{adj}} = Z_g - \widehat{Z}_g,
\qquad
\widehat{Z}_g = \widehat{\beta}_0 + \widehat{\beta}_1 \log(L_g) + \widehat{\beta}_2 \log(S_g),
\]
and the corresponding adjusted two-sided $p$-value as
\[
p_{g,\mathrm{adj}} = 2\Phi\!\left(-\left|Z_g^{\mathrm{adj}}\right|\right),
\]
where $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

\subsection{A2.4 Pathway-level component statistics}

As all pathway statistics are derived from the same within-pathway gene-level evidence, the component tests are mutually dependent. Independence-based reference nulls, therefore, serve only as analytic definitions. Final inference is obtained from the unified dependence-aware calibration framework described below.

\subsubsection{A2.4.1 ACAT}

For each gene,
\[
t_g = \tan\left(\pi\left(\tfrac{1}{2} - p_g\right)\right).
\]
With nonnegative weights $w_g$ such that $\sum_{g \in S} w_g = 1$, the pathway-level ACAT statistic is
\[
T_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S)
=
\sum_{g \in S} w_g \tan\left(\pi\left(\tfrac{1}{2} - p_g\right)\right),
\]
with corresponding analytic combined $p$-value
\[
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S)
=
\tfrac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\left(T_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S)\right).
\]

\subsubsection{A2.4.2 Fisher's method}

Fisher's pathway statistic is
\[
T_{\mathrm{Fisher}}(S) = -2\sum_{g \in S}\log(p_g).
\]
Under independence,
\[
T_{\mathrm{Fisher}}(S) \sim \chi^2_{2G},
\]
which yields the standard analytic Fisher $p$-value.

\subsubsection{A2.4.3 Adaptive soft TFisher}

For a given soft threshold $\tau \in (0,1]$, the soft TFisher statistic is
\[
W^{\mathrm{soft}}(S;\tau)
=
\sum_{g \in S}
\left[-2\log(p_g) + 2\log(\tau)\right]_+,
\qquad
(x)_+ = \max(x,0).
\]

CATFISH evaluates this statistic across a fixed threshold grid
\[
\mathcal{T} = \{0.00001, 0.000001, 0.00000001\},
\]
and defines the adaptive TFisher component as the minimum calibrated TFisher $p$-value across $\tau \in \mathcal{T}$.

\subsubsection{A2.4.4 Stouffer's method}

For a pathway with $G$ genes, the default unweighted Stouffer statistic is
\[
Z_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S)
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{G}}\sum_{g \in S} Z_g,
\]
with one-sided analytic $p$-value
\[
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(Z_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S)\right).
\]

In our implementation, MAGMA gene-level inputs are non-directional and do not encode effect sign. Accordingly, we construct gene-level normal scores by monotone transformation of the MAGMA gene $p$-values, for example
\[
Z_g=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_g)
\]
for one-sided $p_g$, or
\[
Z_g=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_g/2)
\]
for two-sided $p_g$.
Under this formulation, Stouffer's method is used as an inverse-normal $p$-value combination procedure that aggregates evidence across genes within a pathway. It therefore tests for enrichment of small gene-level $p$-values, rather than for a signed or directional effect.

If nonnegative weights $w_g$ are supplied, the weighted version is
\[
Z_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}^{(w)}(S)
=
\frac{\sum_{g \in S} w_g Z_g}{\sqrt{\sum_{g \in S} w_g^2}}.
\]

Since, the MAGMA-derived gene-level inputs used here are non-directional, all CATFISH pathway architectures and simulations are defined by the magnitude, concentration, and distribution of enrichment across genes, rather than by signed positive or negative gene effects.

\subsubsection{A2.4.5 minP}

The minP statistic is defined as
\[
T_{\min}(S) = \min_{g \in S} p_g.
\]
Under independence, its canonical Tippett/\v{S}id\'ak mapping is
\[
p_{\mathrm{minP}}(S)
=
1 - \left(1 - T_{\min}(S)\right)^G.
\]



\subsection{A2.5 Sources of dependence}

Dependence in CATFISH arises at two levels. First, the component pathway tests are deterministically coupled because they all use the same within-pathway gene-level evidence, $\{p_g\}_{g \in S}$ and, when used, $\{Z_g\}_{g \in S}$. Thus, the resulting component pathway $p$-values are correlated even if the genes are independent. Under sparse architectures such as SDA and SGP, one or a few extremely small gene-level $p$-values drive multiple statistics. The same extreme gene determines $\min P$, pushes ACAT via its heavy-tail Cauchy transform, and contributes disproportionately to Fisher and TFisher. Under DPE, no single gene dominates, instead, a weak but widespread shift across many genes creates an excess of moderately small $p$-values and a modest shift in the mean gene-level $Z$ distribution. This broad signal is captured by Fisher and TFisher through the accumulation of many moderate $-\log(p)$ terms, and by Stouffer through coherent aggregation of $Z$ scores. As a result, Fisher, TFisher, and Stouffer become positively correlated under DPE-type alternatives, and Stouffer-like methods tend to perform best because they are directly tuned to elevation in association-strength scores (More examples in the archetype section) (Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_blockA_archetype_recovery_by_m})

Second, gene-level inputs within a pathway may be correlated due to LD, overlapping SNP mappings, shared genomic structure, or broader polygenic effects, creating additional gene–gene dependence and strengthening deterministic coupling among component tests. Post hoc choices across methods, such as taking the minimum statistic or adaptively selecting the TFisher threshold, further increase dependence. Thus, analytic across-method combinations that assume independence can be miscalibrated, motivating the dependence-aware null calibration framework described below.

\subsection{A2.6 Numerical safeguards}

To avoid numerical instability in $\log(\cdot)$ and Cauchy transforms, gene-level $p$-values are clipped to
\[
p_g \leftarrow \min\{1-p_{\min}, \max(p_g, p_{\min})\},
\qquad p_{\min}=10^{-15}.
\]

If a gene appears multiple times in a pathway definition, duplicate entries are collapsed prior to pathway analysis so that each gene contributes only once. When duplicate gene entries occur, the retained gene-level value is the minimum observed $p$-value for that gene.

\subsection{A2.7 Omnibus pathway combination across methods}

Let the available component pathway $p$-values for pathway $S$ be
\[
\mathcal{P}(S)
=
\left\{
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Fisher}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{TFisher}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{minP}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S)
\right\},
\]
with $K = |\mathcal{P}(S)| \le 5$ depending on whether Stouffer is available.

\subsubsection{ACAT-O}

With method-level weights $v_j \ge 0$ satisfying $\sum_{j=1}^{K} v_j = 1$, the across-method ACAT omnibus (default) is
\[
T_{\mathrm{omni,ACAT}}(S)
=
\sum_{j=1}^{K} v_j \tan\!\bigl(\pi(0.5-p_j)\bigr),
\]
and
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni,ACAT}}(S)
=
0.5-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\!\bigl(T_{\mathrm{omni,ACAT}}(S)\bigr).
\]

\subsubsection{minP-O}

The across-method minimum is
\[
T_{\mathrm{omni,min}}(S)=\min_{j=1,\dots,K} p_j(S),
\]
with analytic independence-based mapping
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni,min}}(S)
=
1-\bigl(1-T_{\mathrm{omni,min}}(S)\bigr)^K.
\]

\subsection{A2.7 Dependence-aware null calibration}

As both the gene-level inputs and the component tests are dependent, CATFISH calibrates the omnibus using a null generator that preserves this dependence. We support two complementary strategies for null generation, namely global gene-set resampling (also analytic) and LD-aware MVN calibration. Global resampling preserves the empirical genome-wide marginal distribution of gene-level evidence and the cross-method coupling, but it does not model within-pathway LD. MVN calibration explicitly preserves within-pathway gene--gene dependence and is the recommended inferential procedure.

\subsubsection{A2.7.1 Global resampling}

In the global resampling mode, a null pathway of size $d$ is generated by sampling $d$ genes from a genome-wide pool of genes with valid MAGMA outputs. Each sampled gene contributes its paired evidence $(p_g, Z_g)$ when $Z_g$ is available, ensuring that both $p$-based and $Z$-based component tests are recomputed from the same resampled gene set in each null replicate. For replicate $b$, CATFISH recalculates all component pathway statistics on the resampled pathway and applies the same omnibus operator used for the observed data.

Formally, let $\mathcal{G}$ denote the genome-wide pool of genes with valid pathway inputs, and let pathway $S$ have size $|S|=d$. For each replicate $b=1,\dots,B$, CATFISH samples $d$ genes from $\mathcal{G}$, typically without replacement when $d \le |\mathcal{G}|$, and constructs a null pathway-level evidence set
\[
\mathbf{p}^{(b)}_{\mathrm{global}}(S), \qquad \mathbf{Z}^{(b)}_{\mathrm{global}}(S).
\]
All component tests are recomputed on this resampled pathway, yielding replicate component $p$-values $\{p_j^{(b)}(S)\}$, and the omnibus operator is then applied:
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)
=
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\{p_j^{(b)}(S)\}\right).
\]

If $p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)$ denotes the observed omnibus value, the globally resampled omnibus $p$-value is estimated as
\[
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,global}}(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)\le p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}.
\]

This approach preserves the empirical genome-wide marginal distribution of gene-level evidence and captures cross-method coupling because all component tests are recomputed from the same sampled gene set. However, it does not preserve pathway-specific LD structure and is therefore treated as a secondary calibration mode relative to the LD-aware MVN procedure.

\subsubsection{A2.7.2 LD-aware MVN calibration}

In the LD-aware mode, CATFISH constructs a pathway-specific gene--gene correlation matrix $R_S$ and generates null gene-level score vectors as
\[
Z^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_S),
\qquad b=1,\dots,B.
\]
Here, $R_S$ is treated as fixed for a given pathway and encodes the expected dependence among gene-level statistics under the null. The MVN procedure does not resample the correlation matrix itself, rather, it repeatedly generates new null gene-score vectors whose joint covariance structure is determined by $R_S$.

\paragraph{A2.7.2.1 Construction of the pathway-specific correlation matrix.}
For each pathway $S=\{g_1,\dots,g_d\}$, the pathway-specific gene--gene correlation matrix $R_S$ is constructed from pairwise gene-correlation estimates, typically obtained from MAGMA output. Diagonal entries are set to 1, and off-diagonal entries are filled using the available pairwise correlations for genes within the pathway. When a gene pair is missing from the pairwise correlation output, the corresponding entry is set to 0 by default. This missing-as-zero convention corresponds to assuming independence for unobserved gene pairs while retaining the observed correlation structure.

Formally, if $r_{ij}$ denotes the available pairwise correlation between genes $g_i$ and $g_j$, then
\[
(R_S)_{ii}=1,
\qquad
(R_S)_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
r_{ij}, & \text{if the pairwise estimate is available,} \\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

\paragraph{A2.7.2.2 Numerical stabilization of the MVN null.}
As pathway-specific correlation matrices can be ill-conditioned or only approximately positive definite, CATFISH applies numerical stabilization before MVN simulation when needed. In practice, this can include clipping extreme correlations (for example, $|r_{ij}| \le 0.999$), imputing missing entries to 0 as described above (Fig. \ref{fig:blockC_missing_corr}) , and applying a nearest correlation-matrix correction \citep{Higham2002} or adding a small diagonal jitter. These steps improve numerical stability during MVN simulation without altering the interpretation of the LD-aware null.


\paragraph{A2.7.2.3 Practical implications of incomplete correlation matrices.}
The missing-as-zero convention is a pragmatic approximation used when pairwise gene–gene correlations are unavailable. It treats unobserved gene pairs as independent and, when needed, applies numerical regularization so the resulting correlation matrix is positive semidefinite and suitable for MVN simulation. This improves numerical stability and robustness but does not ensure that the matrix accurately reflects the true null dependence structure. If the true but unobserved correlations are nonzero, imputing them as zero can systematically distort the null covariance model and affect the calibration of the omnibus \(p\)-value. The extent of distortion depends on the proportion of missing entries, pathway dimensionality, and the strength and pattern of the omitted correlations. Thus, CATFISH should use the most complete and accurate pathway-specific correlation estimates available, and results based on highly incomplete matrices should be interpreted cautiously.

\paragraph{A2.7.2.4 Generation of correlated null gene-level scores.}
To simulate from the pathway-specific null, CATFISH factorizes the stabilized matrix $R_S$ as
\[
R_S = LL^\top,
\]
where $L$ is a lower-triangular matrix, for example, obtained by a Cholesky decomposition when $R_S$ is positive definite. Let
\[
u^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)
\]
denote a vector of independent standard normal draws of length $d$, where $I_d$ is the $d\times d$ identity matrix. CATFISH then forms the correlated null gene-score vector as
\[
Z^{(b)} = L u^{(b)}.
\]
By construction,
\[
\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(Z^{(b)}\right)
=
L\,\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(u^{(b)}\right)L^\top
=
L I_d L^\top
=
LL^\top
=
R_S.
\]
Thus, the simulated null scores are random but not independent. Genes with high pairwise correlation in $R_S$ tend to co-vary across null replicates, whereas genes with near-zero correlation vary more independently. This preserves the gene-level dependence structure induced by LD in the pathway-specific null model.

\paragraph{A2.7.2.5 Gaussian-copula mapping to gene-level $p$-values.}
For $p$-based component tests, null gene-level $p$-values are derived from the same latent MVN draw using a Gaussian copula:
\[
U_g^{(b)} = \Phi\!\left(Z_g^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
p_g^{(b)} = 2\min\{U_g^{(b)}, 1-U_g^{(b)}\}.
\]
This preserves dependence across genes while ensuring coherent null inputs across both $Z$-based and $p$-based component statistics. If a one-sided gene-level convention is used instead, the mapping may be written as
\[
p_g^{(b)} = 1-\Phi\!\left(Z_g^{(b)}\right),
\]
with the direction chosen appropriately.

\paragraph{A2.7.2.6 Component recomputation and omnibus calibration.}
For each replicate, CATFISH computes the replicate component vector
\[
\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S)
=
\big(
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Fisher}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{TFisher}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{minP}}^{(b)}(S)
\big),
\]
and then applies the omnibus operator
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S)\right).
\]

If $p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)$ denotes the observed omnibus value from the real pathway data, the calibrated omnibus $p$-value is
\[
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)\le p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}.
\]

This unified calibration targets the null distribution of the full pathway-analysis procedure by recomputing all component tests and the omnibus operator under the same dependence‑preserving null draw. As all component statistics are computed from the same simulated pathways, the MVN procedure preserves both within-pathway gene dependence and cross-method dependence. CATFISH therefore does not calibrate each component and then recalibrate the omnibus. Instead, the recommended inferential quantity is the omnibus statistic calibrated once under the full MVN null pipeline.

\subsection{A2.8 MVN calibration of component statistics}

CATFISH can optionally report MVN-calibrated component $p$-values for diagnostic purposes. For a given component $j$, its MVN-calibrated value is
\[
\hat p_j(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_j^{(b)}(S)\le p_j^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}.
\]

To evaluate whether the MVN framework improves calibration for individual component tests as well as the omnibus statistic, we compared analytically versus MVN-calibrated component \(p\)-values under the null across LD\(_\text{independent}\), LD\(_\text{moderate}\), and LD\(_\text{strong}\) settings for \(m = 5, 25, 50\) (Supp.~Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_blockG_component_mvn}). Under LD\(_\text{independent}\), analytic and MVN-calibrated results were similar for most components, reflecting minimal dependence. Under LD\(_\text{moderate}\) and LD\(_\text{strong}\), however, analytically calibrated Fisher and Stouffer statistics became strongly anti-conservative, with type I error rates far above 0.05. TFisher showed method-specific miscalibration, and minP showed milder deviations from nominal levels. In contrast, MVN-calibrated component tests stayed close to nominal performance across LD structures and pathway sizes, with \(\lambda \approx 1\) and type I error near 0.05. ACAT was relatively well calibrated even analytically, but MVN calibration further harmonized its behavior with the other components. These results indicate that LD-aware MVN calibration functions as intended at the component-test level. However, since component statistics remain jointly dependent after marginal calibration, final inference should still rely on the direct calibration of the omnibus statistic.

\subsection{A2.9 Monte Carlo resolution, tail refinement, and recommended practice}                
                                                                                                
Because CATFISH calibrates the omnibus $p$-value via finite-sample null resampling, the       
resulting Monte Carlo $p$-values are discrete, with resolution determined by the number of    
resamples $B$. Under the plus-one correction, the smallest empirical $p$-value is             
approximately $1/(B+1)$, so permutation or Monte Carlo $p$-values should never be reported as 
exactly zero \citep{Phipson2010}. Thus, a fixed resampling budget with purely empirical       
estimation is adequate for exploratory pathway screening but can be insufficient for          
accurately estimating very small tail probabilities. More broadly, the Monte Carlo and        
permutation-testing literature shows that $p$-value resolution is intrinsically limited by the
number of null draws, and that reliable estimation of extreme tails quickly becomes          
computationally expensive under naive fixed-budget resampling \citep{Knijnenburg2009,Che2014}.
                                                                                            
To overcome this resolution limit, CATFISH implements the generalized Pareto distribution     
(GPD) tail extrapolation method of \citet{Knijnenburg2009}. When enabled via the              
\texttt{tailmode = "hybridgpd"} option, the algorithm fits a GPD to the $k$ most extreme null 
replicates and uses this fitted distribution to extrapolate $p$-values beyond the empirical   
floor. This approach can estimate $p$-values orders of magnitude smaller than $1/B$—for       
example, obtaining $p$-values as small as $10^{-35}$ with $B = 10^6$ permutations—while       
requiring far fewer simulations than purely empirical estimation would demand for equivalent  
resolution. The hybrid mode automatically switches between empirical and GPD-extrapolated     
estimates based on the number of exceedances, ensuring stable estimates across the full range 
of pathway significance.                                                                      
                                                                                            
To make large-scale resampling computationally tractable, CATFISH supports multi-threaded     
parallelization of the inner null computations via the \texttt{nthreads} parameter. Because   
each permutation replicate is independent, this parallelization is embarrassingly parallel and
achieves near-linear speedup with the number of cores—for instance, reducing a 50-hour       
single-threaded run to under 3 hours with 12 threads.                                         
                                                                                            
In practice, we recommend using GPD tail extrapolation with a moderate-to-large resampling    
budget (e.g., $B = 10^5$ to $10^6$) for genome-wide pathway analysis. Omnibus $p$-values use  
the plus-one estimator $(e+1)/(B+1)$ for empirical estimation, switching to GPD extrapolation 
when the number of exceedances falls below a user-specified threshold                         
\citep{Phipson2010,Knijnenburg2009}. This hybrid approach provides both computational         
efficiency and accurate estimation of extreme tail probabilities, enabling reliable           
discrimination among top-ranked pathways that would otherwise be tied at the permutation floor
under purely empirical estimation.  


\subsection{A2.10 Choice of final omnibus \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-value}

Depending on the calibration mode, CATFISH may report an analytic omnibus, a globally resampled omnibus, or an MVN-calibrated omnibus. The final pathway-level omnibus is chosen as

\[
\begin{aligned}
p_{\mathrm{omni,final}}(S)
&=
\begin{cases}
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S), & \text{if MVN calibrated,} \\[4pt]
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,global}}(S), & \text{else if globally calibrated,} \\[4pt]
p_{\mathrm{omni,analytic}}(S), & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\]

\subsection{A2.11 Default implementation choices}

Unless otherwise stated, CATFISH uses equal weights within ACAT and ACAT-O, an adaptive soft-TFisher threshold grid $\mathcal{T}=\{0.20, 0.10, 0.05, 0.01\}$, one-sided Stouffer aggregation for MAGMA association-strength $Z$ scores, clipping of gene-level $p$-values at $p_{\min}=10^{-15}$, and LD-aware MVN calibration as the primary inferential mode. Missing pairwise gene correlations are set to 0 when constructing pathway-specific correlation matrices, with numerical stabilization applied as needed to ensure positive-definite MVN simulation.

When gene-level test statistics are approximately independent (e.g., with negligible linkage disequilibrium or weak within-pathway correlation), CATFISH can use analytic approximations to the null distributions of its component tests. In settings with correlated GWAS statistics, MVN calibration is recommended and used by default.

For reporting and interpretation, we use a tiered framework. When pathway-level signals are strong and many pathways pass the HD FDR procedure, we treat FDR-significant pathways as the main findings. When signals are sparse, we summarize evidence using the CATFISH omnibus \(p\)-value, which retains sensitivity across heterogeneous genetic architectures by combining complementary component tests. When no pathway meets the omnibus \(p\)-value nor FDR threshold, we instead present the highest-ranked pathways (e.g., the top 1\% by omnibus \(p\)-value) as hypothesis-generating candidates, without interpreting them as statistically significant discoveries.

\section{A3 Candidate-gene prioritization score}\label{app:candidate_score}

To prioritize genes supported across multiple analytical layers, we combined evidence from GWAS locus support, MAGMA gene-level significance, and pathway-level enrichment. For each gene $g$, the composite score was defined as
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{score}(g)
&= \mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{GWAS}\}
 + \mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{MAGMA}\}
 + \mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{PATH}\} \\
&\quad + 0.2\left[-\log_{10}\!\left(p_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}\right)\right] \\
&\quad + 0.1\left[-\log_{10}\!\left(p_{\mathrm{GWAS}}\right)\right] \\
&\quad + 0.1\left[-\log_{10}\!\left(p_{\mathrm{PATH}}\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

This formulation gives primary weight to agreement across discrete evidence layers while allowing modest within-layer ranking by continuous signal strength. Genes supported by at least two layers were prioritized for downstream interpretation. The MAGMA continuous term was upweighted (0.2 vs 0.1 for GWAS and pathway terms) because MAGMA provides direct gene-level aggregate evidence, whereas GWAS is locus-centric and pathway enrichment is set-level. This weighting was intended to improve within-gene ranking resolution without overriding the primary cross-layer agreement indicators.



\paragraph{Heuristic candidate-gene prioritization score and sensitivity.}
We emphasize that the multilayer candidate-gene prioritization score is a \emph{heuristic ranking device} rather than a formal inferential model. The primary signal in the ranking is cross-layer agreement (i.e., the number of independent evidence layers supporting a gene); the continuous “strength” terms are used to improve resolution \emph{within} the same support tier and to break ties among genes with identical support counts. We upweighted the MAGMA continuous term (0.2 vs.\ 0.1 for GWAS and pathway terms) because MAGMA is intrinsically gene-resolved and aggregates association evidence across multiple SNPs within a gene, whereas GWAS evidence is locus-centric and depends on SNP-to-gene mapping choices and local LD structure, and pathway enrichment is set-level evidence that can be influenced by overlapping gene membership and correlated signals within pathways. This modest upweighting was chosen to improve within-gene ranking resolution without allowing any single continuous metric to override cross-layer support. Importantly, top-ranked candidates were stable to reasonable weight perturbations (including equal weights and a reduced MAGMA weight), indicating that the prioritization is driven primarily by cross-layer concordance rather than the specific choice of 0.2 for MAGMA.
