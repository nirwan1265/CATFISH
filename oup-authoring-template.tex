\abstract{Gene-set and pathway analyses can improve interpretability and power in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) by aggregating many modest genetic effects, but valid inference is challenging because gene- and pathway-level statistics are strongly correlated due to linkage disequilibrium (LD), shared annotations, and overlapping pathways. We introduce CATFISH, a correlation-aware pathway analysis framework using LD-adjusted MAGMA gene-level summary statistics (p-values and/or Z-scores). CATFISH computes multiple pathway-level tests—ACAT, Fisher’s combination test, adaptive soft TFisher, minP/Tippett’s method, and Stouffer’s method—and combines them via an omnibus operator that maintains sensitivity across diverse enrichment patterns. To control false positives under dependence across genes and across tests, CATFISH calibrates the omnibus statistic against a pathway-specific, LD-informed multivariate normal (MVN) null built from MAGMA-based gene–gene correlation matrices. For p-value–based components, CATFISH uses a Gaussian-copula transform to embed p-values in the MVN framework. Simulations across LD structures and pathway sizes show that standard independence-based calibrations can be anti-conservative for several component tests and naïve omnibus schemes. In contrast, MVN-based calibration achieves near-nominal type I error while retaining strong power for sparse, diffuse, and mixed enrichment. In a sorghum GWAS of stem volume, CATFISH identifies pathways passing Bonferroni and FDR thresholds and supports multi-layer candidate gene prioritization by integrating SNP-, gene-, and pathway-level evidence. CATFISH thus provides a dependence-calibrated, architecture-robust framework for pathway discovery and biological interpretation in GWAS.}

\section{Introduction}

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have transformed complex trait research by identifying thousands of loci linked to disease, development, and quantitative phenotypes. Yet most variants explain only a small fraction of phenotypic variance and often fall in noncoding regions, limiting single-marker mechanistic interpretation \citep{Manolio2009,Boyle2017}. Because complex traits are highly polygenic, relevant signal is spread across many modest-effect loci rather than a few genome-wide significant variants. In this context, gene-set analysis, including pathway- and network-based approaches, is a key post-GWAS strategy for translating summary statistics into biological hypotheses. By aggregating evidence across predefined groups of functionally related genes, pathway-based methods enhance interpretability, reduce the multiple-testing burden, and reveal coordinated pathway disruptions that single-locus analyses may miss \citep{Mooney2015,deLeeuw2015}.

A broad ecosystem of pathway analysis tools supports GWAS and other genome-scale studies. Classical over-representation methods test whether predefined pathways are enriched among significant genes, as implemented in DAVID, g:Profiler, and WebGestalt \citep{Sherman2022,Kolberg2023,Liao2019}. Ranking-based methods such as Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) instead assess whether pathway genes are non-randomly concentrated at the top or bottom of a ranked gene list \citep{Subramanian2005}. GWAS-focused extensions, including MAGENTA, INRICH, and MAGMA, add features such as SNP-to-gene mapping, pathway-level modeling, and adjustment for linkage disequilibrium (LD) or gene size \citep{Segre2010,Lee2012,deLeeuw2015}. Together, these methods have made pathway analysis a standard element of post-GWAS interpretation. In practice, pathway tests are often applied to correlated gene-level summaries and then compared or combined without a dependence-aware calibration step, which can inflate false positives under LD. CATFISH addresses this by calibrating the entire multi-test workflow under a pathway-specific dependence model.

Despite these advances, major challenges still remain. Existing methods differ substantially in their assumptions about SNP-to-gene mapping, null hypothesis specification, LD modeling and adjustment, and their vulnerability to biases from pathway size, gene density, and annotation quality \citep{Mooney2015,Debrabant2017}. Self-contained and competitive testing frameworks also address fundamentally different biological questions, yet their results are often treated as directly comparable. In addition, pathway databases are incomplete and inconsistently curated, and genes are frequently assigned to multiple, overlapping pathways, creating redundancy and complicating interpretation. Benchmarking studies show marked performance variability across enrichment methods, indicating that no single approach is optimal for all genetic architectures \citep{Geistlinger2021}. Together, these issues create a practical gap in GWAS pathway analysis. Real datasets can exhibit pathway signals that differ markedly in sparsity, concentration, and breadth of enrichment, whereas many existing methods perform well only for a limited subset of these patterns.

We developed \textbf{CATFISH}, a correlation-aware pathway analysis framework that integrates multiple complementary gene-set statistics in a unified omnibus strategy. Different pathway tests are sensitive to different genetic architectures. Some are best at detecting broad, distributed enrichment, whereas others are more sensitive to sparse strong signals or heterogeneous evidence. Instead of relying on a single summary statistic and signal, CATFISH evaluates each pathway with several complementary tests and combines them in an omnibus framework. This improves robustness when the true signal configuration is unknown while preserving the interpretability of the component tests, providing a flexible, broadly sensitive alternative for pathway discovery in GWAS-based studies.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Overview and notation}

CATFISH is a pathway-analysis framework developed to maintain high statistical power across heterogeneous signal architectures by aggregating multiple complementary gene-set statistics into a single, LD dependence-aware omnibus test. For a pathway $S$ containing $G = |S|$ genes, let $p_g$ denote the gene-level association $p$-value for gene $g \in S$, and let $Z_g$ denote the corresponding gene-level $Z$-statistic when available. We define $\mathbf{p}_S = (p_1, \dots, p_G)$ and $\mathbf{Z}_S = (Z_1, \dots, Z_G)$ as the vectors of gene-level evidence within pathway $S$. These quantities constitute the common inputs for all CATFISH component tests as well as for the omnibus calibration procedure. An overview of the CATFISH analysis workflow is provided in Fig.~\ref{fig:catfish_workflow}, illustrating the generation of gene-level inputs, component pathway tests, and dependence-aware omnibus calibration under the LD-aware multivariate normal (LD-aware MVN) null model.

\subsection{Assumptions of the LD-aware MVN calibration}
CATFISH inference relies on an LD-aware MVN null, and its key assumptions are stated below.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Approximate MVN assumption at the gene level.}
Under the null, the pathway-level vector of gene statistics is treated as approximately multivariate normal, i.e.,
\[
\mathbf{Z}^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\, \mathbf{R}_S\right).
\]
This makes explicit an assumption that would otherwise remain implicit in MVN-based calibration procedures.

\item \textbf{Correlation-matrix adequacy assumption.}
The pathway-specific matrix $\mathbf{R}_S$, derived from MAGMA gene–gene correlation estimates, is taken as a reasonable approximation to the true dependence among pathway genes. This is crucial because MVN calibration relies on $\mathbf{R}_S$, and MAGMA’s gene-level framework explicitly models LD-induced gene dependence.

\item \textbf{Residual-confounding assumption.}
CATFISH assumes gene-level inputs are already adjusted so that pathway signals are not mainly driven by structural confounders like gene size or SNP density. This matches the MAGMA framework, which uses gene-level adjustment for gene length and SNP density in competitive gene-set analysis.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Gene-level inputs from MAGMA}

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics were aggregated into gene-level association metrics using MAGMA’s LD-aware SNP-wise gene model, employing an external reference panel to characterize local LD structure. SNPs were mapped to genes based on gene boundaries, optionally extended by flanking regions, resulting in per-gene outputs that included a gene-level $p$-value, $p_g$, and an association-strength $Z$ statistic, $Z_g$. To mitigate residual confounding attributable to gene length and SNP density, gene-level $Z$ statistics were optionally regressed on $\log(L_g)$ and $\log(S_g)$, where $L_g$ denotes gene length and $S_g$ the number of mapped SNPs. The residuals from this regression were then transformed back into adjusted gene-level $p$-values (detail in appendix). The adjusted gene-level association evidence was subsequently used as input to CATFISH.

\subsection{Component pathway tests}

Each pathway in CATFISH is evaluated using a panel of complementary component tests, comprising ACAT, Fisher’s method, adaptive soft TFisher, minP, and Stouffer’s method. These five tests were chosen because together they span the main pathway signal archetypes considered in this study, while remaining simple, interpretable, and directly applicable to gene-level $p$-values or $Z$-scores without requiring external weights or pathway-specific tuning parameters. Specifically, ACAT and minP are most powerful for sparse architectures dominated by one or a small number of highly significant genes, whereas Fisher’s and Stouffer’s methods are better suited to detecting broader enrichment patterns involving many modestly associated genes. The adaptive soft TFisher test provides an intermediate, tail-adaptive compromise between these extremes. Formal mathematical definitions of all component statistics are given in Appendix.

\subsection{Dependence structure and motivation for null calibration}

A central feature of CATFISH is that its component tests are not statistically independent. First, they are deterministically coupled because each component test statistic is derived from the same within-pathway, gene-level evidence, typically $\{p_g\}_{g \in S}$ and, in some cases, $\{Z_g\}_{g \in S}$. As a result, even under the hypothetical scenario in which gene-level test statistics are mutually independent, the corresponding component $p$-values would nonetheless be correlated, since they represent distinct transformations of a common underlying signal. For instance, both Fisher’s method and soft TFisher are primarily sensitive to an enrichment of moderately small gene-level p-values; Stouffer’s method is driven by the aggregate accumulation of gene-level normal scores derived from pathway gene p-values; and ACAT and minP tend to yield extreme values when one or a small number of genes exhibit exceptionally small $p$-values.

Second, gene-level inputs within a pathway are themselves frequently correlated as a consequence of LD, overlapping SNP annotations, shared local genomic architecture, and broader polygenic background effects. These factors induce additional dependence among gene-level $p$-values and $Z$-scores and, in turn, further strengthen dependence among the resulting pathway-level component tests. Post hoc selection across methods, for example, by taking the minimum $p$-value across component tests or by adaptively tuning TFisher thresholds, introduces an additional layer of dependence. Consequently, naive cross-method combination procedures that assume independence may be statistically miscalibrated and can become anti-conservative.

To address this, CATFISH interprets analytically derived omnibus statistics that aggregate across methods as descriptive summaries and derives final inferential conclusions using a null calibration procedure that explicitly preserves dependence. CATFISH provides two such dependence-aware null calibration strategies. The primary inferential approach is LD-aware multivariate normal (MVN) calibration, which, by construction, preserves pathway-specific gene–gene dependence as well as cross-method dependence. MAGMA gene-set analysis is explicitly built on the idea that genes are dependent due to LD, and it addresses this by modeling gene-level residuals as multivariate normal with correlation set to a gene–gene correlation matrix computed during gene analysis. This provides a principled motivation for using an MVN-based null generator when you subsequently apply alternative gene-set statistics to MAGMA gene outputs. In addition, CATFISH offers a global gene-set resampling mode, which maintains the empirical genome-wide distribution of gene-level test statistics and cross-method dependence, but does not explicitly retain the within-pathway LD structure. As the MVN-based approach more directly captures pathway-specific dependence, we recommend it as the default calibration strategy, while presenting the global resampling approach in Appendix A as an auxiliary option.



\subsection{LD-aware MVN omnibus calibration}

To account simultaneously for correlation among genes within a pathway and dependence among the component pathway tests, CATFISH calibrates the final omnibus statistic under a pathway-specific multivariate normal (MVN) null. For a pathway $S=\{g_1,\dots,g_d\}$, let $R_S$ denote the corresponding gene--gene correlation matrix, typically obtained from MAGMA-derived gene-correlation estimates. Under the LD-aware null, CATFISH generates replicate null gene-level score vectors as
\[
Z^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_S), \qquad b=1,\dots,B.
\]

For $p$-based component statistics, null gene-level $p$-values are obtained from the same latent MVN draw via a Gaussian-copula mapping. Under the default two-sided convention,
\[
U_g^{(b)}=\Phi\!\left(Z_g^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
p_g^{(b)} = 2\min\{U_g^{(b)},\,1-U_g^{(b)}\},
\]
where $\Phi(\cdot)$ denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function. This ensures that both $Z$-based and $p$-based component tests are recomputed from a common dependence-preserving null realization.

For each replicate, CATFISH recomputes all component pathway tests from the same simulated pathway and forms the replicate component vector
\[
\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S)
=
\Big(
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Fisher}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{TFisher}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{minP}}^{(b)}(S)
\Big),
\]
with the observed component vector $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)$ defined analogously from the real data. CATFISH then applies a prespecified omnibus operator $\mathcal{O}(\cdot)$, such as ACAT across methods or a minP-type across-method operator, to obtain
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S)\right),
\qquad
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)\right).
\]

The final MVN-calibrated omnibus $p$-value is estimated empirically as
\[
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)\le p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}.
\]

This calibration targets the null distribution of the complete pathway-analysis procedure rather than the marginal null distribution of each component test considered in isolation. Because all component statistics are recomputed from the same simulated pathway in each replicate, the MVN procedure preserves both within-pathway gene dependence and cross-method dependence by construction. In practice, CATFISH fixes the pathway-specific correlation matrix $R_S$ and repeatedly simulates correlated null gene-score vectors from this matrix; details on construction, stabilization, and factorization of $R_S$ are provided in Appendix~A.

In addition to this LD-aware MVN approach, CATFISH also supports global gene-set resampling as a secondary calibration mode. However, MVN calibration is the recommended inferential procedure because it directly preserves pathway-specific dependence structure induced by LD while calibrating the final omnibus statistic under the full analysis pipeline.


\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{LD-aware MVN calibration for pathway $S$}
\label{alg:mvn_calibration}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Pathway $S=\{g_1,\dots,g_d\}$, pairwise gene-correlation input, number of replicates $B$, omnibus operator $f_{\mathrm{omni}}$
\Ensure Calibrated omnibus $p$-value $\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)$

\State Construct the pathway-specific correlation matrix $R_S$
\State Set diagonal entries to 1
\State Fill off-diagonal entries with available pairwise correlations $r_{ij}$
\State Set missing pairs to 0
\State Clip extreme correlations if needed (e.g.\ $|r_{ij}|\le 0.999$)
\State Enforce positive-definiteness of $R_S$ if needed

\State Compute the observed component pathway $p$-values for $S$
\State Compute the observed omnibus value
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
=
f_{\mathrm{omni}}\!\left(\{p_j^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)\}\right)
\]

\For{$b=1,\dots,B$}
    \State Simulate correlated null gene-level scores
    \[
    Z^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,R_S)
    \]
    \State Map the same latent draw to null gene-level $p$-values using a Gaussian copula
    \[
    U_g^{(b)}=\Phi\!\left(Z_g^{(b)}\right),
    \qquad
    p_g^{(b)} = 2\min\{U_g^{(b)},\,1-U_g^{(b)}\}
    \]
    \State Recompute all $p$-based component tests from $\{p_g^{(b)}\}$
    \State Recompute Stouffer from $\{Z_g^{(b)}\}$
    \State Combine replicate component $p$-values using the same omnibus rule:
    \[
    p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)
    =
    f_{\mathrm{omni}}\!\left(\{p_j^{(b)}(S)\}\right)
    \]
\EndFor

\State Estimate the calibrated omnibus $p$-value as
\[
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)\le p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}
\]

\State \Return $\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Omnibus pathway combination across methods}

Let
\[
\mathcal{P}(S)=
\left\{
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Fisher}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{TFisher}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{minP}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S)
\right\}
\]
denote the available component pathway $p$-values for pathway $S$, with $K=|\mathcal{P}(S)| \le 5$. CATFISH provides two omnibus summary statistics: (i) ACAT-O, which aggregates method-specific $p$-values via a Cauchy-based transformation, and (ii) minP-O, which is defined as the minimum $p$-value across methods and is calibrated using an analytic mapping that assumes independence. As the component tests are dependent, these analytic omnibus values are mainly useful for descriptive ranking, whereas the primary inferential measure is the dependence-calibrated omnibus \$p\$-value obtained from the MVN procedure above.


\subsection{Multiple testing correction}

Across all pathways, final omnibus $p$-values were adjusted using the Benjamini--Hochberg FDR procedure. The final omnibus $p$-value is calibrated under the complete multi-test selection/combination procedure, so we report a single pathway-level $p$-value per pathway. Multiplicity adjustment is then applied across pathways using BH FDR.

\subsection{Candidate-gene prioritization}

To prioritize genes beyond top-SNP, MAGMA, or top-pathway evidence alone, we integrated support across these three complementary layers. For each gene, we summarized multi-layer support using a support-count plus strength score that gives primary weight to agreement across layers and secondary weight to continuous evidence strength within each layer. Genes supported by at least two layers were prioritized for downstream interpretation. The exact scoring rule is given in Appendix~B.


\section*{Pathway signal archetypes and archetype-recovery validation}

CATFISH was developed for analytical contexts in which the \emph{true} pattern of pathway-level signal is unknown. In practice, some pathways are driven predominantly by a single “bottleneck” gene, others exhibit weak but broadly distributed enrichment across many genes, and still others combine a small number of strong driver genes with more diffuse supporting effects. To systematize these distinct behaviors, we define five archetypal patterns of pathway signal and then use simulation studies to demonstrate that the component tests within CATFISH exhibit distinct and interpretable response profiles to each archetype.

\subsection*{Pathway signal archetypes: statistical signatures and biological intuition}

We divide pathway signals into a set of archetypes that describe different ways a pathway can be enriched in a biological sense, as explained in detail below. We then use a combination of statistical tests chosen to be representative of each of these behaviors and provide a biological example for each archetype \ref{fig:supp_archetypes_rankcurves}.

\subsubsection{Archetype I --- Sparse Driver Architecture (SDA)}

Sparse Driver Architecture (SDA) describes pathway/network patterns in which the overall signal is dominated by a small subset of strongly associated genes, while the majority of genes within the pathway exhibit behavior consistent with the null hypothesis. Formally, under SDA there exists a subset of genes with extremely small ordered $p$-values, $p_{(1)}, \dots, p_{(K)} \ll \alpha$, followed by a large collection of genes whose $p$-values, $p_{(K+1)}, \dots, p_{(G)}$, are approximately distributed as $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$. The ranked gene-level $p$-value curve therefore shows a pronounced elbow (Fig 1A) that suggests the association is driven by a small number of genes rather than by widespread enrichment across the entire pathway.

From a biological perspective, SDA is expected when the behavior of a pathway is governed predominantly by one or a small number of bottleneck genes, such as a committed-step enzyme, a rate-limiting regulatory factor, or an essential transporter, such that genetic effects are concentrated at a limited set of key control points (ref). A canonical example is aspartokinase in the aspartate-derived amino acid biosynthetic pathway. This pathway converts aspartate into the essential amino acids lysine, threonine, methionine, and isoleucine. Its initial reaction is catalyzed by aspartokinase, which phosphorylates aspartate to generate aspartyl-phosphate. As this enzyme functions at the entry point of a branched pathway, variation in its activity can influence flux across the entire network, whereas the effects of many downstream enzymes are more distributed or buffered. This network architecture is therefore expected to produce a sparse association pattern in which one or a few genes exhibit very strong signals, while the majority of genes remain close to null. Within CATFISH, pathways of the SDA type are most efficiently detected by ACAT and minP.

\subsubsection{Archetype II --- Coordinated Moderate Enrichment (CME)}

Coordinated Moderate Enrichment (CME) refers to architectures in which a large number of genes exhibit modest yet non-negligible statistical associations, without any single gene exerting a disproportionately strong influence on the overall signal. Here, a substantial proportion of genes attain moderately small $p$-values, typically on the order of $10^{-3}$ to $0.05$, and the most highly ranked genes are not several orders of magnitude more significant than the bulk of the distribution. Consequently, the ordered gene-level $p$-value profile is characterized by a broad shoulder rather than a pronounced elbow, indicating a diffuse enrichment pattern distributed across multiple members of the pathway.

From a biological standpoint, CME is anticipated where pathway behavior arises from the coordinated activity of numerous molecular components, particularly in systems characterized by redundancy, feedback loops, and multilayered regulatory architecture (ref). A canonical example is cytokine-driven immune signaling, notably TNF$\alpha$/IL-1$\beta$–to–NF-$\kappa$B pathways, in which the functional output depends on a cascade involving receptor complex assembly, adaptor protein recruitment, kinase activation, and negative feedback regulation, rather than being governed by a single dominant regulatory node. In such pathways, naturally occurring genetic variation is more likely to manifest as multiple modest perturbations distributed across the network than as a single variant exerting a large, isolated effect. Accordingly, CME-type pathways are characterized using Fisher’s method and Stouffer’s method, with the soft TFisher procedure also demonstrating strong performance under conditions of moderate tail enrichment.


\subsubsection*{Archetype III --- Diffuse Polygenic Enrichment (DPE)}

Diffuse Polygenic Enrichment (DPE) denotes architectures in which constituent genes exhibit, on average, slightly stronger association evidence than the genome-wide background, even though few or none individually surpass conventional significance thresholds. In such pathways, most gene-level p-values remain greater than 0.05, yet the aggregate evidence shows a subtle but systematic departure from the null distribution. This pattern is reflected as a mild excess of modestly small p-values and a gentle downward deflection in the ranked p-value curve relative to its null expectation. DPE therefore represents a broadly distributed, weak pathway-wide enrichment pattern across many genes.

From a biological perspective, DPE corresponds to canonical polygenic architectures in which pathway-level signals emerge from the cumulative contribution of many small effects. Human height is a classic example, as it is influenced by thousands of variants across many loci while most individual variant or gene effects are small. Instead, biologically coherent pathways, such as TGF-$\beta$ signaling, Hedgehog signaling, and genes involved in the growth-plate extracellular matrix, may exhibit enrichment only at the level of cumulative pathway-wide association. Under this regime, methods that are sensitive to distributed enrichment across many genes, such as Stouffer’s method and Fisher’s method, are more congruent with the underlying biological architecture.


\subsubsection{Archetype IV --- Hybrid Driver--Support (HDS)}

Hybrid Driver–Support (HDS) denotes architectures in which a limited number of genes exhibit strong or very strong association signals, while a broader set of additional genes provides more moderate evidence of association. The ranked gene-level profile combines features of both sparse and coordinated patterns. One or a few top genes show very strong significance, several others show moderate signals, and the rest are essentially null. This results in a pronounced peak at the top of the ranking, accompanied by a discernible “shoulder” of supporting signal, positioning HDS, on statistical grounds, between the SDA and the CME models.

From a biological perspective, HDS is expected where pathway activity is governed by a relatively small number of key regulatory factors but is further modulated by a broader, auxiliary network. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol metabolism provides an illustrative example. Genes such as \textit{LDLR}, \textit{APOB}, and \textit{PCSK9} function as principal determinants with well-characterized, large-effect contributions, whereas an extended set of genes involved in lipoprotein assembly, remodeling, and cholesterol transport exhibits smaller yet biologically meaningful effects. Consequently, pathway-level enrichment captures the combined influence of a few high-impact loci along with more modest, distributed contributions. Within CATFISH, this architecture aligns particularly well with the adaptive soft TFisher method and the classical Fisher combination test.

\subsubsection{Archetype V --- Single-Gene Proxy Pathway (SGP)}

The Single-Gene Proxy (SGP) pattern describes pathways that achieve statistical significance predominantly due to the presence of a single, highly associated gene, while the remaining genes are essentially null. In such cases, the leading gene exhibits an extremely small \(p\)-value, whereas the rest of the pathway shows negligible enrichment. Accordingly, the apparent pathway-level association largely disappears once this single gene is excluded from the analysis. From a statistical perspective, this configuration can be viewed as a boundary case of SDA. However, its substantive interpretation differs, as the pathway-level finding provides little additional information beyond what is already captured by the underlying gene-level association.

From a biological perspective, SGP emerges when a pathway annotation is very small or when multiple pathway definitions redundantly include the same major driver gene. A representative example is \textit{PAH} in phenylalanine metabolism. Phenylalanine hydroxylase represents the principal rate-limiting enzyme governing the conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine, and pathogenic variation in \textit{PAH} constitutes the predominant genetic cause of phenylketonuria and related forms of hyperphenylalaninemia. Although the broader pathway encompasses additional genes involved in transport, cofactor metabolism, and recycling, these typically exert much weaker effects at the population level. Within CATFISH, this pattern is most readily identified by minP and ACAT. However, such results should be interpreted conservatively as gene-centric associations, rather than as robust evidence that the pathway as a whole is jointly implicated.


\subsection{Archetype-recovery}

We conducted a targeted simulation study with correlated gene-level associations to assess whether CATFISH component tests can statistically identify the proposed pathway signal archetypes. Our aims were to determine if each archetype preferentially improves its corresponding component test and whether these method-specific performance profiles remain interpretable across pathway sizes. Because CATFISH uses non-directional MAGMA gene-level inputs in the present study, all simulated alternatives are defined in terms of the magnitude and distribution of pathway enrichment rather than signed positive versus negative gene effects.

\subsubsection*{Simulation design}

We performed a correlated MVN power/ranking simulation under
\[
Z \sim N(\mu,\Sigma),
\]
where $\Sigma$ is a correlation matrix with pairwise correlation $\rho = 0.20$, and $\mu$ specifies the archetype-specific enrichment profile on the latent normal-score scale. In this setting, departures from zero represent stronger aggregate association evidence rather than signed positive versus negative biological effects. In the primary simulation, pathway size was fixed at \(m = 20\) genes, and 500 independent replicates were generated per archetype. For each replicate, we applied the five CATFISH component tests (ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, Stouffer) and the omnibus test. Performance was assessed using: (i) statistical power at \(\alpha = 0.05\); (ii) the mean within-replicate rank of each method’s pathway-level \(p\)-value (lower is better); and (iii) the “top-method probability,” the probability that a method yields the smallest pathway-level \(p\)-value among all methods in a replicate.

To assess how pathway size affects archetype-recovery patterns identified at \(m = 20\), we repeated the simulations for pathway sizes \(m = 5, 20,\) and \(50\) genes (Supp.~Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_blockA_archetype_recovery_by_m}). To preserve each archetype’s qualitative structure across sizes, we scaled signal layers proportionally with \(m\) rather than fixing the absolute number of affected genes. Under this scaling, SDA consistently produced a sparse, strong-driver architecture with a small, highly perturbed layer. CME preserved a coordinated, moderate-effect architecture with most genes modestly perturbed. DPE maintained a diffuse, weak-enrichment architecture affecting most genes. HDS retained both a strong driver layer and a broader, moderately perturbed support layer. SGP remained a single-gene proxy boundary case.

\subsubsection*{Pathway-size sensitivity of archetype recovery}

Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_blockA_archetype_recovery_by_m} shows that the broad archetype-to-method mapping remains stable across pathway sizes, although the degree of separation among methods varies meaningfully with \(m\).

\paragraph{Sparse architectures remain the most stable across pathway sizes.}
The clearest, most consistent patterns occur under SDA and SGP. Under SDA, minP and ACAT dominate across all pathway sizes. For \(m = 5\), minP has the highest power (0.762), with ACAT close behind (0.720), and minP most often ranking first (top-method probability 0.504). For \(m = 20\), minP and ACAT again have the highest power (0.920 and 0.900), but ACAT most often yields the smallest \(p\)-value (0.710). For \(m = 50\), ACAT and minP have nearly identical power (0.958 and 0.964), yet ACAT overwhelmingly dominates winner frequency (0.882 vs. 0.048). Thus, under the sparse-driver architecture, extreme-tail–sensitive procedures consistently dominate. The minP test maximizes rejection probability, while ACAT increasingly dominates winner frequency as pathway size grows.

This pattern is even stronger under SGP. For \(m = 5\), SGP is detected almost exclusively by ACAT, minP, and TFisher, with minP having the highest power (0.958) and ACAT the highest top-method probability (0.772). For \(m = 20\), minP again has the highest power (0.908), while ACAT most often produces the smallest \(p\)-value (0.688). For \(m = 50\), minP remains the most powerful (0.938), and ACAT continues to dominate winner frequency (0.786). Across all pathway sizes, the omnibus procedure has relatively high power under SGP (0.926, 0.704, 0.632 for \(m = 5, 20, 50\)), yet it rarely attains the highest top-method probability (the probability of yielding the smallest p-value across methods). This aligns with SGP being a highly concentrated gene–proxy architecture, where procedures targeting extreme-tail signals are inherently favored.

\paragraph{DPE remains a diffuse-enrichment regime best captured by Stouffer-like behavior.}
Within the DPE archetype, absolute power differences across pathway sizes are modest, but rank-based and winner-based summaries are highly stable. At \(m = 5\), Stouffer’s method has the highest power (0.208), lowest mean rank (2.863), and largest top-method probability (0.442). At \(m = 20\), Fisher’s method, Stouffer’s method, and the omnibus procedure have nearly identical power (0.264, 0.266, 0.264), yet Stouffer again has the best mean rank (2.922) and highest top-method probability (0.384), indicating closer agreement with the diffuse weak-enrichment alternative. At \(m = 50\), Stouffer still gives the greatest power (0.270), best mean rank (3.110), and highest top-method probability (0.296), while the omnibus method retains similar power (0.268) but is rarely ranked first (0.062). Overall, these results support DPE as a genuine pathway-wide weak-enrichment regime. Stouffer’s method aligns most closely with the archetype, Fisher’s method remains competitive, and the omnibus procedure shows intermediate performance.

\paragraph{CME remains intermediate, but separation improves modestly with pathway size.}
CME remains the least clearly resolved archetype, though method performance becomes more structured as \(m\) increases. At \(m = 5\), Fisher’s and Stouffer’s methods have the highest power (0.330 and 0.326), Stouffer’s has the highest top-method probability (0.352), and the omnibus shows only moderate power (0.308). At \(m = 20\), TFisher is most powerful (0.486), Fisher’s has the best mean rank (2.808), and the omnibus is competitive but not dominant (power 0.448; mean rank 3.281). At \(m = 50\), TFisher strengthens further (power 0.552; top-method probability 0.340), Fisher’s still has the best mean rank (2.795), and the omnibus remains mid-range (power 0.478; mean rank 3.203; top-method probability 0.140). Overall, CME is the archetype most sensitive to pathway size, tending to stay in an intermediate regime rather than clearly approaching either a sparse-tail or diffuse weak-enrichment pattern.

\paragraph{HDS becomes increasingly favorable to balanced procedures as pathway size increases.}
Among the non-sparse archetypes, HDS depends most strongly on pathway size. At \(m = 5\), Fisher’s method has the highest power (0.566), Stouffer’s has the highest top-method probability (0.374), and the omnibus is competitive in power (0.536) but is never top-ranked, suggesting that very small pathways compress the driver-plus-support structure into a few moderate signals. At \(m = 20\), the expected HDS pattern emerges: TFisher has the highest power (0.606), Fisher’s has the best mean rank (2.765), and the omnibus remains close in power (0.568) but with a low top-method probability (0.040). At \(m = 50\), the HDS structure is clearer. TFisher has the highest power (0.718), the omnibus attains the best mean rank (2.899), and Fisher’s and TFisher both retain substantial top-method probabilities (0.196 and 0.282). Thus, as pathway size grows, HDS becomes more statistically identifiable, and the omnibus procedure becomes more competitive once both driver and support layers are well represented.

\paragraph{The omnibus is rarely the winner, but usually remains in the competitive middle.}
Across pathway sizes, the omnibus procedure is rarely the most archetype-aligned method under winner-based summaries, but it also seldom falls into the lowest performance tier except in extremely sparse settings. In SDA and SGP, it retains appreciable power but is consistently outperformed by ACAT and minP under both rank- and winner-based summaries. In DPE, it has power similar to the leading procedures but worse rank stability than Stouffer. In CME and HDS, it is generally intermediate but becomes more competitive as pathway size increases. 

These results indicate that the CATFISH component tests are not redundant. Sparse architectures favor ACAT and minP, diffuse architectures favor Stouffer- or Fisher-type aggregation, and hybrid architectures increasingly favor Fisher and TFisher as pathway size increases. In this setting, the omnibus procedure is best viewed as a hedge rather than as an architecture-specific optimum. It is seldom the single most powerful test for any one archetype, but it retains meaningful power across all five and is most useful when the underlying pathway architecture is unknown.


\subsection{Effect-size sensitivity across pathway signal archetypes}

To characterize effect-size sensitivity within each pathway signal family, we performed an archetype-specific power simulation using correlated gene-level statistics
\[
Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma),
\]
with exchangeable within-pathway correlation $\rho = 0.20$. We evaluated pathway sizes
\[
m \in \{5,20,50\},
\]
five archetypes (SDA, CME, DPS, HDS, SGP), and effect-size multipliers
\[
\{0.5,\,0.8,\,1.0,\,1.2,\,1.5,\,1.8\}
\]
applied to the baseline archetype-specific mean-shift layers. For each $(m,\text{archetype},\text{effect})$ setting, we generated 250 replicates and computed ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, Stouffer, and omnibus $p$-values using MVN null calibration ($\alpha = 0.05$).

Across archetypes, no single component test was uniformly best, and the identity of the top method changed with architecture, pathway size, and effect size (power curves figure). Sparse settings (SDA, SGP) were primarily dominated by ACAT/minP, DPS was most often led by Stouffer, and HDS/CME showed mixed leadership among TFisher/Fisher/Stouffer depending on $m$ and effect strength. This finding supports the intended view of component complementarity. Each test is sensitive to the underlying architecture rather than being a redundant measure.

The omnibus pattern is best summarized by power regret (second figure), defined as
\[
\max_j \mathrm{Power}_j - \mathrm{Power}_{\mathrm{Omnibus}}
\]
within each setting. Regret was smallest in DPS (mean 0.024; within 0.05 of best in 88.9\% of settings), and moderate in CME/HDS (means 0.050 and 0.047; within 0.05 in 61.1\% and 72.2\%). Regret was larger in sparse regimes, especially SGP (mean 0.148; max 0.500) and SDA (mean 0.088; max 0.380), where tail-specialized tests are expected to dominate. Thus, the omnibus is not a universally optimal solution, it is a hedging strategy that is nearly optimal in diffuse or heterogeneous architectures but less competitive in highly sparse ones. 

This sensitivity analysis complements archetype recovery by showing that the same qualitative pattern holds as effect size changes. Specialized tests perform best on matching architectures, while the omnibus procedure delivers robust, cross-architecture performance when the true pathway signal form is unknown.

\section{Results and discussion}

\subsection{Simulation-based validation of CATFISH pathway tests}

To evaluate CATFISH under realistic pathway dependence, we performed MVN-based simulations that varied (i) signal architecture, (ii) pathway size and effect strength, (iii) within-pathway LD (gene–gene correlation), and (iv) calibration information quality, including missing/zero-imputed correlations and component-misspecification stress scenarios. Full simulation details and parameters are in Appendix 1. Here, we summarize the main findings.

\subsubsection{Null calibration under LD and robustness to missing correlations}

We simulated MVN gene-level \(Z\)-scores under three LD scenarios: (i) LD\_independent (\(\rho=0\)), (ii) LD\_moderate (\(\rho=0.3\)), and (iii) LD\_strong (\(\rho=0.7\)), across pathway sizes \(m\in\{5,25,50\}\) (used throughout the null-calibration analyses unless otherwise noted). For each LD\(\times m\) condition, we generated 500 null replicates, computed ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, Stouffer, and omnibus \(p\)-values, and summarized calibration using genomic-control inflation \(\lambda\) and empirical type I error at \(\alpha=0.05\). For a well-calibrated test under the null, \textit{λ} should be close to 1 and the empirical type I error should be close to 0.05. MVN calibration was based on condition-specific empirical null distributions precomputed from 1000 MVN null draws per condition. We report both analytic calibration (independence-based reference distributions) and MVN calibration (empirical null calibration from LD-matched MVN resampling). Thus, all methods are evaluated on the same MVN-generated data, but with different p-value calibration schemes (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockB_null_calibration}).

\paragraph{Null calibration under LD.}

Figure~\ref{fig:blockB_null_calibration} shows that calibration is acceptable under LD\_independent for most methods, but deteriorates under LD\_moderate and LD\_strong when analytic assumptions ignore correlation. In the LD\_independent setting, empirical type I error remains near nominal for ACAT (mean 0.045), Fisher (0.051), Stouffer (0.055), minP (0.043), and OMNIBUS\_MVN (0.038), with only mild drift for \texttt{OMNIBUS\_Analytical} (0.056). The main outlier is TFisher, which shows low $\lambda$ (mean 0.234) despite only moderate type I deviation (mean 0.065), highlighting that $\lambda$ and tail error are not always aligned (Table).

Under LD\_moderate and LD\_strong, several analytically calibrated methods become clearly anti-conservative. The mean type I error increases to 0.174 and 0.222 for Fisher, 0.214 and 0.297 for Stouffer, 0.103 and 0.102 for TFisher, and 0.180 and 0.243 for OMNIBUS\_Analytical (Table). ACAT is comparatively stable but still mildly inflated (0.067 and 0.061). ACAT was the least anti-conservative component test overall under correlated nulls. In contrast, OMNIBUS\_MVN remains close to the nominal level across LD settings, with a type I error of 0.060 under LD\_moderate and 0.053 under LD\_strong (ranges across pathway sizes: 0.052--0.066 and 0.044--0.066, respectively). For example, at LD\_moderate with $m=50$, type I error is 0.052 for OMNIBUS\_MVN versus 0.072 for ACAT and 0.224 for OMNIBUS\_Analytical, and at LD\_strong with $m=50$, OMNIBUS\_MVN is slightly conservative (0.044) while OMNIBUS\_Analytical is strongly inflated (0.326). Overall, these results support OMNIBUS\_MVN as the most reliable default for controlling false positives under LD, while remaining well-calibrated under LD\_independent.

\paragraph{Robustness to missing correlations.} 
We next evaluated whether missing gene--gene correlations affect the omnibus statistic. Empirical analyses in real biological data often yield only partial estimates of gene--gene correlations, so we evaluated omnibus calibration under incomplete correlation matrices (Supp.~Fig.~\ref{fig:blockC_missing_corr}). Null data were generated from a true block-correlation matrix (\(\rho=0.5\)) for pathway sizes \(m\in\{5,25,50\}\). We then removed a fraction of off-diagonal entries (\(0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7\)) by setting them to zero, symmetrized the matrix, and projected to the nearest positive-definite correlation matrix before MVN null resampling. We compared three omnibus strategies: (i) analytic fallback (ignores correlation), (ii) MVN calibration with the full true correlation matrix (true correlation), and (iii) MVN calibration with the damaged/zero-imputed correlation matrix.

Two patterns were consistent. First, the analytic fallback was strongly miscalibrated across all settings, with a mean type I error \(0.201\) (max \(0.284\)) and mean \(\lambda=0.293\). Second, MVN calibration substantially improved calibration. The true correlation MVN gave a mean type I error \(0.054\) (max \(0.078\)) and mean \(\lambda=0.944\), while zero-imputed MVN gave a mean type I error \(0.076\) (max \(0.146\)) and mean \(\lambda=0.900\). Thus, zero-imputed MVN was clearly better than the analytic fallback, but generally less accurate than the true correlation MVN, especially at larger pathway sizes and higher missingness. For example, at a missing fraction \(0.7\) and \(m=50\), the type I error was \(0.284\) (analytic fallback), \(0.146\) (zero-imputed MVN), and \(0.052\) (MVN true correlation).

Overall, these results support using MVN calibration with the best available correlation estimate as the default. With incomplete correlations, zero-imputed MVN is still much safer than independence-based assumptions but should be treated as an approximation, not a fully calibrated replacement for a complete pathway correlation matrix. In stress tests, calibration error rose with pathway size and the fraction of missing entries, showing that incomplete matrices can leave residual anti-conservatism in large or poorly observed pathways.

\subsubsection{Stress test: omnibus robustness under component misspecification}

To evaluate robustness under component misspecification, defined here as anti-conservative component behavior (empirical type I error above the nominal level), we conducted a structured component-breakage stress test under the global null. For each LD setting (LD\(_\text{independent}\), LD\(_\text{moderate}\), LD\(_\text{strong}\)) and pathway size (\(m=5,25,50\)), we selected \(k=1,2,3,4\) of the five component tests (ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, Stouffer) and applied anti-conservative breakage to those selected components (broken \(p\)-values transformed as \(p^\gamma\), \(\gamma=0.7\)). For each \((m,\mathrm{LD},k)\), we evaluated all \(\binom{5}{k}\) breakage combinations, generated 500 null replicates per combination, and summarized metrics after averaging across combinations. In each replicate and breakage pattern, the omnibus statistic was recomputed from the broken component set and calibrated using a breakage-matched MVN null. Null component replicates were subjected to the same breakage pattern before constructing the omnibus reference distribution (\(B=1000\) null draws per condition; Fig.~\ref{fig:blockD_breakage}).


As the breakage extent increased (larger \(k\)), individual components became increasingly unstable, especially under LD\(_\text{moderate}\)/LD\(_\text{strong}\). For example, at \(k=4\), LD\(_\text{strong}\), \(m=25\), type I error reached 0.370 for Stouffer and 0.273 for Fisher, whereas the MVN-recalibrated omnibus remained 0.058. A similar pattern appeared at LD\(_\text{moderate}\), \(m=50\), \(k=4\): Stouffer 0.326, Fisher 0.294, omnibus 0.072. Even when inflation was milder, component trajectories generally worsened with \(k\), while the omnibus stayed near the nominal target. This pattern was observed across the LD and pathway sizes.

Across all stress-test settings, omnibus (MVN) had a mean type I error of 0.0595, a mean absolute deviation from 0.05 of 0.0113, and a maximum type I error of 0.0736, making it the closest method to nominal control overall. In contrast, the mean type I error was 0.220 for Stouffer, 0.178 for Fisher, 0.108 for ACAT, 0.104 for TFisher, and 0.0768 for minP. The corresponding \(\lambda\) trends showed the same instability pattern for several components (e.g., Stouffer up to \(\lambda\approx 2.21\)), whereas the omnibus \(\lambda\) remained comparatively flatter and closer to 1.

These results show that robustness requires calibration matched to both the LD structure and the component-failure pattern. In this stress setting, breakage-matched MVN recalibration preserves near-nominal error control for the omnibus test as misspecification increases.

\subsubsection{Adaptive calibration and leave-one-out diagnostics}

Finally, we evaluated two robustness-oriented diagnostics for the omnibus framework, namely (i) adaptive component filtering and (ii) leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:blockE_adaptive_loo}). In the adaptive procedure, component tests are first screened using training null simulations, and components showing poor calibration are excluded before recomputing the omnibus. We compared four omnibus variants
(i) \textbf{Analytical}, the omnibus computed from all five components using analytic calibration
(ii) \textbf{MVN}, the omnibus computed from all five components with MVN calibration
(iii) \textbf{Adaptive+Analytical}, the omnibus recomputed after filtering miscalibrated components and then analytically calibrated
(iv) \textbf{Adaptive+MVN}, the omnibus recomputed after filtering miscalibrated components and then MVN-calibrated.
For each $(m,\rho)$ setting ($m\in\{5,25,50\}$, $\rho\in\{0,0.3,0.7\}$), adaptive filtering used $n_{\text{train}}=200$ null replicates to screen components and $n_{\text{test}}=300$ null replicates for evaluation. The LOO analysis used $n=500$ null replicates per setting. MVN calibration used $B=1000$ null draws per condition.

In the averaged summary across pathway sizes and LD settings, the uncalibrated Analytical omnibus remained strongly anti-conservative (type I error $=0.156$) despite a conservative $\lambda$ estimate ($\lambda=0.439$), further illustrating that $\lambda$ alone can be misleading for calibration assessment. By contrast, both MVN-based variants were near nominal type I control (MVN $=0.041$, Adaptive+MVN $=0.053$) with $\lambda$ close to 1 (0.990 and 0.971, respectively). Adaptive+Analytical was also near nominal in this averaged view (0.047), but it remained highly conservative in $\lambda$ (0.454), indicating that analytic $p$-value construction is less stable than LD-aware MVN calibration. Both are well calibrated and similar in performance, so MVN is the simpler default and Adaptive+MVN is best treated as an optional robustness check rather than a clear improvement.

The LOO analysis provides a complementary sensitivity check by recomputing the MVN-calibrated omnibus after excluding each component in turn ($-$ACAT, $-$Fisher, $-$TFisher, $-$minP, $-$Stouffer) and comparing it with the full set (All). Across all exclusions, calibration remained stable. Type I error ranged from 0.051 to 0.058 and $\lambda$ ranged from 1.03 to 1.08, with no single exclusion producing a qualitative breakdown. This supports the interpretation that no individual component is solely driving omnibus calibration behavior and that MVN-based calibration provides a robust inferential layer for CATFISH under component perturbation.

\subsection{From SNP associations to pathway-level aggregation}

We performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) for stem volume in the sorghum association panel (SAP) (ref). The SNP scan identified multiple genome-wide significant signals, including a prominent peak on chromosome 9 (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}A). Using a genome-wide threshold of $-\log_{10}(p) > 7$, we mapped significant SNPs to 423 genes. Most strong associations occurred in a single interval on chromosome 9: 34 of the 36 top candidate genes fell within a $\sim$2.8~Mb region (56.1--58.9~Mb), indicating a major QTL for stem volume in this region.

We then aggregated SNP-level associations to genes using MAGMA, testing 33,967 annotated sorghum genes and assigning SNPs within gene boundaries plus $\pm$25~kb flanks. After Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction, 861 genes were significant at FDR $< 0.05$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}B). The top gene-level association was SORBI\_3009G206700 ($-\log_{10}(p) = 14.64$). Gene-level signals were similarly enriched on chromosome 9, mirroring the SNP-level peak and supporting a concentrated locus with multiple associated genes.

To assess pathway-level organization, we applied CATFISH pathway analysis to 410 sorghum PlantCyc metabolic pathways (ref). CATFISH evaluated pathway enrichment using five component tests (ACAT, Fisher's method, adaptive soft TFisher, minP, and Stouffer's method) and an omnibus test combining them. Statistical inference used MVN resampling, with gene--gene correlation matrices from MAGMA to account for LD. We identified 102 pathways at FDR $< 0.05$, of which 73 remained significant after Bonferroni correction ($p < 0.05/410$). Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}C shows the top 20 pathways and their $p$-value profiles across component and omnibus tests. The strongest pathway-level signals were detected for gluconeogenesis I (GLUCONEO-PWY), L-glutamate biosynthesis IV (GLUGLNSYN-PWY), L-glutamine degradation I (GLUTAMINDEG-PWY), phospholipases (LIPASYN-PWY), and aerobic respiration I (PWY-3781). Together, these pathways provide a coherent framework for interpreting variation in stem volume. Overall, these results show a clear progression from SNP-level associations to gene-level aggregation and then to pathway-level interpretation under LD-aware calibration (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure4}A--C).

\subsection{Integrated candidate gene analysis identifies high-confidence targets for stem volume and sucrose metabolism}

To prioritize genes supported by multiple modalities, we combined evidence from SNP-level GWAS, MAGMA gene-based tests, and CATFISH pathway enrichment (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}). Significance thresholds were defined as follows: GWAS $-\log_{10}(p) > 7$; MAGMA FDR $< 0.05$; and pathway support as membership in a Bonferroni-significant pathway with $p < 0.05$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}A). In total, 2,016 genes met at least one criterion, and 36 were supported by all three layers, forming the highest-confidence set. We then tested whether pathway membership was associated with stronger gene-level signals. Genes in significant pathways showed more extreme GWAS and MAGMA statistics than non-pathway genes (Wilcoxon $p = 6.34 \times 10^{-4}$ for GWAS; $p = 7.35 \times 10^{-3}$ for MAGMA; Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}C), indicating that pathway-level signals preferentially highlight genes with stronger trait-relevant associations rather than random pathway members. The correspondence between SNP- and gene-level association signals is shown in the GWAS versus MAGMA scatter plot (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure5}D). The 36 genes supported by all three analytic layers cluster in the upper-right quadrant (red), indicating a strong single-variant and aggregated gene-level association. This cross-layer concordance suggests a robust biological signal rather than method-specific artifacts.

These 36 triply supported genes constitute our primary set for downstream functional interpretation and experimental validation (Table~\ref{tab:top9_genes}). Although the stem-volume GWAS identified 423 significant genes and MAGMA identified 861, prioritizing biologically meaningful candidates from either layer alone is difficult because many loci are supported by only one framework. By integrating SNP-level associations, gene-level aggregation, and pathway-level enrichment, our multi-layer scoring strategy narrows this broad candidate space to a tractable subset of genes that pass all three filters. This prioritization is biologically informative rather than a collection of isolated statistical hits, and it helps us narrow down the list of candidate genes. The top-ranked genes converge on functions directly implicated in stem growth, biomass accumulation, and carbon-nitrogen homeostasis

Among these, SORBI\_3009G225700 is annotated for glutamate biosynthesis, glutamine turnover, and organic nitrogen assimilation. This is notable because the GS/GOGAT cycle is the central ammonium assimilation route in plants, converting glutamine to glutamate and supporting nitrogen remobilization to developing sink tissues (\cite{Tamura2011} Tabuchi et al., 2007; Tamura et al., 2011). In rice, disruption of NADH-GOGAT2 reduces biomass and grain yield, directly linking glutamate-mediated nitrogen reutilization to biomass production (Tamura et al., 2011). SORBI\_3009G231100, associated with CMP-KDO biosynthesis, implicates rhamnogalacturonan II (RG-II) assembly and primary cell wall integrity. In Arabidopsis, CMP-KDO synthetase activates KDO for RG-II incorporation, and loss of this activity impairs pollen tube elongation, showing that a fully assembled RG-II complex is required to maintain cell wall integrity in rapidly expanding tissues (Kobayashi et al., 2011). Together, these findings support a direct connection between the genes and stem expansion through cell wall architecture and growth biomechanics.

Additional top-ranked candidates further support a stem-focused signal. SORBI\_3009G233200, implicated in sucrose biosynthesis, is consistent with an SPS-related role in stem carbohydrate metabolism. In rice, SPS8 is the major stem-expressed sucrose phosphate synthase during grain filling. Its disruption reduces stem SPS activity, impairs the remobilization of non-structural carbohydrates, increases stem starch, and decreases grain yield (Ma et al., 2024). SORBI\_3009G225100, annotated in phosphatidylserine/phosphatidylethanolamine biosynthesis, is consistent with a SUI-family phosphatidylserine synthase. In rice, SUI-family genes regulate internode elongation and panicle rachis expansion, linking phospholipid metabolism to stem development and cell expansion (Yin et al., 2013). SORBI\_3009G229300 is associated with xylan biosynthesis, a key determinant of grass secondary cell wall architecture. In rice, GT43 genes are required for xylan backbone elongation, and complementation studies showed that appropriate GT43 members restore stem strength, vessel morphology, and xylan-related cell wall properties, underscoring the role of xylan biosynthesis in structural stem integrity (Lee et al., 2014).

Overall, these results indicate that our integrative framework effectively refines broad GWAS-, MAGMA-, and pathway-derived candidate sets into a smaller, high-confidence subset of genes enriched for stem-relevant biological functions, including nitrogen assimilation, carbohydrate remobilization, membrane lipid homeostasis, and cell wall biogenesis. Concordant support across these three levels strengthens confidence in these candidates and underscores the value of multi-layer integration for prioritizing genes. This framework also has broader translational relevance, as stem sugars and structural biomass are principal feedstocks for lignocellulosic bioethanol production (ref). Accordingly, the prioritized candidates may inform future efforts to improve both biomass accumulation and fermentable carbon availability.

\section{Conclusion}

CATFISH is a correlation-aware pathway analysis framework for genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in settings where pathway enrichment architecture is unknown and gene-level statistics are strongly correlated due to linkage disequilibrium (LD) and shared functional annotations. By computing multiple complementary gene-set statistics and combining them into an omnibus score, CATFISH provides an interpretable, portfolio-like summary of pathway evidence instead of relying on a single test optimized for a narrow class of alternatives. Its main innovation is an LD-aware multivariate normal (MVN) calibration of the entire multi-test procedure, using MAGMA-derived gene–gene correlation estimates and a shared latent MVN draw to recompute both Z-based and p value–based components. This calibration directly targets the null distribution of the full pipeline and mitigates anti-conservative behavior from analytic or independence assumptions applied to correlated gene sets. In simulations spanning varying LD strengths, pathway sizes, missing-correlation scenarios, and stress tests with component misspecification, MVN calibration yields robust type I error control, while the omnibus procedure maintains competitive power across sparse, diffuse, and hybrid enrichment models. In a sorghum stem-volume GWAS, CATFISH detects significant metabolic pathways and aids candidate gene prioritization by integrating SNP-, gene-, and pathway-level evidence. A practical limitation of the LD-aware MVN framework is that its calibration quality depends on the completeness and accuracy of the pathway-specific gene--gene correlation matrix. Although zero-imputation of missing correlations provides a useful fallback and performs substantially better than independence-based calibration, incomplete matrices can still introduce residual calibration error, particularly for larger pathways and under high missingness. Other limitations include reliance on MAGMA-based gene–gene correlation estimates to capture pathway-specific dependence, finite Monte Carlo resolution for extremely small p values, and sensitivity to gene definitions, reference panels, and pathway redundancy. Future work should benchmark CATFISH against established gene-set tools (including MAGMA’s competitive test and dependence-adjusted p value combination methods), extend validation to more GWAS traits and species, and release a fully reproducible software package with documented defaults and performance benchmarks.

\section{Bias warning}

Pathway enrichment analyses are susceptible to biases driven by gene size, pathway size, SNP coverage, and LD, which must be addressed explicitly \citep{White2020,PMC6391732}. CATFISH mitigates these effects in three steps. First, SNP-to-gene aggregation is performed with MAGMA's LD-aware multi-SNP model so that gene-level statistics account for local LD structure and SNP density. Second, MAGMA gene $Z$-scores are regressed on $\log(\text{gene length})$ and $\log(\text{number of SNPs})$, and the resulting residual-based $p_{\mathrm{adj}}$ is used for downstream pathway analysis to reduce residual dependence on gene size and SNP density. Third, pathway-level inference is based on LD-aware, gene-label permutations that preserve each gene's adjusted $p$-value and the observed distribution of pathway sizes, producing enrichment $p$-values that are robust to these established sources of bias.

Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6391732/

\section{Mathematical details of CATFISH}\label{app:catfish_math}

\subsection{Extended notation}

For a pathway (gene set) $S$, let $G = |S|$ denote the number of genes in the pathway. For each gene $g \in S$, let $p_g$ denote the gene-level association $p$-value and let $Z_g$ denote the corresponding gene-level $Z$ statistic when available. We collect these into pathway-level vectors
\[
\mathbf{p}_S = (p_1, p_2, \dots, p_G),
\qquad
\mathbf{Z}_S = (Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_G).
\]

When gene-level adjustment is applied, we denote the adjusted quantities by
\[
\mathbf{p}_{S,\mathrm{adj}} = (p_{1,\mathrm{adj}}, \dots, p_{G,\mathrm{adj}}),
\qquad
\mathbf{Z}_{S,\mathrm{adj}} = (Z_{1,\mathrm{adj}}, \dots, Z_{G,\mathrm{adj}}).
\]

Unless otherwise stated, definitions below are written using the unadjusted inputs.

\subsection{Gene-level association statistics}

Gene-level association evidence was obtained with MAGMA, which aggregates SNP-level signals within each gene while accounting for local LD via an external reference panel. We used MAGMA's SNP-wise gene model, which combines mean-like and top-SNP-like within-gene architectures into a single gene-level association summary. The resulting gene-level p-values and Z statistics from MAGMA were used as the common input for all downstream CATFISH pathway analyses.

\subsection{Gene-level adjustment for gene size and SNP density}

To reduce residual dependence of gene-level signal on gene size and SNP density, we optionally regressed the MAGMA gene-level $Z$ statistic on gene-level covariates. Let $L_g$ denote gene length and $S_g$ denote the number of SNPs mapped to gene $g$. We fit
\[
Z_g = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(L_g) + \beta_2 \log(S_g) + \varepsilon_g.
\]

We then define the adjusted residual score as
\[
Z_g^{\mathrm{adj}} = Z_g - \widehat{Z}_g,
\qquad
\widehat{Z}_g = \widehat{\beta}_0 + \widehat{\beta}_1 \log(L_g) + \widehat{\beta}_2 \log(S_g),
\]
and the corresponding adjusted two-sided $p$-value as
\[
p_{g,\mathrm{adj}} = 2\Phi\!\left(-\left|Z_g^{\mathrm{adj}}\right|\right),
\]
where $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

\subsection{Pathway-level component statistics}

As all pathway statistics are derived from the same within-pathway gene-level evidence, the component tests are mutually dependent. Independence-based reference nulls, therefore, serve only as analytic definitions. Final inference is obtained from the unified dependence-aware calibration framework described below.

\subsubsection{ACAT}

For each gene,
\[
t_g = \tan\left(\pi\left(\tfrac{1}{2} - p_g\right)\right).
\]
With nonnegative weights $w_g$ such that $\sum_{g \in S} w_g = 1$, the pathway-level ACAT statistic is
\[
T_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S)
=
\sum_{g \in S} w_g \tan\left(\pi\left(\tfrac{1}{2} - p_g\right)\right),
\]
with corresponding analytic combined $p$-value
\[
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S)
=
\tfrac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\left(T_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S)\right).
\]

\subsubsection{Fisher's method}

Fisher's pathway statistic is
\[
T_{\mathrm{Fisher}}(S) = -2\sum_{g \in S}\log(p_g).
\]
Under independence,
\[
T_{\mathrm{Fisher}}(S) \sim \chi^2_{2G},
\]
which yields the standard analytic Fisher $p$-value.

\subsubsection{Adaptive soft TFisher}

For a given soft threshold $\tau \in (0,1]$, the soft TFisher statistic is
\[
W^{\mathrm{soft}}(S;\tau)
=
\sum_{g \in S}
\left[-2\log(p_g) + 2\log(\tau)\right]_+,
\qquad
(x)_+ = \max(x,0).
\]

CATFISH evaluates this statistic across a fixed threshold grid
\[
\mathcal{T} = \{0.20, 0.10, 0.05, 0.01\},
\]
and defines the adaptive TFisher component as the minimum calibrated TFisher $p$-value across $\tau \in \mathcal{T}$.

\subsubsection{Stouffer's method}

For a pathway with $G$ genes, the default unweighted Stouffer statistic is
\[
Z_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S)
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{G}}\sum_{g \in S} Z_g,
\]
with one-sided analytic $p$-value
\[
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(Z_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S)\right).
\]

In our implementation, MAGMA gene-level inputs are non-directional and do not encode effect sign. Accordingly, we construct gene-level normal scores by monotone transformation of the MAGMA gene $p$-values, for example
\[
Z_g=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_g)
\]
for one-sided $p_g$, or
\[
Z_g=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_g/2)
\]
for two-sided $p_g$.
Under this formulation, Stouffer's method is used as an inverse-normal p-value combination procedure that aggregates evidence across genes within a pathway. It therefore tests for enrichment of small gene-level $p$-values, rather than for a signed or directional effect.

If nonnegative weights $w_g$ are supplied, the weighted version is
\[
Z_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}^{(w)}(S)
=
\frac{\sum_{g \in S} w_g Z_g}{\sqrt{\sum_{g \in S} w_g^2}}.
\]

Since, the MAGMA-derived gene-level inputs used here are non-directional, all CATFISH pathway architectures and simulations are defined by the magnitude, concentration, and distribution of enrichment across genes, rather than by signed positive or negative gene effects.

\subsubsection{minP}

The minP statistic is defined as
\[
T_{\min}(S) = \min_{g \in S} p_g.
\]
Under independence, its canonical Tippett/\v{S}id\'ak mapping is
\[
p_{\mathrm{minP}}(S)
=
1 - \left(1 - T_{\min}(S)\right)^G.
\]

\subsection{Sources of dependence}

Dependence in CATFISH arises at two levels. First, the component pathway tests are deterministically coupled because they all use the same within-pathway gene-level evidence, $\{p_g\}_{g \in S}$ and, when used, $\{Z_g\}_{g \in S}$. Thus, the resulting component pathway $p$-values are correlated even if the genes are independent. Under sparse architectures such as SDA and SGP, one or a few extremely small gene-level $p$-values drive multiple statistics. The same extreme gene determines $\min P$, pushes ACAT via its heavy-tail Cauchy transform, and contributes disproportionately to Fisher and TFisher. Under DPE, no single gene dominates, instead, a weak but widespread shift across many genes creates an excess of moderately small $p$-values and a modest shift in the mean gene-level $Z$ distribution. This broad signal is captured by Fisher and TFisher through the accumulation of many moderate $-\log(p)$ terms, and by Stouffer through coherent aggregation of $Z$ scores. As a result, Fisher, TFisher, and Stouffer become positively correlated under DPE-type alternatives, and Stouffer-like methods tend to perform best because they are directly tuned to elevation in association-strength scores (More examples in the archetype section) (Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_blockA_archetype_recovery_by_m})

Second, gene-level inputs within a pathway may be correlated due to LD, overlapping SNP mappings, shared genomic structure, or broader polygenic effects, creating additional gene–gene dependence and strengthening deterministic coupling among component tests. Post hoc choices across methods, such as taking the minimum statistic or adaptively selecting the TFisher threshold, further increase dependence. Thus, analytic across-method combinations that assume independence can be miscalibrated, motivating the dependence-aware null calibration framework described below.

\subsection{Numerical safeguards}

To avoid numerical instability in $\log(\cdot)$ and Cauchy transforms, gene-level $p$-values are clipped to
\[
p_g \leftarrow \min\{1-p_{\min}, \max(p_g, p_{\min})\},
\qquad p_{\min}=10^{-15}.
\]

If a gene appears multiple times in a pathway definition, duplicate entries are collapsed prior to pathway analysis so that each gene contributes only once. When duplicate gene entries occur, the retained gene-level value is the minimum observed $p$-value for that gene.

\subsection{Omnibus pathway combination across methods}

Let the available component pathway $p$-values for pathway $S$ be
\[
\mathcal{P}(S)
=
\left\{
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Fisher}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{TFisher}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{minP}}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}(S)
\right\},
\]
with $K = |\mathcal{P}(S)| \le 5$ depending on whether Stouffer is available.

\subsubsection{ACAT-O}

With method-level weights $v_j \ge 0$ satisfying $\sum_{j=1}^{K} v_j = 1$, the across-method ACAT omnibus (default) is
\[
T_{\mathrm{omni,ACAT}}(S)
=
\sum_{j=1}^{K} v_j \tan\!\bigl(\pi(0.5-p_j)\bigr),
\]
and
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni,ACAT}}(S)
=
0.5-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\!\bigl(T_{\mathrm{omni,ACAT}}(S)\bigr).
\]

\subsubsection{minP-O}

The across-method minimum is
\[
T_{\mathrm{omni,min}}(S)=\min_{j=1,\dots,K} p_j(S),
\]
with analytic independence-based mapping
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni,min}}(S)
=
1-\bigl(1-T_{\mathrm{omni,min}}(S)\bigr)^K.
\]

\subsection{Dependence-aware null calibration}

As both the gene-level inputs and the component tests are dependent, CATFISH calibrates the omnibus using a null generator that preserves this dependence. We support two complementary strategies for null generation, namely global gene-set resampling (also analytic) and LD-aware MVN calibration. Global resampling preserves the empirical genome-wide marginal distribution of gene-level evidence and the cross-method coupling, but it does not model within-pathway LD. MVN calibration explicitly preserves within-pathway gene--gene dependence and is the recommended inferential procedure.

\subsubsection{Global resampling}

In the global resampling mode, a null pathway of size $d$ is generated by sampling $d$ genes from a genome-wide pool of genes with valid MAGMA outputs. Each sampled gene contributes its paired evidence $(p_g, Z_g)$ when $Z_g$ is available, ensuring that both $p$-based and $Z$-based component tests are recomputed from the same resampled gene set in each null replicate. For replicate $b$, CATFISH recalculates all component pathway statistics on the resampled pathway and applies the same omnibus operator used for the observed data.

Formally, let $\mathcal{G}$ denote the genome-wide pool of genes with valid pathway inputs, and let pathway $S$ have size $|S|=d$. For each replicate $b=1,\dots,B$, CATFISH samples $d$ genes from $\mathcal{G}$, typically without replacement when $d \le |\mathcal{G}|$, and constructs a null pathway-level evidence set
\[
\mathbf{p}^{(b)}_{\mathrm{global}}(S), \qquad \mathbf{Z}^{(b)}_{\mathrm{global}}(S).
\]
All component tests are recomputed on this resampled pathway, yielding replicate component $p$-values $\{p_j^{(b)}(S)\}$, and the omnibus operator is then applied:
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)
=
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\{p_j^{(b)}(S)\}\right).
\]

If $p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)$ denotes the observed omnibus value, the globally resampled omnibus $p$-value is estimated as
\[
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,global}}(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)\le p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}.
\]

This approach preserves the empirical genome-wide marginal distribution of gene-level evidence and captures cross-method coupling because all component tests are recomputed from the same sampled gene set. However, it does not preserve pathway-specific LD structure and is therefore treated as a secondary calibration mode relative to the LD-aware MVN procedure.

\subsubsection{LD-aware MVN calibration}

In the LD-aware mode, CATFISH constructs a pathway-specific gene--gene correlation matrix $R_S$ and generates null gene-level score vectors as
\[
Z^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_S),
\qquad b=1,\dots,B.
\]
Here, $R_S$ is treated as fixed for a given pathway and encodes the expected dependence among gene-level statistics under the null. The MVN procedure does not resample the correlation matrix itself, rather, it repeatedly generates new null gene-score vectors whose joint covariance structure is determined by $R_S$.

\paragraph{Construction of the pathway-specific correlation matrix.}
For each pathway $S=\{g_1,\dots,g_d\}$, the pathway-specific gene--gene correlation matrix $R_S$ is constructed from pairwise gene-correlation estimates, typically obtained from MAGMA output. Diagonal entries are set to 1, and off-diagonal entries are filled using the available pairwise correlations for genes within the pathway. When a gene pair is missing from the pairwise correlation output, the corresponding entry is set to 0 by default. This missing-as-zero convention corresponds to assuming independence for unobserved gene pairs while retaining the observed correlation structure.

Formally, if $r_{ij}$ denotes the available pairwise correlation between genes $g_i$ and $g_j$, then
\[
(R_S)_{ii}=1,
\qquad
(R_S)_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
r_{ij}, & \text{if the pairwise estimate is available,} \\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

\paragraph{Numerical stabilization of the MVN null.}
As pathway-specific correlation matrices can be ill-conditioned or only approximately positive definite, CATFISH applies numerical stabilization before MVN simulation when needed. In practice, this can include clipping extreme correlations (for example, $|r_{ij}| \le 0.999$), imputing missing entries to 0 as described above (Fig. \ref{fig:blockC_missing_corr}) , and applying a nearest correlation-matrix correction \citep{Higham2002} or adding a small diagonal jitter. These steps improve numerical stability during MVN simulation without altering the interpretation of the LD-aware null.

\paragraph{Practical implications of incomplete correlation matrices.}
The missing-as-zero convention is a pragmatic approximation used when pairwise gene–gene correlations are unavailable. It treats unobserved gene pairs as independent and, when needed, applies numerical regularization so the resulting correlation matrix is positive semidefinite and suitable for MVN simulation. This improves numerical stability and robustness but does not ensure that the matrix accurately reflects the true null dependence structure. If the true but unobserved correlations are nonzero, imputing them as zero can systematically distort the null covariance model and affect the calibration of the omnibus \(p\)-value. The extent of distortion depends on the proportion of missing entries, pathway dimensionality, and the strength and pattern of the omitted correlations. Thus, CATFISH should use the most complete and accurate pathway-specific correlation estimates available, and results based on highly incomplete matrices should be interpreted cautiously.

\paragraph{Generation of correlated null gene-level scores.}
To simulate from the pathway-specific null, CATFISH factorizes the stabilized matrix $R_S$ as
\[
R_S = LL^\top,
\]
where $L$ is a lower-triangular matrix, for example, obtained by a Cholesky decomposition when $R_S$ is positive definite. Let
\[
u^{(b)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)
\]
denote a vector of independent standard normal draws of length $d$, where $I_d$ is the $d\times d$ identity matrix. CATFISH then forms the correlated null gene-score vector as
\[
Z^{(b)} = L u^{(b)}.
\]
By construction,
\[
\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(Z^{(b)}\right)
=
L\,\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(u^{(b)}\right)L^\top
=
L I_d L^\top
=
LL^\top
=
R_S.
\]
Thus, the simulated null scores are random but not independent. Genes with high pairwise correlation in $R_S$ tend to co-vary across null replicates, whereas genes with near-zero correlation vary more independently. This preserves the gene-level dependence structure induced by LD in the pathway-specific null model.

\paragraph{Gaussian-copula mapping to gene-level $p$-values.}
For $p$-based component tests, null gene-level $p$-values are derived from the same latent MVN draw using a Gaussian copula:
\[
U_g^{(b)} = \Phi\!\left(Z_g^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
p_g^{(b)} = 2\min\{U_g^{(b)}, 1-U_g^{(b)}\}.
\]
This preserves dependence across genes while ensuring coherent null inputs across both $Z$-based and $p$-based component statistics. If a one-sided gene-level convention is used instead, the mapping may be written as
\[
p_g^{(b)} = 1-\Phi\!\left(Z_g^{(b)}\right),
\]
with the direction chosen appropriately.

\paragraph{Component recomputation and omnibus calibration.}
For each replicate, CATFISH computes the replicate component vector
\[
\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S)
=
\big(
p_{\mathrm{ACAT}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Fisher}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{TFisher}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{Stouffer}}^{(b)}(S),\,
p_{\mathrm{minP}}^{(b)}(S)
\big),
\]
and then applies the omnibus operator
\[
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S)\right).
\]

If $p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)$ denotes the observed omnibus value from the real pathway data, the calibrated omnibus $p$-value is
\[
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{(b)}(S)\le p_{\mathrm{omni}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}.
\]

This unified calibration targets the null distribution of the full pathway-analysis procedure by recomputing all component tests and the omnibus operator under the same dependence‑preserving null draw. As all component statistics are computed from the same simulated pathways, the MVN procedure preserves both within-pathway gene dependence and cross-method dependence. CATFISH therefore does not calibrate each component and then recalibrate the omnibus. Instead, the recommended inferential quantity is the omnibus statistic calibrated once under the full MVN null pipeline.

\subsection{MVN calibration of component statistics}

CATFISH can optionally report MVN-calibrated component $p$-values for diagnostic purposes. For a given component $j$, its MVN-calibrated value is
\[
\hat p_j(S)
=
\frac{
1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(
p_j^{(b)}(S)\le p_j^{\mathrm{obs}}(S)
\right)
}{
B+1
}.
\]

To evaluate whether the MVN framework improves calibration for individual component tests as well as the omnibus statistic, we compared analytically versus MVN-calibrated component \(p\)-values under the null across LD\(_\text{independent}\), LD\(_\text{moderate}\), and LD\(_\text{strong}\) settings for \(m = 5, 25, 50\) (Supp.~Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_blockG_component_mvn}). Under LD\(_\text{independent}\), analytic and MVN-calibrated results were similar for most components, reflecting minimal dependence. Under LD\(_\text{moderate}\) and LD\(_\text{strong}\), however, analytically calibrated Fisher and Stouffer statistics became strongly anti-conservative, with type I error rates far above 0.05. TFisher showed method-specific miscalibration, and minP showed milder deviations from nominal levels. In contrast, MVN-calibrated component tests stayed close to nominal performance across LD structures and pathway sizes, with \(\lambda \approx 1\) and type I error near 0.05. ACAT was relatively well calibrated even analytically, but MVN calibration further harmonized its behavior with the other components. These results indicate that LD-aware MVN calibration functions as intended at the component-test level. However, since component statistics remain jointly dependent after marginal calibration, final inference should still rely on the direct calibration of the omnibus statistic.

\subsection{Monte Carlo resolution, tail refinement, and recommended practice}

Because CATFISH calibrates the omnibus $p$-value via finite-sample null resampling, the resulting Monte Carlo $p$-values are discrete, with resolution determined by the number of resamples $B$. Under the plus-one correction, the smallest empirical $p$-value is about $1/(B+1)$, so permutation or Monte Carlo $p$-values should never be reported as exactly zero \citep{Phipson2010}. Thus, a fixed resampling budget is adequate for exploratory pathway screening but can be insufficient for accurately estimating very small tail probabilities. More broadly, the Monte Carlo and permutation-testing literature shows that $p$-value resolution is intrinsically limited by the number of null draws, and that reliable estimation of extreme tails quickly becomes computationally expensive under naive fixed-budget resampling \citep{Knijnenburg2009,Che2014}.

When extremely small pathway $p$-values are scientifically important, several extensions are available. One is adaptive or sequential resampling, which targets additional null draws to the most promising pathways instead of wasting effort on clearly null cases; this approach has a long history in Monte Carlo testing and has been adapted to large-scale genomics \citep{Besag1991,Gandy2009,Che2014}. A second is explicit tail modeling, for example fitting an extreme-value distribution such as a generalized Pareto to the most extreme null replicates, which can greatly improve very small $p$-value estimates with far fewer simulations than the empirical estimator \citep{Knijnenburg2009}. Finally, for ACAT-type combination tests, analytic Cauchy-tail approximations provide accurate small-$p$ behavior under broad dependence structures without explicit tail resampling for that component \citep{LiuXie2020}.

In practice, we recommend a moderate fixed resampling budget for the initial genome-wide pathway scan, followed by adaptive refinement for the top pathways. Omnibus $p$-values should use the plus-one estimator $(e+1)/(B+1)$, where $e$ is the number of null replicates at least as extreme as the observed statistic, to avoid zero-valued Monte Carlo $p$-values \citep{Phipson2010}. A fixed $B$ suffices for broad screening and ranking, whereas pathways near the reporting threshold or multiple-testing cutoff should be re-evaluated with additional resampling to sharpen tail estimates \citep{Che2014}. For further methodological advances, sequential stopping rules or generalized-Pareto tail fitting offer principled ways to estimate extreme tails more efficiently, while analytic ACAT tails remain useful for interpreting ACAT-type components in isolation \citep{Knijnenburg2009,LiuXie2020}. Within the current CATFISH framework, the most natural extension is adaptive resampling of the final omnibus statistic for top pathways, which preserves dependence-aware calibration while increasing resolution only where extra precision is scientifically warranted.

\subsection{Choice of final omnibus \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-value}

Depending on the calibration mode, CATFISH may report an analytic omnibus, a globally resampled omnibus, or an MVN-calibrated omnibus. The final pathway-level omnibus is chosen as

\[
\begin{aligned}
p_{\mathrm{omni,final}}(S)
&=
\begin{cases}
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,mvn}}(S), & \text{if MVN calibrated,} \\[4pt]
\hat p_{\mathrm{omni,global}}(S), & \text{else if globally calibrated,} \\[4pt]
p_{\mathrm{omni,analytic}}(S), & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\]

\subsection{Default implementation choices}

Unless otherwise stated, CATFISH uses equal weights within ACAT and ACAT-O, an adaptive soft-TFisher threshold grid $\mathcal{T}=\{0.20, 0.10, 0.05, 0.01\}$, one-sided Stouffer aggregation for MAGMA association-strength $Z$ scores, clipping of gene-level $p$-values at $p_{\min}=10^{-15}$, and LD-aware MVN calibration as the primary inferential mode. Missing pairwise gene correlations are set to 0 when constructing pathway-specific correlation matrices, with numerical stabilization applied as needed to ensure positive-definite MVN simulation.

\section{Candidate-gene prioritization score}\label{app:candidate_score}

To prioritize genes supported across multiple analytical layers, we combined evidence from GWAS locus support, MAGMA gene-level significance, and pathway-level enrichment. For each gene $g$, the composite score was defined as
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{score}(g)
&= \mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{GWAS}\}
 + \mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{MAGMA}\}
 + \mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{PATH}\} \\
&\quad + 0.2\left[-\log_{10}\!\left(p_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}\right)\right] \\
&\quad + 0.1\left[-\log_{10}\!\left(p_{\mathrm{GWAS}}\right)\right] \\
&\quad + 0.1\left[-\log_{10}\!\left(p_{\mathrm{PATH}}\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

This formulation gives primary weight to agreement across discrete evidence layers while allowing modest within-layer ranking by continuous signal strength. Genes supported by at least two layers were prioritized for downstream interpretation. The MAGMA continuous term was upweighted (0.2 vs 0.1 for GWAS and pathway terms) because MAGMA provides direct gene-level aggregate evidence, whereas GWAS is locus-centric and pathway enrichment is set-level. This weighting was intended to improve within-gene ranking resolution without overriding the primary cross-layer agreement indicators.



\section{Evaluation of Pathway Enrichment Methods for Sorghum Stem Volume}

To assess consistency in pathway enrichment across p-value combination methods, we compared significant pathways from each CATFISH component test (ACAT, Fisher, TFisher, minP, Stouffer) and the omnibus test using Bonferroni correction ($p < 0.05/410$). Figure~\ref{fig:suppfig}A) shows substantial but incomplete overlap. TFisher detected the most significant pathways (84), followed by Omnibus (73), Fisher (48), ACAT (47), minP (47), and Stouffer (24). Nineteen pathways were significant in all six methods, indicating highly robust signals. ACAT and minP had identical significant pathway sets (Jaccard similarity = 1.0), consistent with their shared sensitivity to sparse, strong signals.

Furthermore, we characterized the omnibus p-value distribution using a quantile–quantile plot of observed versus expected \(-\log_{10}(p)\) values across all pathways (\(n=410\); Fig.~\ref{fig:suppfig}B). Pathways were color-coded by significance as Bonferroni-significant (red), Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) FDR \(<0.05\) but not Bonferroni-significant (blue), and non-significant (gray). Most non-significant pathways align with the diagonal, indicating little global inflation of test statistics, whereas deviation in the upper tail (blue/red) suggests genuine pathway-level enrichment. The 73 Bonferroni-significant pathways cluster near the minimum attainable p-value under MVN resampling (\(-\log_{10}(p)\approx 4\), i.e., \(p\approx10^{-4}\) for \(B=10{,}000\) resamples), and 102 pathways are significant at BH FDR \(<0.05\).

Next, the pairwise relationship among methods was assessed using the Spearman correlation of $-\log_{10}(p)$ values (upper triangle) and the Jaccard similarity of significant pathway sets (lower triangle) (Figure~\ref{fig:suppfig}C). All methods showed high pairwise correlations ($\rho = 0.71$--$0.97$), indicating consistent pathway ranking despite differences in significant set membership. The highest correlations were observed between ACAT and minP ($\rho = 0.97$), ACAT and TFisher ($\rho = 0.95$), and Omnibus with all component tests ($\rho = 0.84$--$0.97$). Stouffer showed the lowest correlations with other methods ($\rho = 0.71$--$0.90$), consistent with its distinct weighting scheme that is more robust to outliers. Jaccard similarities of significant sets ranged from 0.29 (Stouffer vs.\ TFisher) to 1.00 (ACAT vs.\ minP). The Omnibus test showed the highest Jaccard overlap with TFisher (0.87), reflecting TFisher's dominant contribution to the omnibus statistic for pathways with hybrid signal patterns. The lower Jaccard values for Stouffer (0.29--0.50) reflect its greater selectivity for pathways with coordinated, diffuse signals.

Finally, the distribution of $-\log_{10}(p)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:suppfig}D) revealed method-specific characteristics. TFisher produced the highest median $-\log_{10}(p)$, consistent with its sensitivity to pathways containing both strong driver genes and moderate supporting signals. ACAT and minP showed similar distributions with heavy right tails, reflecting their power for sparse signal detection. Fisher and Stouffer showed more symmetric distributions centered at lower $-\log_{10}(p)$ values. The Omnibus distribution combined features of all component methods, providing balanced sensitivity across signal patterns.

Together, these comparisons show that the individual CATFISH component tests capture complementary pathway-level enrichment patterns for sorghum stem volume. ACAT and minP behaved similarly, mainly detecting sparse, driver-like association signals, whereas Stouffer highlighted a distinct set of pathways consistent with coordinated, polygenic enrichment. Despite high rank-based concordance, the pathways exceeding significance thresholds overlapped only partially, indicating that thresholded discoveries are strongly method-dependent. The omnibus test reduces this dependence by combining evidence across component tests while retaining substantial power across different signal architectures. Its quantile–quantile behavior under MVN resampling also suggests adequate global calibration, with upper-tail deviations more consistent with true biological enrichment than with general statistical inflation.
