\abstract{
\textbf{Motivation:} Pathway analysis of genome-wide association study (GWAS)
summary statistics must contend with two distinct sources of dependence. Genes
within a pathway are correlated through linkage disequilibrium (LD), and
pathways are correlated with one another through shared gene membership. Most
pipelines address only the first, calibrating each pathway against its own
LD-aware null and then applying a Bonferroni or Benjamini--Hochberg correction
that assumes the resulting pathway $p$-values are independent. They are not,
and the correction is consequently miscalibrated in a direction that depends on
how strongly the pathway family overlaps.\\
\textbf{Results:} We extend CATFISH, a correlation-aware omnibus pathway
framework combining ACAT, Fisher, adaptive soft TFisher, minP and Stouffer
statistics, with a Westfall--Young-style family-wise calibration. A single
genome-wide correlated null draw, generated from a multivariate normal model of
gene--gene correlation, is propagated through every pathway in the family; read
down its columns it gives each pathway a calibrated marginal $p$-value, and read
across its rows the minimum over pathways gives a family-wise threshold that
accounts for between-pathway dependence without modelling it. Numerical
precision is treated as a correctness requirement: in a GWAS of $n\approx1.3$
million individuals the omnibus statistic underflows double precision for the
majority of pathways, and the resulting ties silently destroy the ranking.
Across simulated null families spanning three LD regimes and three pathway
sizes, independence-based calibration was anti-conservative while per-pathway
and Westfall--Young calibration held type~I and family-wise error near nominal,
and both remained stable when up to four of the five component tests were
deliberately miscalibrated. Under five stylised signal architectures, the
component predicted to be sensitive to each was the best-performing component in
$\nArchCellsIn$ of $\nArchCellsTot$ architecture-by-size combinations. Applied to a maize
grain-nitrogen GWAS, the Westfall--Young ordering recovered L-homoserine
biosynthesis as the leading pathway and placed a known aspartate kinase isozyme
first among 34,873 genes. Applied to GLGC LDL- and HDL-cholesterol GWAS, it
exposed a regime in which the self-contained null is false almost everywhere:
11.2\% of genes reach genome-wide significance for LDL-C and essentially every
pathway is significant, so significance no longer discriminates. The ordering
still does: plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling and clearance ranked first
of $\nLipNpw$ Reactome pathways for LDL-C and third for HDL-C, and the combined
gene score ranked \textit{LDLR} and \textit{ABCA1} first and recovered
established lipid genes better than ranking by GWAS association alone. In a
sorghum bioenergy panel of 330 lines, where gene--gene correlation is far
denser than in the human data, the correlated null reduced the excess
rejection of the independence-based omnibus, no pathway was declared
significant, and the pathway layer raised all three pre-specified lignin genes
in the gene ranking. We argue
that the saturation is a property of self-contained testing at very large $n$,
not a failure of multiple-testing correction.\\
\textbf{Availability:} CATFISH is an R package available at
\url{https://github.com/nirwan1265/CATFISH} under the MIT licence. All
simulation, analysis and figure code for this paper is in the
\texttt{catfish2/} directory of that repository.\\
\textbf{Contact:} \href{mailto:ntanduk@ncsu.edu}{ntanduk@ncsu.edu}\\
\textbf{Supplementary information:} Supplementary data are available at
\textit{Bioinformatics} online.}

\boxedtext{Key Messages}{
\begin{itemize}
\item Pathway $p$-values are correlated because pathways share genes;
Bonferroni and Benjamini--Hochberg corrections applied to them are therefore
miscalibrated, and the direction of the error depends on the overlap structure
of the particular pathway database used.
\item A Westfall--Young max-statistic null, built by propagating one
genome-wide correlated draw through the whole pathway family, controls the
family-wise error rate in the strong sense without assuming the pathways are
independent; its validity rests instead on the multivariate normal correlation
model used to generate the draw.
\item Implementing the omnibus in log space is a correctness requirement, not an
optimisation: at GWAS sample sizes above roughly one million the analytic
omnibus underflows double precision and the pathway ranking collapses into ties.
\item Self-contained pathway tests saturate at very large $n$. When 11\% of all
genes are genome-wide significant, a random 50-gene set contains several of
them, almost every pathway rejects, and exact FWER control does not restore
discrimination. Saturation is a property of the hypothesis being tested, not of
the correction applied to it.
\end{itemize}}

\section{1 Introduction}

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have transformed complex trait research
by identifying thousands of loci linked to disease, development, and
quantitative phenotypes. Yet most variants explain only a small fraction of
phenotypic variance and often fall in noncoding regions, limiting single-marker
mechanistic interpretation \citep{Manolio2009,Boyle2017}. Complex traits are
often highly polygenic, with relevant signals distributed across many
modest-effect loci rather than concentrated in a few genome-wide significant
variants \citep{Akula2025,Lappalainen2024}. Gene-set analysis, including
pathway-based approaches, is therefore a key post-GWAS strategy for translating
summary statistics into biological hypotheses. By aggregating evidence across
predefined groups of functionally related genes, pathway-based methods enhance
interpretability, reduce the multiple-testing burden, and reveal coordinated
pathway disruptions that single-locus analyses may miss
\citep{Mooney2015,deLeeuw2015}. This is especially valuable for polygenic
traits, where many genes may influence the same phenotype through shared
biological processes. In such settings, a candidate gene becomes more compelling
when locus- or gene-level association evidence is reinforced by pathway-level
enrichment from a biologically relevant process. Pathway support therefore
provides functional context for prioritization, although it is best interpreted
as complementary rather than definitive evidence because pathway annotations
remain incomplete and often overlapping.

A plethora of pathway analysis tools has been developed for GWAS. Classical
over-representation methods test whether predefined pathways are enriched among
significant genes, as implemented in DAVID, g:Profiler, and WebGestalt
\citep{Sherman2022,Kolberg2023,Liao2019}. Ranking-based methods such as Gene Set
Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) assess whether pathway genes are non-randomly
concentrated at the top or bottom of a ranked gene list
\citep{Subramanian2005}. GWAS-focused extensions, including MAGENTA, INRICH, and
MAGMA, add features such as SNP-to-gene mapping, pathway-level modeling, and
adjustment for linkage disequilibrium (LD) or gene size
\citep{Segre2010,Lee2012,deLeeuw2015}. Accounting for LD is essential in GWAS
pathway analysis because nearby variants are often correlated and therefore do
not provide independent evidence of association. If LD is ignored, clusters of
linked SNPs can be effectively counted multiple times, leading to inflated
gene-level aggregation and enrichment biases toward larger genes or regions with
dense marker coverage. Several widely used post-GWAS methods therefore
explicitly account for LD through model-based or reference-based dependence
estimates rather than assuming independence. VEGAS uses multivariate normal
simulation, Pascal uses LD from a reference population to compute gene and
pathway scores, and MAGMA models gene-level dependence through an approximated
correlation structure
\citep{Liu2010,deLeeuw2015,Lamparter2016,Watanabe2017}.

These methods address dependence \emph{within} a pathway. A second source of
dependence operates \emph{between} pathways and is far less often handled.
Curated pathway databases are built from overlapping annotations: a gene that
participates in several metabolic steps is a member of several pathways, and
hierarchical resources such as Reactome place parent and child pathways in the
same family. The pathway-level $p$-values returned by any method are therefore
correlated with one another, sometimes strongly. Nonetheless, the final
multiple-testing step in essentially every published pipeline is a Bonferroni or
Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment of those $p$-values, both of which are derived
under independence or under positive-regression dependence conditions that
overlapping gene sets do not generally satisfy. The resulting family-wise error
rate is not the nominal one, and the size of the discrepancy is a property of
the particular database rather than of the method, which makes it invisible to
the kind of simulation study that generates independent pathways.

The classical solution to exactly this problem is the Westfall--Young
max-statistic procedure \citep{Westfall1993}. Rather than correcting each
$p$-value in isolation, one generates a null realization of the \emph{entire}
family of tests, records the single most extreme statistic observed in that
realization, and repeats. The distribution of that maximum is the reference
distribution for the observed minimum, and the resulting threshold controls the
family-wise error rate under the complete null whatever the dependence structure
among the tests, because the dependence is inherited from the way the null
realizations are generated rather than assumed. In a GWAS pathway setting this means drawing
correlated gene-level null statistics genome-wide, propagating that single draw
through every pathway in the family, and recording the minimum pathway
$p$-value. Nothing about the between-pathway correlation needs to be estimated,
modelled, or even named.

Here we extend CATFISH (Combining \textbf{C}auchy combination test (ACAT),
\textbf{A}daptive \textbf{T}Fisher (soft) test, \textbf{F}isher's test,
m\textbf{I}n-P, and \textbf{S}touffer's method for \textbf{H}olistic pathway
analysis), a correlation-aware pathway analysis framework that integrates
multiple complementary gene-set statistics in a unified omnibus strategy, with
Westfall--Young family-wise calibration. Different pathway tests are sensitive
to different genetic architectures: some are best at detecting broad,
distributed enrichment, whereas others are more sensitive to sparse strong
signals or heterogeneous evidence. Instead of relying on a single summary
statistic, CATFISH evaluates each pathway with several complementary tests and
combines them in an omnibus framework. This improves robustness when the true
signal configuration is unknown while preserving the interpretability of the
component tests. Beyond pathway-level inference, CATFISH prioritizes candidate
genes by integrating locus-level GWAS signals, gene-level MAGMA results, and
pathway-level support.

This paper makes three contributions beyond the original CATFISH framework.
First, we add the Westfall--Young layer described above and characterize how it
behaves: the same correlated null, read down its columns, supplies each
pathway's marginal $p$-value, and read across its rows, the family-wise one. Second, we show that the entire statistic must be computed in log
space. This sounds like an implementation detail and is not: at the sample sizes
now routine in human GWAS consortia, the analytic omnibus $p$-value for a
typical pathway falls below the smallest representable double-precision number,
every such pathway is assigned the same floor value, and the ranking that the
whole analysis exists to produce is destroyed without any error being raised.
Third, we report a result we believe is general. In GLGC LDL- and
HDL-cholesterol GWAS of roughly 1.3 million individuals, the self-contained null
hypothesis is false for almost every gene set that could be constructed and
nearly every pathway is significant, so family-wise significance no longer
discriminates between pathways. We interpret this as saturation of the
self-contained hypothesis rather than as a defect of the correction. The
ordering of pathways, read against their size, still recovers lipoprotein
metabolism in both traits, and the gene-level prioritization recovers the
established genes of each trait better than GWAS association alone. We discuss
what saturation implies for the choice between self-contained and competitive
testing as GWAS sample sizes continue to grow.

CATFISH is implemented as an R package and is freely available at
\url{https://github.com/nirwan1265/CATFISH} under the MIT licence.

\subsection*{1.1 Pathway signal archetypes}

A pathway can be associated with a trait in very different ways. Its evidence
may come from one extreme gene, from a few strong genes, from many genes of
moderate effect, or from a faint shift shared by nearly all of its members, and
the component tests of CATFISH are sensitive to different parts of that range.
We formalise the range as five pathway signal archetypes: Strong Sparse Signal
(SSS), Single-Gene Proxy (SGP), Moderate Dense Signal (MDS), Weak Diffuse
Enrichment (WDE) and Mixed Signal Architecture (MSA). They are statistical
templates for interpreting test behaviour, not empirically learned classes of
real pathways, and they are the reason CATFISH combines complementary components
instead of choosing one in advance. Section~3.1 defines the archetypes, relates
each to a biological setting in which it is expected, and tests the predicted
correspondence between archetype and component under the family-wise decision
rule used throughout this paper. Supplementary Note~S1 gives the expanded
descriptions and the simulation design.

\section{2 Methods}

\subsection*{2.1 Gene-level inputs and component tests}

For a pathway $S$ containing $m=|S|$ genes, let $p_g$ denote the gene-level
association $p$-value for gene $g\in S$ and $Z_g$ the corresponding gene-level $Z$-statistic. We write $\mathbf{p}_S=(p_1,\dots,p_m)$ and $\mathbf{Z}_S=(Z_1,\dots,Z_m)$. Gene-level statistics are obtained with MAGMA \citep{deLeeuw2015}, which aggregates SNP-level GWAS evidence across variants assigned to a gene while accounting for LD, gene size, and local marker density, and which additionally returns the gene--gene correlation matrix induced by LD. These quantities are the common inputs for every CATFISH component test and for both calibration layers.

Each pathway is assessed using five complementary component tests (Table~\ref{tab:components}): ACAT \citep{Liu2019ACATV,Liu2020ACAT}, Fisher \citep{Fisher1932}, adaptive soft-thresholding TFisher \citep{Zhang2020TFisher}, minP, and Stouffer \citep{Stouffer1949}. Together, they cover the signal archetypes of Section~3.1 and apply directly to gene-level $p$-values or $Z$-scores without external weights or pathway-specific tuning. We combine the component $p$-values into a single omnibus statistic via an ACAT-type cross-method operator $\mathcal{O}(\cdot)$. This omnibus is a pathway-level summary of evidence across components, not an additional gene-level test.

Four components (ACAT, Fisher, minP, Stouffer) are fully determined by gene-level $p$-values and require no tuning. Soft TFisher is the exception: it adapts over a grid of truncation thresholds $\tau$, which determines which genes contribute. This is the only tunable choice in CATFISH, and we justify it below.

Following \citet{Zhang2020TFisher}, the grid spans small, intermediate and large thresholds rather than concentrating at one end: we use \(\tau\in\{10^{-2},\,5\times10^{-2},\,5\times10^{-1}\}\) and take the minimum calibrated TFisher \(p\)-value over the grid. The original authors' grid also includes \(\tau=1\), which we omit because \(\tau=1\) is Fisher's method and Fisher is already carried as a separate component. Spanning the range matters because the number of genes surviving truncation is \(\mathrm{Binomial}(m,\tau)\): a grid confined to small \(\tau\) leaves \(m\tau\ll1\) at realistic set sizes, the statistic is then zero on a large fraction of pathways, and the component is degenerate precisely where it is most often applied.

A threshold is informative mainly when \(m\tau\) is not far below one, so which \(\tau\) values are active depends on set size: \(\tau=5\times10^{-1}\) is informative from the smallest sets upward, whereas \(\tau=10^{-2}\) requires \(m\approx100\). Adapting across \(\tau\) rather than fixing a single value is what allows one component to serve both sparse and diffuse architectures, and users can assess the grid for their own database using \(m\tau\).

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Undefined components.} When no gene in a pathway falls below any threshold in the grid, the soft TFisher statistic is zero at every \(\tau\) and its one-sided \(p\)-value is exactly one. We treat this as \emph{undefined} rather than as evidence against enrichment, and the omnibus for that pathway is formed from the components that are defined. The distinction is not cosmetic. The ACAT operator maps each component \(p\)-value through \(\tan\{(1/2-p)\pi\}\), so a value at the upper boundary contributes a large negative term; a component that could not be computed would otherwise vote against the pathway and bias the omnibus toward the null. The rule is applied per pathway rather than by set size, because a large pathway in which no gene is individually strong is equally uncomputable. It does condition the component on having at least one gene below a threshold, so where TFisher does contribute its \(p\)-values are stochastically small; because the resampling null is generated under the identical rule, that conditioning appears in the null distribution and is absorbed by the calibration. An analytic omnibus \(p\)-value under this rule would be anti-conservative, whereas the resampled one is not.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Precision limit.} The soft TFisher implementation returns the null distribution function of the soft-thresholding statistic, so the one-sided \(p\)-value is formed as one minus that value. In double precision the difference is exactly zero once the distribution function exceeds \(1-10^{-16}\), and a strongly enriched pathway would then report \(p=0\). We cap the soft TFisher \(p\)-value at \(10^{-16}\), the smallest value the computation can resolve, rather than at the general lower floor applied to components whose tails are computed in closed form. A cap below the precision limit asserts resolution the statistic does not have; Section~4.6 shows what that does to the family-wise threshold.

\subsection*{2.2 Two sources of dependence}

CATFISH statistics are correlated at two levels. The two arise from different
causes, and both have to be addressed before any pathway $p$-value means what
it claims to.

\emph{Within a pathway}, component tests are dependent for two reasons: each is a deterministic function of the same $\mathbf{p}_S$, inducing cross-method dependence even if genes were independent; and gene-level evidence is correlated via LD, overlapping SNP-to-gene assignments, and shared local genomic architecture. Thus, any analytic reference distribution assuming independence is wrong for every component and typically anti-conservative, because correlated genes contain less information than the same number of independent genes.

\emph{Between pathways}, pathway-level $p$-values are correlated because databases assign genes to multiple sets. A gene shared across pathways propagates its (possibly extreme) null draw to all of them, shifting their evidence in the same direction. The strength and pattern of this dependence depend on database structure: hierarchies with nested sets create widespread but weak dependence, whereas largely disjoint curated collections create rare but strong dependence among overlapping sets. Consequently, independence-based multiple-testing corrections (Bonferroni or Benjamini--Hochberg under its positive-dependence conditions) need not achieve nominal error rates; the discrepancy is driven by the database, not the method.

These are often treated as separate problems, but in CATFISH both reduce to the same object: a vector of correlated gene-level statistics. A single genome-wide null draw, carrying the estimated LD, is scored through all pathways at once. Within a pathway, it provides null components and accounts for within-pathway dependence; across pathways, it provides a null for the family minimum and accounts for between-pathway dependence. No correlation structure is estimated or modeled; both are inherited. Section~2.3 develops this.

The construction relies on three assumptions: (i) \emph{Approximate normality}: under the null, the pathway-level vector satisfies $\mathbf{Z}_S\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R_S)$. (ii) \emph{Correlation-matrix adequacy}: correlation matrices from MAGMA-reported gene pairs provide an LD-informed working approximation to the unobserved gene-level covariance; unreported pairs are set to zero, and adequacy is evaluated via calibration and robustness analyses below. (iii) \emph{No residual structural confounding}: gene-level inputs are adjusted so pathway signals are not primarily driven by gene size or SNP density.

\subsection*{2.3 Calibration and family-wise control from a shared correlated null}

CATFISH obtains two different $p$-values for every pathway: a marginal one,
which asks whether that pathway is enriched, and a family-wise one, which asks whether evidence this strong would appear anywhere among the $K$ pathways tested by chance. Both are read from a single set of resampled null draws. The procedure has four steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: generate the null.}
Gene--gene correlations are read from MAGMA's \texttt{.genes.raw} output as a list of gene pairs and their correlations. For each chromosome $c$ containing $m_c$ genes we form an $m_c\times m_c$ correlation matrix $R_c$ by starting from the identity and filling in the reported pairs,
\[
R_c = I_{m_c} + \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}_c}
      r_{ij}\left(e_ie_j^{\top}+e_je_i^{\top}\right),
\]
where $\mathcal{P}_c$ is the set of reported pairs for chromosome $c$; pairs not in $\mathcal{P}_c$ remain zero. We compute correlations only for gene pairs close enough to share LD, so $R_c$ is banded by construction: zeros for distant genes are approximations, not imputations, whereas zeros for nearby but unreported pairs introduce approximation error (see Section~5). Genes on different chromosomes are treated as independent, which is structurally correct. If $R_c$ is not positive definite, we floor eigenvalues at $10^{-6}$, reconstruct, and renormalise the diagonal; the implementation reports affected chromosomes.

Writing $R_c=U_c^{\top}U_c$ for the Cholesky factor, replicate $b$ draws
\[
\mathbf{Z}^{(b)}_c = U_c^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\qquad
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m_c}),
\]
independently per chromosome, and the concatenation $\mathbf{Z}^{(b)}$ is one genome-wide null realisation carrying the estimated LD structure. Gene-level null $p$-values follow by the Gaussian copula $p_g^{(b)}=1-\Phi(Z_g^{(b)})$, matching MAGMA's one-sided sign convention. This is repeated for $b=1,\dots,B$.

We draw genome-wide, not pathway-by-pathway, because Step~4 requires all $K$ pathways to be scored on the \emph{same} realisation so shared genes perturb them jointly. Drawing each pathway from its own correlation submatrix would be marginally correct but would remove the dependence structure Step~4 needs.

\paragraph{Step 2: score every pathway on every replicate.}
For each replicate we recompute all five component statistics and the omnibus operator $\mathcal{O}(\cdot)$ for every pathway in the family $\mathcal{F}=\{S_1,\dots,S_K\}$, giving a $B\times K$ matrix of null omnibus values
\[
Q^{(b)}_k = \mathcal{O}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S_k)\right),
\qquad b=1,\dots,B,\quad k=1,\dots,K .
\]
Because all components are recomputed from the same draw, $Q$ preserves within-pathway gene dependence, cross-method dependence, and—since all $K$ pathways share the draw—between-pathway dependence. The observed values $q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{obs}}(S_k))$ are computed the same way from the real data. Applying $\mathcal{O}$ to the observed components without resampling yields the uncalibrated analytic omnibus $\mathrm{Omnibus}_{\mathrm{An}}(S_k)=q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k$, which assumes gene independence and serves as the baseline.

\paragraph{Step 3: the marginal \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-value.}
Compare each pathway's observed statistic with its own null values across the $B$ replicates. The LD-aware marginal $p$-value is the Monte Carlo tail probability
\[
\tilde p_k \;=\;
\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(Q^{(b)}_k\le q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k\right)}{B+1}.
\]
This quantity is computed from the genome-wide draw rather than from the pathway-specific submatrix $R_{S_k}$; because $R_{S_k}$ is simply the $R_c$ submatrix for that pathway's genes, both use the same correlation information, so nothing is lost. We call $\tilde p_k$ the per-pathway calibrated $p$-value and use it to rank pathways. It corresponds to what is often called MVN calibration; we avoid that label because the multivariate normal model is the generator of the null draws, not a separate calibration step, and the same draws serve Step~4.

\paragraph{Step 4: the family-wise \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-value.}
Within each replicate, record the smallest statistic across the whole family,
\[
M^{(b)} = \min_{1\le k\le K} Q^{(b)}_k ,
\]
and compare each observed statistic with the resulting distribution. This gives the Westfall--Young adjusted $p$-value \citep{Westfall1993}
\[
\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k \;=\;
\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(M^{(b)}\le q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k\right)}{B+1},
\]
Rejecting all pathways with $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k\le\alpha$ controls the family-wise error rate at level $\alpha$ under the complete null, whatever the dependence among pathways, because a false rejection anywhere in the family requires the family minimum to cross the threshold. Three further points apply. First, the control is in the strong sense, not only under the complete null. Westfall--Young procedures control the family-wise error rate strongly when the joint distribution of the statistics for the true null hypotheses does not depend on which other hypotheses are false---the condition of subset pivotality \citep{Westfall1993}. Two features of the construction give it. Each pathway statistic is a deterministic function of its own genes' $z$-scores only; and under the model of Step~1, with association represented as a shift in the means of the associated genes, the genes of a pathway containing no associated gene are jointly $\mathcal{N}(0,R)$, restricted to those genes, whatever happens elsewhere. The observed statistics of the null pathways therefore have exactly their complete-null joint distribution, and because the minimum over any subset of pathways is never smaller than the minimum over the whole family, the probability that any null pathway crosses the threshold is at most $\alpha$. The argument requires the soft TFisher truncation grid to be fixed in advance: a grid chosen from the empirical distribution of all gene-level $p$-values, as in data-adaptive implementations, would make every pathway's statistic depend on genes outside it. Second, the null is parametric: it is generated from the multivariate normal model of Step~1 rather than by permuting phenotypes, so the procedure is Westfall--Young in construction but inherits the adequacy of that model. Third, the counting form of the adjusted $p$-value above, in which null minima equal to the observed value count as at least as extreme, is required rather than a matter of taste. A threshold taken as a quantile of $M$ and applied with an inclusive comparison rejects every observation tied at that quantile, and if the omnibus loses resolution in the tail so that many null minima coincide, the empirical family-wise error rate can approach one (Section~4.6).

Between-pathway correlation is never explicitly estimated or modelled. Because all $K$ pathways are evaluated on the same draw in Step~2, genes shared across pathways perturb them jointly, as in the observed data, and this dependence is reflected in the distribution of $M$. The threshold $\hat t_\alpha=\mathrm{quantile}_\alpha(M)$ is the family-wise significance cutoff implied by the pathway database in use; its ratio to the Bonferroni cutoff $\alpha/K$ quantifies the database’s departure from independence at no extra cost, provided it is read on calibrated statistics; on the analytic scale the ratio also absorbs any miscalibration of the per-pathway statistic (Section~4.1).

Steps~3 and~4 use the same matrix $Q$ and require no additional resampling. Their outputs relate as a raw and adjusted $p$-value: $\tilde p_k$ assesses pathway $S_k$ alone, while $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k$ accounts for testing $K$ pathways. Both are reported; neither replaces the other.

\paragraph{Size standardisation.}
A minimum across tests is meaningful only when the tests share a scale. The raw omnibus does not: Fisher’s statistic increases with $m$, so the Step~4 family minimum would mechanically favor the largest sets. We therefore also report a variant that first standardizes each observed value within its own column of $Q$ as a $z$-score, $z_k=(q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k-\mu_k)/\sigma_k$, where $\mu_k$ and $\sigma_k$ are that column’s mean and standard deviation, and then takes the row minimum. This rescales values for the minimum operation; it is not a pathway-size bias correction, and Section~4.1 shows it does not behave as one. In the simulations of Section~2.6 every pathway in a configuration has the same size and dependence structure, so the pathways are exchangeable, share one marginal null, and the row minimum is taken on the raw scale; standardisation is needed only when pathway sizes differ, as in real data. We standardise by location and scale rather than by each pathway's empirical null distribution function. The latter would put pathways on an exactly common scale, but would resolve each to only $1/(B+1)$, which is coarser than the family minimum requires and would reintroduce ties.

\subsection*{2.4 Numerical implementation in log space}

The omnibus statistic must be computed entirely in log space. This is a correctness requirement, not an optimisation, and it prevents silent failure.

The smallest positive normalized double is $\approx2.2\times10^{-308}$. In a GWAS with $n\approx1.3$ million, gene-level $p<10^{-300}$ is common: in the GLGC LDL-cholesterol data (Section~3.9), the published $p$-value is exactly zero for 473 chromosome~1 variants, including the \textit{SORT1} lead rs12740374 at $-\log_{10}p=1725$. Combining even a few such genes with Fisher’s method yields a pathway statistic whose double-precision $p$-value underflows to zero. All affected pathways then share the same floor value, ties are broken by storage order, and the ranking the analysis aims to produce is silently corrupted. In our first implementation, the top-ranked pathways were (on inspection) alphabetical.

Five changes are needed. (i) Compute all component statistics on $\ell_g=\log p_g$ and return log $p$-values via \texttt{stats::pchisq(\dots,\,log.p=TRUE)} and \texttt{stats::qnorm(\dots,\,log.p=TRUE)}. (ii) Evaluate the ACAT sum using the small-$p$ asymptote: for $p<10^{-15}$, use $\tan\{\pi(\tfrac12-p)\}\approx 1/(p\pi)$, accumulating those terms as a log-sum-exp of $-\ell_g-\log\pi$; use the trigonometric form for the rest. (iii) Replace $p=\tfrac12-\arctan(T)/\pi$ with $p\approx1/(T\pi)$ for $T>10^{6}$, where cancellation near $\tfrac12$ dominates truncation error. (iv) Since ACAT can be negative when most inputs exceed $0.5$, handle that case in a separate branch computing $\tfrac12-\arctan(T)/\pi$ directly; clamping would set $p=0.5$ and destroy ordering in the upper half. (v) The minP inversion $1-(1-q)^m$ underflows for $q\lesssim10^{-16}/m$, causing minP to drop out; use the $m q$ asymptote whenever $\log m+\ell_{\min}<\log 10^{-8}$.

These changes let the pipeline represent pathway $p$-values down to $e^{-745}\approx10^{-324}$ and, crucially, preserve their \emph{ordering} well below where probability-scale computations fail. They are implemented in the scripts used for the real-data analyses. The simulations of Section~2.6 instead call the component functions of the CATFISH package directly, so that they validate the code a user runs; those functions work on the probability scale with a lower floor of $10^{-300}$, which is adequate there because no simulated null approaches it. Soft TFisher is subject to its own, much higher, precision limit (Section~2.1).

\subsection*{2.5 Candidate-gene prioritization}

The output a biologist acts on is a short list of genes, so CATFISH ranks genes by a composite score over three evidence layers:
\[
\mathrm{Score}(g)=S_{\mathrm{GWAS}}(g)+S_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}(g)
+0.5\,S_{\mathrm{PATH}}(g)+0.3\,B_{\mathrm{hub}}(g).
\]
Each layer enters by rank rather than by its raw statistic,
$S_\bullet(g)=-\log_{10}\!\left(r_\bullet(g)/N_\bullet\right)$, where
$r_{\mathrm{GWAS}}$ is the gene's rank by minimum SNP-level $p$-value,
$r_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}$ its rank by gene-level $p$-value, and $r_{\mathrm{PATH}}$ the rank of its best pathway by the size-standardised Westfall--Young statistic $z_k$ of Section~2.3; rank one is the strongest evidence. We rank pathways on $z_k$ for two reasons. It is the statistic the family-wise test is computed on, so the gene score uses the same pathway ordering that is reported; and it measures each pathway against its own correlated null, whereas the analytic omnibus assumes independent genes and overstates pathways whose genes are in LD (Section~3.4). The family-wise $p$-value gives the same ordering but is floored at $1/(B+1)$, which ties the strongest pathways. The hub term $B_{\mathrm{hub}}(g)=\log_{10}(n_{\mathrm{path}}(g)+1)$ rewards membership in several pathways. Genes missing a layer score zero for it, so an absent layer neither helps nor penalises.

We use ranks instead of $-\log_{10}p$ to make layers with different tail behaviour comparable and to avoid the $p$-value floors in Section~2.4. Pathway evidence is halved because the pathway $p$-value is a set-level statistic shared across member genes; the hub term is intentionally secondary. We fixed the weights \emph{a priori} and assessed them afterwards over a grid, $w_{\mathrm{path}}\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ and $w_{\mathrm{hub}}\in\{0,0.1,\dots,0.5\}$, in a truth-planted simulation and in the real analyses of Section~3.9 (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:weights}). The simulation draws $400$ replicate datasets of $500$ genes and $50$ pathways, plants causal genes concentrated in five pathways, and generates correlated GWAS-, MAGMA- and pathway-level evidence for them \citep{Tandukar2025Thesis}. Rankings were stable across the grid: the Spearman correlation of each full ranking with the default ranking was at least $0.986$, and the top twenty genes overlapped the default top twenty by at least $88\%$. Recovery of planted causal genes in the top twenty rose steadily with the pathway weight, from $0.71$ to $0.85$, and was $0.80$ at the defaults, so the defaults are not the optimum of that simulation. They are a deliberately conservative middle value: the pathway layer is set-level evidence shared by every member gene and, in curated databases, partly encodes prior knowledge, so it should not outweigh direct genetic evidence. We did not re-tune the weights on the validation genes of Section~3.9.

The score is a heuristic ranking metric, not an inferential model: it has no null distribution or $p$-value. We also report per-layer ranks, because the GWAS and MAGMA layers use the same variants and are not independent evidence.

\subsection*{2.6 Simulation design}

Simulations are run on the omnibus statistic under its null, except where a signal is injected deliberately (item~6). Two dependence designs are used. In the \emph{shared-universe} design, pathways are overlapping subsets of one gene universe: $G=3000$ genes in independent blocks of 50 with exchangeable within-block correlation $\rho$, standing in for chromosomes and LD blocks, from the first 600 of which we draw $K=150$ pathways of $m$ genes each at random. Each gene therefore belongs to several pathways, a pathway spans several blocks, and a single genome-wide draw moves every pathway containing a given gene---the between-pathway dependence the family-wise procedure is designed to control. In the \emph{exchangeable} design, used only in item~1, each pathway is drawn independently and all $m$ of its genes are mutually correlated at $\rho$. That design has no between-pathway dependence; it isolates within-pathway correlation at an intensity well beyond what block-structured LD produces, and serves as a stress test. Each study reports two summaries of the null matrix: per-pathway marginal type~I error (by columns) and family-wise error rate (by rows), with Bonferroni as the family-wise comparator. Thresholds are fitted on one set of draws and evaluated on a held-out set, so rates are not correct by construction. Component statistics are computed with the CATFISH package functions, using the soft TFisher grid, undefined-component rule and precision cap of Section~2.1. We do not simulate genotypes or phenotypes or run a full GWAS; that is future work (Section~5) and is not needed for the questions here. Each study corresponds to a script in \texttt{catfish2/scripts/} and writes a CSV to \texttt{catfish2/results/} from which the tables and figures are generated.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Null calibration and type~I error} (\texttt{01\_null\_calibration.R}), exchangeable design. Under the global null we compare four decision rules: the analytic independence-based $p$-value; per-pathway calibration against a null bank shared by all pathways of a given size; Bonferroni; and the Westfall--Young adjusted $p$-value of Step~4, with the null distribution of the family minimum estimated on one half of the simulated families and evaluated on the other. We vary $\rho\in\{0,0.3,0.6\}$ and $m\in\{5,20,50\}$ and report type~I error at $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.01\}$, family-wise error, the genomic-control inflation factor $\lambda=\mathrm{median}(\chi^2_1\text{-transformed }p)/0.4549$, and the number of distinct values taken by the family minimum, which detects loss of resolution directly.

\item \textbf{Component-breakage stress test} (\texttt{02\_breakage\_stress.R}).
We miscalibrate $k$ of five components by replacing each affected component’s $p$-value with $p^{\gamma}$, and test whether the omnibus and its calibration remain valid. We run both corruption directions: for $0<p<1$, $\gamma<1$ yields $p^{\gamma}>p$ (conservative) and $\gamma>1$ yields $p^{\gamma}<p$ (anti-conservative), the latter being most relevant for false positives. We use $\gamma\in\{0.7,1.4\}$; sweep $k=0,\dots,4$; evaluate up to three component combinations per $k$; and cross with the same LD regimes at $m\in\{20,50\}$. We apply corruption to both null replicates and observed statistics, allowing resampling calibration to absorb it.

\item \textbf{Leave-one-out component sensitivity} (\texttt{03\_adaptive\_loo.R}).
We remove each component in turn, recompute the null bank and the calibration from scratch, and measure the change in type~I error and $\lambda$. This isolates how much each component contributes to, or destabilizes, the calibrated omnibus, and identifies components whose removal would change inference.

\item \textbf{Per-component calibration} (\texttt{09\_component\_calibration.R}).
Rather than combining, we calibrate each of the five components against its own null and report analytic and calibrated type~I error, family-wise rate and $\lambda$ per component. This identifies which statistics LD actually breaks and whether a single calibrated component is usable in place of the omnibus when the signal architecture is known in advance.

\item \textbf{Robustness to an incomplete correlation file}
(\texttt{08\_missing\_correlation.R}). Data are simulated under the true correlation, but calibration (null bank, marginal $p$-values, and Westfall--Young threshold) uses a matrix where a fraction $\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ of off-diagonal entries is set to zero, mimicking unreported gene pairs. Evaluation draws are shared across missingness levels; only calibration varies. For real data, \texttt{10\_pair\_coverage.R} places a correlation file on this scale by reporting the fraction of within-pathway gene pairs on the same chromosome and close enough to be correlated for which MAGMA reports a value.

\item \textbf{Archetype power} (\texttt{17\_archetype\_power\_wy.R}), shared-universe design with $\rho=0.3$. One pathway in each family carries signal shaped as one of the five archetypes of Section~3.1 (Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}); the remaining $K-1$ are null. Signal is a mean shift on the gene-level $z$ scale applied to a fixed fraction of the pathway's genes, at positions permuted in every replicate: SSS shifts $15\%$ of genes by $3.0$; MDS $60\%$ by $1.1$; WDE $90\%$ by $0.55$; MSA $10\%$ by $1.8$ and a further $60\%$ by $1.1$; and SGP $5\%$ by $4.0$, with at least one gene in every layer. Shifts are multiplied by an effect scale over a grid running from undetectable to saturated, at $m\in\{5,20,50\}$. For each component and for the omnibus we record the probability that the signal pathway clears its own Westfall--Young threshold. Regret is the difference in that probability between the best single component and the omnibus in the same cell.
\end{enumerate}

Unless stated otherwise, each configuration uses $B$ null draws for the
calibration bank and a separate set of replicate datasets for evaluation, with
both values set at the top of each script so that a reduced smoke-test
configuration can be run with \texttt{bash run\_all.sh quick}.

\subsection*{2.7 Real-data analyses}

\textbf{Maize grain nitrogen.} Gene-level statistics were computed with MAGMA
from a mixed-linear-model (MLM) GWAS of grain nitrogen content, using a
$10\,\mathrm{kb}$ upstream and downstream window for SNP-to-gene assignment.
Pathways were taken from CornCyc \citep{Hawkins2021CornCyc,Caspi2008BioCyc},
giving $172$ pathways after size filtering. Gene--gene correlations were
recovered per chromosome from MAGMA's \texttt{.genes.raw} output; we verified
explicitly that all ten chromosomes were represented, after an earlier run in
which only chromosome~1 correlations had been parsed and $85\%$ of the genome
was silently treated as independent. Candidate-gene scoring used the three-layer
score of Section~2.5 over all $34{,}873$ annotated genes.

\textbf{Human LDL and HDL cholesterol.} We used the Global Lipids Genetics
Consortium LDL- and HDL-cholesterol summary statistics for the
European-ancestry meta-analysis ($n\approx1.3$ million)
\citep{Graham2021GLGC}. The two traits were processed identically, as
described below.

\emph{Variant filtering and the $p$-value floor.} Variants were restricted to
rsIDs present in the 1000 Genomes European reference panel used for LD. The
released \texttt{pvalue} column underflows to exactly zero at the strongest
loci, so all probabilities were reconstructed from \texttt{pvalue\_neg\_log10}.
For the MAGMA gene analysis, whose \texttt{--pval} input requires a
probability, these were floored at $10^{-300}$; MAGMA is unaffected because the
floor is far beyond any gene-level resolution it reports. The candidate-gene
GWAS layer is affected, because it ranks genes by their minimum variant
$p$-value and the floor collapses the strongest genes into a single tie. That
layer therefore reads an unfloored $-\log_{10}p$ file written by
\texttt{11\_munge\_neglog10.sh}; Section~3.9 reports what the floor costs when
it is not removed.

\emph{Gene analysis.} Variants were assigned to genes with a $10\,\mathrm{kb}$
upstream and downstream window using the NCBI37.3 gene locations. MAGMA gene
analysis was run per chromosome with \texttt{--batch} and combined with
\texttt{--merge}, which preserves the gene--gene correlations in the merged
\texttt{.genes.raw} file; running the chromosomes as independent jobs and
concatenating the outputs would discard them.

\emph{Pathways.} Pathway inference used the Reactome subset of MSigDB C2:CP
($1{,}390$ sets after size filtering), with HumanCyc additionally converted into
the CATFISH pathway format for comparison. We report Reactome because a
hierarchical database is where between-pathway dependence is strongest, which is
the regime the family-wise procedure is designed for.

\emph{Candidate genes.} The GWAS layer uses MAGMA's own
\texttt{.genes.annot} file rather than re-deriving a window, so that the GWAS
and gene layers are assigned from identical variant sets. The pathway layer is
ranked on the size-standardised statistic $z_k$ rather than on the
standardised Westfall--Young $p$-value. The two give the identical ordering,
since the $p$-value is a monotone transform of $z_k$, but the $p$-value is
floored at $1/(B+1)$ and the entire top of the list is tied there at the $B$
used here.

\emph{Positive controls.} Thirteen genes with established roles in LDL-C
biology---\textit{LDLR}, \textit{PCSK9}, \textit{APOB}, \textit{APOE},
\textit{SORT1}, \textit{HMGCR}, \textit{NPC1L1}, \textit{ABCG8}, \textit{LPA},
\textit{CETP}, \textit{LDLRAP1}, \textit{MYLIP} and \textit{ANGPTL3}---and
twelve with established roles in HDL-C biology---\textit{CETP},
\textit{LIPC}, \textit{LIPG}, \textit{ABCA1}, \textit{APOA1},
\textit{APOA5}, \textit{LCAT}, \textit{SCARB1}, \textit{PLTP},
\textit{LPL}, \textit{ANGPTL4} and \textit{CUBN}---were named before the
prioritization was run and are used to assess it. They are a
validation set, not a discovery; the score has no access to them.

\textbf{Sorghum bioenergy traits.} We analysed dry biomass yield (dry tons per
acre) and lignin content (\% of dry matter) in the sorghum Bioenergy
Association Panel (BAP) \citep{Brenton2016}: $330$ phenotyped lines, with
variant--gene LD taken from the panel's own genotypes ($363$ lines; variants
with minor allele frequency $\ge0.01$). Association was tested with the
univariate linear mixed model of GEMMA \citep{Zhou2012GEMMA}, giving
$\nSorSnps$ variants with a $p$-value after MAGMA's sample-size filter.

\emph{Gene analysis.} MAGMA v1.10 \citep{deLeeuw2015} was run separately for
each chromosome, with variants assigned to BTx623 v3 gene models using a
$25\,\mathrm{kb}$ upstream and downstream window. MAGMA computes gene--gene
correlations only between nearby genes on the same chromosome, so the ten
per-chromosome \texttt{.genes.raw} files together carry the complete
correlation structure. Of MAGMA's gene models we used the SNP-wise mean, the
model also used for the lipid traits; Section~3.10.1 shows why the SNP-wise
top and multi-model $p$-values were not used.

\emph{Pathways and calibration.} Pathways were taken from SorghumBicolorCyc
\citep{Hawkins2021CornCyc}, restricted to $5$--$200$ genes, giving
$\nSorDryNpw$ pathways. Westfall--Young calibration used the same settings as
the other analyses ($B=10{,}000$; same truncation grid and seed).

\emph{Candidate genes.} The GWAS layer reads the variant $p$-values that MAGMA
itself analysed and assigns them to genes by MAGMA's own gene windows, so that,
as for the lipid traits, the GWAS and gene layers see identical variant sets.

\emph{Positive controls.} Eleven cloned genes were named before the analysis
was run. For dry biomass, eight genes controlling flowering time and plant
height, the main determinants of biomass in this photoperiod-sensitive panel:
\textit{Ma1}/\textit{PRR37} \citep{Murphy2011PRR37}, \textit{Ma3}/\textit{PHYB}
\citep{Childs1997PHYB}, \textit{Ma5}/\textit{PHYC}, %% TODO: add the Ma5/PHYC citation
\textit{Ma6}/\textit{GHD7} \citep{Murphy2014Ghd7}, \textit{Ma2}
\citep{Casto2019Ma2}, \textit{Dw1} \citep{Hilley2016Dw1}, \textit{Dw2}
\citep{Hilley2017Dw2} and \textit{Dw3} \citep{Multani2003Dw3}. For lignin, the
three cloned brown-midrib genes \textit{bmr2}/4CL \citep{Saballos2012bmr2},
\textit{bmr6}/CAD \citep{Saballos2009bmr6} and \textit{bmr12}/COMT
\citep{Bout2003bmr12}. SorghumBicolorCyc describes metabolism, so none of the
flowering-time and height genes is in any tested pathway, while all three
\textit{bmr} genes are; the two sets therefore test the score with and without
a contribution from the pathway layer.

\emph{Phenotype-permutation null.} The simulations of Section~2.6 draw gene
$z$-scores from the same correlation model that generates the null, so they
test the procedure given its model. To test the model as well, dry biomass was
permuted among the $330$ phenotyped lines $100$ times. A permutation removes
any association while keeping the genotypes, their LD, the kinship among lines
and the distribution of the phenotype. Each permuted phenotype was analysed
with the whole pipeline of the real analysis: the GEMMA mixed model, MAGMA per
chromosome with the same windows and SNP-wise mean model, and Westfall--Young
calibration with the same pathways and settings. MAGMA's gene--gene
correlations depend only on the genotypes, so one correlation file served all
permutations. Every pathway is null in every permutation; the calibrated
$p$-values should therefore be uniform, and a permutation with any pathway at a
family-wise $p$-value of $0.05$ or less should occur in at most $5\%$ of
permutations.

\textbf{Gene-level genomic control.} In all real analyses (the ten lipid
trait--ancestry sets and both sorghum traits), gene $p$-values were centred
before the pathway test. Each gene $p$-value was converted to a $z$-score,
$z_g=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_g)$, computed from $\log p_g$ so that values below
$10^{-300}$ keep their order (Section~2.4); the median over all genes of the
GWAS, $\hat\delta$, was subtracted; and the centred score was converted back,
$p^{\mathrm{GC}}_g=1-\Phi(z_g-\hat\delta)$. This is the location form of
genomic control \citep{Devlin1999}: one constant per GWAS, which leaves the
order of genes, the gene--gene correlations and the null draws of Section~2.3
unchanged and moves the median gene $p$-value to $0.5$. We added it because
the permutation null showed that each GWAS carries its own genome-wide shift in
gene $z$-scores, which a self-contained test reads as pathway signal and which
the correlated null does not contain (Section~3.8). In a GWAS without such a
shift, centring changes nothing. In a saturated GWAS the median gene is itself
associated, and centring removes part of the real polygenic signal: the test
then asks whether a pathway's genes are more associated than the median gene of
the genome, which moves it towards the competitive question (Section~4.2). The
shift $\hat\delta$ was $0.15$--$0.33$ in the eight non-European lipid sets,
$1.38$ (LDL-C) and $1.77$ (HDL-C) in the European ones, and $0.13$ for both
sorghum traits.

\section{3 Results}

\subsection*{3.1 Five pathway signal archetypes, and their component tests}

Consider a pathway-level working null and alternative hypotheses. Under the null, the gene-level evidence within a pathway matches the background distribution after accounting for dependence, with no systematic excess of small $p$-values. Under the alternative, the pathway carries excess evidence, However, that evidence can be distributed among its genes in many ways. We define five archetypes based on specific profiles (Figure~\ref{fig:archetypes}; Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}). These are templates for how pathways might behave. However, real pathways may fall between archetypes or combine features, and the archetype is not known in advance. Where each archetype is expected, Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary} lists each profile shape and its predicted component. We describe the biological contexts where each is expected, with details in Supplementary Note~S1.

SSS is expected when pathway output is constrained by a bottleneck such as a committed-step enzyme, rate-limiting regulator, or essential transporter \citep{MorenoSanchez2008,Naz2023,Tanner2018,Song2020}. Aspartokinase catalyzes the entry step of the aspartate-derived pathway, producing lysine, threonine, methionine, and isoleucine \citep{Galili2011,Hasenoehrl2019}, so the signal can be strong and localized there while downstream genes are near null. SGP is the limiting case, where a pathway is effectively one gene, typically in small or highly overlapping annotations. \textit{PAH} is the rate-limiting enzyme converting phenylalanine to tyrosine and the main cause of phenylketonuria \citep{Flydal2013PAH,Elhawary2022PKU}, so any signal in a broader phenylalanine-metabolism set would largely reflect it. Such pathways add little beyond gene-level association and should be interpreted as gene-centric.

MDS is expected when pathway output depends on many interacting components rather than a single effector, as in TNF$\alpha$/IL-1$\beta$ cytokine signaling to NF-$\kappa$B via receptor engagement, adaptor recruitment, kinase cascades, feedback, and transcriptional control \citep{Jiang2021CytoSig,Megha2021Inflammation,Verstrepen2008NFkB,vanLoo2011NFkB,Cargnello2011MAPK}. WDE reflects a highly polygenic architecture. The human height is the canonical example \citep{Guo2018HeightGWAS,Yengo2022HeightSaturation}, where coherent processes (e.g., TGF-$\beta$, Hedgehog, growth-plate extracellular matrix) may be detectable only in aggregate \citep{LangoAllen2010Height}. MSA is expected when a few pathway-defining genes with large effects sit within a broader network of auxiliary components. LDL-cholesterol metabolism (Section~3.9, explained as a biological example) illustrates this. Rare high-effect variants in \textit{LDLR}, \textit{APOB}, and \textit{PCSK9} cause familial hypercholesterolaemia, while common variants in \textit{ABCA1}, \textit{LIPC}, \textit{CETP}, \textit{PLTP}, \textit{ANGPTL3}, and \textit{SORT1} shape population LDL-C \citep{Chasman2009LipoproteinProfiles,Peloso2022WGSbloodlipids,Umlai2025LDLC}.

% The five components interpret the ranked profile differently (Table~\ref{tab:components}). minP uses only the top gene. ACAT averages Cauchy-transformed $p$-values; its heavy tail makes the smallest few dominate, so it is head-driven like minP but also rewards a second or third strong gene. Fisher sums $-2\log p_g$ across genes; because contributions grow only logarithmically, it favors many moderately small $p$-values over a few extreme ones. Stouffer averages $Z_g$ with equal weights; since $Z_g$ grows even more slowly, small shared shifts accumulate with pathway size, while a single extreme gene is diluted by $1/\sqrt{m}$. Soft TFisher drops genes with $p>\tau$ and sums the remaining excess evidence, shifting from minP-like at small $\tau$ toward Fisher as $\tau\to1$; adapting over $\tau$ lets it track the profile up to its shoulder. These properties predict which components are sensitive to each archetype (Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}, fourth column).


\subsection*{3.2 Each component detects the archetype predicted for it}

We next ran a simulation to check whether each component test detects the archetype predicted for it. The archetype--component mapping in Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary} is a prediction testable only by simulation, since real pathway archetypes are unknown. We embedded one pathway per archetype, carrying its ranked signal shape, in $149$ null pathways from the same correlated gene set, scaled effects from undetectable to saturated, and recorded how often it exceeded the family-wise Westfall--Young threshold (Section~2.6, item~6).

The predictions are well supported. In $\nArchCellsIn$ of $\nArchCellsTot$ archetype-by-size cells, the component most often attaining the highest family-wise power lay in the predicted set (Table~\ref{tab:archverify}, Figure~\ref{fig:archpower}). The pattern is clear and directional. The diffuse enrichment favors Stouffer, with extreme-tail tests near null. The sparse or single-gene signals favor minP and ACAT, with Fisher and Stouffer nearly powerless. Finally, dense or mixed architectures favor Fisher, with soft TFisher close behind. No component dominates across settings.

The lower placings are also informative. Across the $\nArchInfCells$ informative cells, Fisher placed first $\nFisherFirst$ times but fourth or worse in $\nFisherFourthPlus$. The minP finished last $\nMinPLast$ times. Soft TFisher won only $\nTFFirst$ cell but never placed below third. It is the only component never among the weakest, and it supports Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}. Soft TFisher is sensitive to dense and mixed signals but is not the top test for any archetype. Thus, CATFISH reports every component separately, its archetypes help interpret real pathways.

\subsection*{3.3 Combining the components into the CATFISH omnibus}

Because no single component dominates and the true pathway archetype is unknown, users must either pick a component and risk mis-specification or combine them. CATFISH combines all five component $p$-values using an ACAT-type cross-method operator to produce one omnibus statistic per pathway.

This statistic lacks a usable analytic null. The components are deterministic functions of the same gene-level $p$-values, and those $p$-values are correlated via LD. Thus, independence-based reference distributions are incorrect and often anti-conservative. CATFISH therefore calibrates by resampling. It generates genome-wide null draws from the gene--gene correlation matrix (MAGMA output, preserving estimated LD), scores all pathways at once, and repeats this $B$ times, giving $B$ null values for each of the $K$ pathways, i.e., one row per replicate and one column per pathway.

These values address different questions. Fix a pathway and use its $B$ null scores to form its null distribution; the fraction at least as extreme as the observed statistic is the calibrated marginal $p$-value. For a fixed replicate, the minimum across all $K$ pathways is recorded, and these per-replicate minima constitute the null distribution of the family minimum. The fraction at least as extreme as a pathway’s observed statistic is its Westfall--Young FWER-adjusted $p$-value (the Section~3.2 threshold). Because all pathways are scored on the same draw, shared genes perturb them jointly as in the observed data, so between-pathway dependence is inherited, not modeled.

Figure~\ref{fig:archpower} shows the cost of combining across Section~3.2 cells. The omnibus usually matches the best component, falling behind mainly for single-gene signals. The practical issue is how much is lost when the best component is unknown. We quantify this as \emph{regret}, the gap between the best component and the omnibus within a cell. Mean regret ranges from $\nArchRegretBest$ for $\nArchRegretBestA$ to $\nArchRegretWorst$ for $\nArchRegretWorstA$ (Table~\ref{tab:archregret}), while the best--worst component gap in the same cells is often $>0.5$ in power. Pre-committing to one component risks the full gap, while the omnibus risks only the regret.

An exception is the single-gene case, where the omnibus can lose up to $\nArchMaxRegret$ in one cell. Here, minP and ACAT detect the pathway, Fisher and Stouffer are near null, and averaging dilutes a signal present in only two components. Thus, this reflects the combination method, not any component, and suggests using a gene-level rather than pathway-level test.


\subsection*{3.4 Independence-based calibration is anti-conservative; calibration against the correlated null is not}

Here, we examine the type I error. With no signal, how often does each statistic reject? We adopt the exchangeable setup in Section~2.6, item~1 to separate dependence. The pathways are mutually independent, whereas the $m$ genes within a pathway share a common correlation $\rho$ with higher than standard block LD and therefore serving as a stress test. We vary $\rho\in\{0,0.3,0.6\}$ and $m\in\{5,20,50\}$, simulate $500$ families per setting, and compare four rules: the analytic independence-based $p$-value; per-pathway calibration under the correlated null; Bonferroni on the analytic $p$-value; and the Westfall--Young adjusted $p$-value (Section~2.3, Step~4). The first two are per-pathway (evaluated by marginal type I error), while the last two are family-wise (evaluated by the probability any pathway is rejected) (Table~\ref{tab:null_calibration}; Figure~\ref{fig:type1}).

The analytic $p$-value is valid only under within-pathway independence, and the grid shows the impact of violating this. At $\rho=0$, $m=5$, the analytic type~I error is $\nTAnInd$ (nominal $0.05$). As $\rho$ or $m$ increases, it becomes increasingly anti-conservative, reaching $\nTAnStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$. This is expected as correlation reduces effective information, so the statistic appears more extreme than the independence-based reference implies.

Per-pathway calibration to the correlated null eliminated most errors. Empirical type I error was $\nTMvnInd$ at $\rho=0$, $m=5$ and $\nTMvnStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$, staying within $[\nTMvnMin,\nTMvnMax]$ over the grid. The analytic calibration deviated more in the tail. At $\alpha=0.01$ it reached $\nTAnMaxOne$ versus $\nTMvnMaxOne$ for the calibrated statistic.

The family-wise columns evaluate the Westfall--Young procedure using sample splitting. The family-minimum null is estimated on half the simulated families and tested on the other half, so results are not correct by construction. Empirical FWER ranged from $\nTWyMin$ to $\nTWyMax$, within Monte Carlo error for 250 held-out families (SE $\approx 0.014$ at the nominal level), and the family minimum remained a continuous statistic throughout, taking $\nWyDistinctMin$ distinct values across the $500$ simulated families even in the worst configuration, so the threshold is not resting on a tie. Bonferroni on the analytic $p$-value inherits its miscalibration. FWER was $\nTBonfInd$ under independence with $m=5$ (where the analytic $p$-value is valid) but increased to $\nTBonfMod$ at $\rho=0.3$, $m=20$, and $\nTBonfStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$. With independent pathways, this inflation is entirely due to per-test miscalibration compounded across the family.

None of this is visible in the genomic-control factor $\lambda$, which points the wrong way (Figure~\ref{fig:lambda}). For the analytic omnibus, $\lambda$ was $\nLamAnInd$ at $\rho=0$, $m=50$ and dropped to $\nLamAnStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$, well below one, in the same setting where analytic type I error was largest. There is no contradiction. The $\lambda$ reflects the median null $p$-value, whereas type I error depends on the lower tail. Under strong within-pathway correlation, the analytic null is reshaped rather than shifted, with extra mass in the lower tail and a median above one half, so $\lambda$ can suggest conservatism while the tail is anti-conservative. After per-pathway calibration, $\lambda$ lay in $[\nLamMvnMin,\nLamMvnMax]$. Thus, $\lambda$ near or below one is not evidence that the tail is calibrated.

\subsection*{3.5 No single component is load-bearing}

An omnibus is useful only if no misbehaving component can dominate it, and a family-wise procedure only if that robustness persists at the family level. We tested both on the overlapping pathway universe (Methods), where each gene belongs to $\nBrkMembership$ pathways on average, by deliberately corrupting components and by removing them.

For corruption, we replaced the $p$-value of each of $k$ selected components with $p^{\gamma}$: conservative at $\gamma=0.7$ and anti-conservative at $\gamma=1.4$ (Table~\ref{tab:breakage}, Figure~\ref{fig:breakage}). With $k=0$, the analytic omnibus had mean marginal type~I error $\nBrkAnKzero$ and the calibrated omnibus $\nBrkMvnKzero$, while family-wise rates were $\nBrkWyKzero$ (Westfall--Young) and $\nBrkBonfKzero$ (Bonferroni). Anti-conservative corruption of four of five components increased error for the analytic omnibus to $\nBrkAnKfour$ and for Bonferroni to $\nBrkBonfKfour$, while calibrated marginal and Westfall--Young rates remained stable at $\nBrkMvnKfour$ and $\nBrkWyKfour$. Conservative corruption shifted the analytic omnibus to $\nBrkConsAnKfour$ (loss of power), but calibrated rates again held at $\nBrkConsMvnKfour$ and $\nBrkConsWyKfour$. Across all settings, the family-wise rate stayed within $[\nBrkWyRangeLo,\nBrkWyRangeHi]$ and the marginal rate within $[\nBrkMvnRangeLo,\nBrkMvnRangeHi]$.

Removal is a harsher test than corruption because the component is absent. Dropping each component in turn and rebuilding the null bank, marginal calibration, and family-wise threshold (Table~\ref{tab:loo}, Figure~\ref{fig:loo}) kept marginal type~I error within $[\nLooMvnMin,\nLooMvnMax]$ vs.\ $\nLooFullMvn$ and the Westfall--Young rate within $[\nLooWyMin,\nLooWyMax]$ vs.\ $\nLooFullWy$. The largest change was from removing \texttt{\nLooWorst} ($\nLooWorstVal$). Genomic-control inflation stayed near one for all deletions and both corruption directions (Figures~\ref{fig:breakage_lambda} and~\ref{fig:loo}).

Both findings share the same explanation. Resampling calibration applies the same corruption to null replicates and observed data, so uniform miscalibration largely cancels, and the family-wise threshold (a quantile of the corrupted null) shifts accordingly. Under deletion, the null bank is recomputed for the remaining components, so both $p$-values remain valid for any subset. Neither approach handles data-dependent component error, which would not cancel and was not tested. Analytic calibration offers neither safeguard and therefore inherits corruption directly.

The leave-one-out table shows the clearest contrast between the two family-wise procedures. Averaged over pathway sizes and variants, Westfall--Young stayed at nominal across LD regimes ($\nLooWyInd$, $\nLooWyMod$, $\nLooWyStr$), while Bonferroni rose from $\nLooBonfInd$ to $\nLooBonfMod$ to $\nLooBonfStr$, reaching $\nLooBonfMax$ at worst. Here Bonferroni corrects an analytic $p$-value that is anti-conservative under LD (Section~3.4), so it compounds an uncalibrated per-test statistic with a correction that cannot fix it. Under independence, where the analytic $p$-value is valid, Bonferroni was near nominal. The overlap of $\nBrkMembership$ pathways per gene is not enough to make it noticeably conservative. Family-wise correction inherits per-test miscalibration, while the Westfall--Young threshold, based on the same null as the statistic, does not.

The removed component affects Bonferroni predictably. Dropping Stouffer, Fisher, or soft TFisher lowers the rate, while dropping ACAT or minP raises it. Stouffer and Fisher are inflated by within-pathway correlation (Section~3.6), and soft TFisher is stochastically small because it takes a minimum over thresholds without calibration. ACAT and minP stay near nominal, so removing them makes the omnibus more dominated by inflated components. These choices affect sensitivity, not validity, so users can drop a component they distrust (e.g., minP when gene-level $p$-values are floored), at a power cost for architectures where it performs best. The default should therefore be to run all five. The archetype of a real pathway is unknown in advance, and carrying every component costs only the small regret mentioned in Section~3.3, whereas dropping the one that happens to be sensitive forfeits its power entirely. Dropping a component is a decision for a particular dataset with a stated reason, not a default.


\subsection*{3.6 What the calibration needs from the correlation structure}

The omnibus obscures which input fails. Calibrating components separately (Table~\ref{tab:components_cal}, Figure~\ref{fig:components}) shows that LD damage is concentrated in two of five components, and which two can be predicted from what each statistic measures.

Under the independent null, all components were correctly sized (e.g., ACAT at $\nCmpACATInd$ and Stouffer at $\nCmpStoufferInd$). Under strong LD, the aggregation statistics inflated well beyond the nominal $0.05$, with Fisher (summing $-2\log p$) at $\nCmpFisherStr$ and Stouffer (summing $Z$) at $\nCmpStoufferStr$. The extreme-value statistics changed little by comparison, with ACAT at $\nCmpACATStr$ and minP at $\nCmpminPStr$. Aggregation methods effectively count correlated genes as repeated evidence, inflating the statistic. The extreme-value methods depend on the single strongest gene and are insensitive to near-duplicates. The omnibus inherits its worst inputs’ inflation, reaching $\nCmpOmnibusStr$.

Calibration to the correlated null removed inflation for every component, yielding Fisher $\nCmpFisherCal$, Stouffer $\nCmpStoufferCal$, and omnibus $\nCmpOmnibusCal$. Thus, if a user knows a pathway’s signal architecture, a single calibrated component can replace the omnibus without losing error control. Soft TFisher requires two cautions: (1) it is undefined when no gene falls below any truncation threshold, so such components must be excluded from the omnibus rather than set to $p=1$, since ACAT’s $\tan\{(1/2-p)\pi\}$ transform would treat this boundary value as evidence against the pathway, pushing the median omnibus $p$-value above $1/2$, lowering $\lambda$ below 1, while inflating the tail; and (2) its uncalibrated statistic is a minimum over thresholds, not a $p$-value, so only its calibrated and family-wise entries in Table~\ref{tab:components_cal} are interpretable.

All results so far assume the gene–gene correlation matrix used for calibration matches the data-generating matrix. In practice, the matrix is built by inserting MAGMA-reported pairs into an identity matrix, treating unreported pairs as independent. We quantified the impact by generating data under the true correlations while calibrating with matrices where a known fraction of off-diagonals were set to zero (Table~\ref{tab:missing}, Figure~\ref{fig:missing}). With a complete matrix, marginal type I error was $\nMissMvnZero$ and Westfall–Young family-wise error $\nMissWyZero$ (both nominal). Zeroing half the pairs increased these to $\nMissMvnHalf$ and $\nMissWyHalf$, while zeroing all pairs (pure independence) yielded $\nMissMvnFull$ and $\nMissWyFull$ under moderate LD and $\nMissMvnStrFull$ and $\nMissWyStrFull$ under strong LD.

Scaled by each procedure’s own nominal level (the only fair comparison between per-pathway and per-family rates), the marginal calibration was $\nMissRatioMvnHalf\times$ nominal at $50\%$ missingness and the family-wise procedure $\nMissRatioWyHalf\times$. Westfall–Young is thus more sensitive to an incomplete correlation file than its marginal basis. Because taking the minimum over $K$ pathways is a tail statistic, it depends on the null extreme (where correlation errors hurt most), whereas marginal $p$ values depend mainly on the bulk. This does not negate family-wise control because the relevant comparison is to the correction it replaces. Bonferroni’s family-wise rate in the same runs was $\nMissBonfMod$ and $\nMissBonfStr$ and is unaffected by the correlation file (it uses analytic $p$-values). Westfall–Young remained better up to $\nMissCrossover\%$ missingness, failing only when correlation information was nearly absent, so its null no longer matched the data.

Although it does affect reporting. A Westfall–Young $p$-value is only as reliable as the correlation file, whose completeness can be measured. The check is specific. MAGMA correlation matrices are expected to be sparse because only nearby gene pairs sharing LD are correlated, so zeros for distant genes are appropriate. What matters is the fraction of within-pathway gene pairs that are on the same chromosome and close enough to be correlated for which a value is actually reported.

As in Section~3.4, $\lambda$ flagged none of this. Across the missingness sweep it ranged from $\nMissLamLo$ to $\nMissLamHi$ and drifted \emph{below} one as correlation information was removed, even as the marginal type~I error more than doubled. This is the second independent case where $\lambda \le 1$ coincides with an inflated tail, showing that $\lambda$ should not be the only calibration diagnostic in pathway analysis.

% \subsection*{3.4 The omnibus degrades gracefully when its components are broken}

% A multi-test omnibus is only useful if a single misbehaving component cannot
% dominate it, and a family-wise procedure is only useful if that robustness
% survives at the family level too. We tested both by corrupting components on
% purpose, replacing the $p$-value of each of $k$ selected components with

% With no components corrupted ($k=0$), the analytic omnibus had mean marginal

% family level the Westfall--Young rate was $\nBrkWyKzero$ and Bonferroni
% $\nBrkBonfKzero$. Under anti-conservative corruption of four of the five

% the Westfall--Young family-wise rate at $\nBrkWyKfour$ against Bonferroni's
% $\nBrkBonfKfour$. Under conservative corruption the analytic omnibus moved the
% other way, to $\nBrkConsAnKfour$, losing power rather than control, while the

% \subsection*{3.6 Linkage disequilibrium breaks the aggregation tests, not the
% extreme-value tests}

% The omnibus hides which of its inputs is failing. Calibrating each component
% separately (Table~\ref{tab:components_cal}, Figure~\ref{fig:components}) shows
% that the damage LD does is concentrated in two of the five, and that the
% identity of those two is predictable from what the statistics measure.

% Under the independent null every component was correctly sized (for example
% ACAT at $\nCmpACATInd$ and Stouffer at $\nCmpStoufferInd$), which is the
% expected behaviour when the generating assumption holds. Under strong LD the
% two aggregation statistics---Fisher, which sums $-2\log p$ across the pathway,
% and Stouffer, which sums $Z$-scores---inflated to $\nCmpFisherStr$ and
% $\nCmpStoufferStr$ against the nominal $0.05$. The two extreme-value
% statistics barely moved: ACAT went to $\nCmpACATStr$ and minP to
% $\nCmpminPStr$. The mechanism is transparent. An aggregation statistic adds one
% term per gene and implicitly treats each as independent evidence, so correlated
% genes are counted repeatedly and the statistic grows; a statistic that depends
% on the most extreme gene is insensitive to how many near-duplicates of it the
% pathway contains. The omnibus inherited the inflation of its worst inputs,
% reaching $\nCmpOmnibusStr$.

% Calibration against the correlated null removed this for every component,
% bringing Fisher to $\nCmpFisherCal$, Stouffer to $\nCmpStoufferCal$ and the
% omnibus to $\nCmpOmnibusCal$. The practical consequence is that a user who knows a pathway's signal
% architecture may use a single calibrated component in place of the omnibus
% without losing error control, which is what makes the architecture-specific
% testing described in Section~2.3 usable.

% This result also explains an observation in Section~3.5: removing an
% aggregation component lowered the Bonferroni family-wise rate while removing
% ACAT or minP raised it. The aggregation components are the ones that were
% inflated in the first place.

% Three implementation notes belong here, all about soft TFisher. First, the
% statistic is undefined on a pathway in which no gene falls below any truncation
% threshold. Under the grid of Section~2.1 this did not occur at $m\ge20$ in any
% configuration, but it is frequent in small pathways under grids confined to
% small $\tau$, and a single undefined draw in a calibration bank makes that
% pathway's calibrated $p$-value undefined unless non-finite draws are filtered
% before the tail count, which the implementation does. Second, an undefined
% component must be excluded from the omnibus rather than entered at $p=1$
% (Section~2.1). Because ACAT weights by $\tan\{(1/2-p)\pi\}$, entering the
% boundary value makes a test that could not be computed act as evidence against
% the pathway; under the null it pushes the median omnibus $p$-value above one
% half, so that $\lambda$ falls below one while the tail is simultaneously
% inflated. Third, soft TFisher's uncalibrated value is a minimum over thresholds
% rather than a $p$-value, so its analytic columns in
% Table~\ref{tab:components_cal} are not interpretable as calibration; only its
% calibrated and family-wise columns are.

% \subsection*{3.7 Both calibrations degrade when the correlation file is
% incomplete, and the family-wise one degrades faster}

% Every result above assumes the gene--gene correlation matrix is the one the
% data were generated from. In practice it is assembled by filling MAGMA's
% reported gene pairs into an identity matrix, so a pair MAGMA does not report is
% silently treated as independent. We measured the cost directly by generating
% data from the true correlation structure while calibrating from a matrix with a
% known fraction of its off-diagonal entries zeroed
% (Table~\ref{tab:missing}, Figure~\ref{fig:missing}).

% With a complete matrix the marginal type~I error was $\nMissMvnZero$ and the
% Westfall--Young family-wise rate $\nMissWyZero$, both at nominal. Dropping half
% the pairs raised them to $\nMissMvnHalf$ and $\nMissWyHalf$; dropping all of
% them, which is the independence assumption in its pure form, gave
% $\nMissMvnFull$ and $\nMissWyFull$ under moderate LD and $\nMissMvnStrFull$ and
% $\nMissWyStrFull$ under strong LD.

% Expressed as multiples of each procedure's own nominal level---the only fair
% comparison between a per-pathway rate and a per-family one---the marginal
% calibration was $\nMissRatioMvnHalf\times$ nominal at $50\%$ missingness and
% the family-wise procedure $\nMissRatioWyHalf\times$. Westfall--Young is
% therefore more sensitive to an incomplete correlation file than the marginal
% calibration it is built from. The reason is structural rather than
% incidental: the family minimum over $K$ pathways is a tail statistic, so it
% depends on the extreme of the null distribution, which is precisely where an
% error in the correlation structure does the most damage. The marginal
% $p$-value depends only on the bulk.

% This does not overturn the case for family-wise control, because the comparison
% that matters is against the correction it replaces rather than against the
% marginal rate it is not competing with. Bonferroni's family-wise rate in the
% same runs was $\nMissBonfMod$ and $\nMissBonfStr$ and does not depend on the
% correlation file at all, since it is computed from the analytic $p$-value.
% Westfall--Young remained the better family-wise procedure up to $\nMissCrossover\%$
% missingness, and lost only when essentially no correlation information
% remained---at which point it is calibrating against a null that does not
% resemble the data.

% It does, however, change how the procedure should be reported. A
% Westfall--Young $p$-value is only as trustworthy as the correlation file behind
% it, and the completeness of that file is measurable rather than assumed. The
% quantity to check is narrow: a correlation matrix from MAGMA is \emph{supposed}
% to be sparse, because correlations are computed only for gene pairs close
% enough to share LD, so a zero between distant genes is correct rather than
% missing. What matters is the fraction of within-pathway gene pairs that are on
% the same chromosome and close enough to be correlated for which a value is
% actually reported. We provide \texttt{10\_pair\_coverage.R} for this, and
% recommend that it be run and its result stated alongside any family-wise
% $p$-value.

% Finally, and consistently with Section~3.3, $\lambda$ did not flag any of this.
% Across the missingness sweep it ranged from $\nMissLamLo$ to $\nMissLamHi$,
% drifting \emph{below} one as correlation information was removed, while the
% marginal type~I error more than doubled. This is the second independent setting
% in which $\lambda$ at or below one coexists with an inflated tail, and it is the
% clearest argument we can make that $\lambda$ should not be used as the sole
% calibration diagnostic in pathway analysis.

\subsection*{3.7 Across five ancestry groups, human blood lipids yield the same leading pathway and a stronger candidate gene lists}

We analysed LDL-C and HDL-C using GLGC 2021 meta-analyses \citep{Graham2021GLGC} across five ancestry groups. European (EUR, $n\approx1.32$ million), African/African American (AFR, $n\approx99{,}400$), East Asian (EAS, $n\approx146{,}500$), South Asian (SAS, $n\approx41{,}000$) and Hispanic (HIS, $n\approx48{,}100$). Each ancestry file is an independent meta-analysis with distinct allele frequencies and LD. The four non-European samples are 9--32$\times$ smaller than EUR. All ten trait--ancestry sets were processed identically, with LD from 1000 Genomes Phase 3 panels matched to each group (AMR for HIS), variant-to-gene mapping over a $10\,\mathrm{kb}$ window using NCBI37.3 that yielded $18{,}429$--$18{,}563$ genes per set, and pre-specified settings ($1{,}390$ Reactome pathways, $B=10{,}000$, five components, gene-level genomic control, candidate-gene score and weights, and established-gene lists, Section~2.7). No dataset-specific tuning was performed, so sets differ only by phenotype and population.

In the European data, significance no longer discriminates between pathways. At the gene level, $44.1\%$ of genes had $p<0.05$ for LDL-C and $11.2\%$ were genome-wide significant. For HDL-C the figures were $52.7\%$ and $14.8\%$. Thus, a random set of 50 genes contains on average $5.6$ (LDL-C) and $7.4$ (HDL-C) genome-wide significant genes, so the self-contained null is false for most sets. Accordingly, $\nLipSigLDL$ (LDL-C) and $\nLipSigHDL$ (HDL-C) of the $\nLipNpw$ pathways were significant under Westfall--Young family-wise control at $\alpha=0.05$. The correction, however, is not the issue. In a saturated genome, large sets accumulate evidence simply because they are large. The 20 top-ranked pathways had median sizes of $\nLipTopTwentyMedSizeLDL$ (LDL-C) and $\nLipTopTwentyMedSizeHDL$ (HDL-C) genes versus $\nLipMedSizeAll$ overall, and the Spearman correlation between set size and $z_{\mathrm{std}}$ was $\nLipSizeRhoLDL$ and $\nLipSizeRhoHDL$. Table~\ref{tab:density} shows that this size dependence tracks gene-level signal density.

Plasma lipoprotein metabolism is the top pathway across populations. The Reactome plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling and clearance pathway (including the LDL receptor and apolipoproteins) ranked 1 of $1{,}390$ in all ten trait--ancestry sets (Figure~\ref{fig:replication}A,B). Related sub-pathways were also consistently highly ranked. In the plasma-lipoprotein subtree, five or six of nine pathways placed in the top ten in every non-European set, including plasma lipoprotein clearance/remodeling, LDL clearance (LDL-C), and HDL/chylomicron remodeling (HDL-C). Two LXR-mediated pathways regulating \textit{ABCA1} and other lipoprotein genes also ranked in the top ten in most groups.

Below the very top, Europeans differed because many pathways were near-saturated. The smaller lipoprotein sets ranked $7$--$342$ (LDL-C) and $8$--$853$ (HDL-C), behind large generic sets (e.g., interferon-$\gamma$ signalling, SUMOylation), though they remained extreme relative to size-matched pathways (Figure~\ref{fig:lipid_size}). Among pathways within a factor of $1.25$ in gene count, the subtree’s median size-matched percentile was $\nLipFamPctMedLDL\%$ (LDL-C) and $\nLipFamPctMedHDL\%$ (HDL-C), versus $\nLipAllPctMedLipoFreeLDL\%$ and $\nLipAllPctMedLipoFreeHDL\%$ for all other pathways. In non-European sets, with only $93$--$592$ significant pathways, the background was sparse and lipoprotein pathways led without size adjustment. The same pathway ranking first under both saturated and sparse conditions, across five populations with different LD, supports a biological rather than dataset-driven ordering. Table~\ref{tab:lipid_pathways} lists the ten leading European pathways for each trait.

Furthermore, LDL-C and HDL-C converge on the same pathway via different genes. At the gene level, the traits disagree. The Spearman correlation of gene-level $Z$ statistics was $\nLipGeneRho$, 13 of the top 100 genes overlapped, and the Jaccard overlap of genome-wide significant genes was 0.208 (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}A). In the lipoprotein pathway, LDL-C is driven by receptor uptake (\textit{LDLR}, \textit{PCSK9}, \textit{LDLRAP1}, \textit{APOB}, \textit{APOE}), whereas HDL-C is driven by reverse transport and lipolysis (\textit{ABCA1}, \textit{LIPC}, \textit{SCARB1}, \textit{LPL}, \textit{CETP}). Only four of the 12 top drivers per trait are shared (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}B). Thus, two GWAS with different driver genes map to the same process because it contains both arms. Across pathways, trait ranks are correlated ($\nLipCrossRho$; Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}C). This largely reflects a shared size-dependent background, so the evidence for convergence comes from driver-level overlap instead.

Replication occurs at the locus level, with the pathway layer selecting the gene. Seven genes for LDL-C and eight for HDL-C ranked in the top twenty in all five groups (Figure~\ref{fig:replication}C,D): for LDL-C, established \textit{LDLR}, \textit{APOE}, \textit{PCSK9} and \textit{APOB}, plus \textit{APOC1}, \textit{PVRL2} and \textit{TOMM40}; for HDL-C, established \textit{ABCA1}, \textit{CETP}, \textit{LPL}, \textit{LIPC} and \textit{APOA5}, plus \textit{NLRC5}, \textit{SLC12A3} and \textit{HERPUD1}. In Europeans, the three-layer score (Section~2.5) ranked \textit{LDLR} first for LDL-C and \textit{ABCA1} first for HDL-C (Table~\ref{tab:lipid_candidates}). The non-established genes neighbour established ones—either in the chromosome~19 \textit{APOE} cluster or near \textit{CETP} on chromosome~16—and rank highly because they share the lead variant; \textit{APOE}, \textit{APOC1} and \textit{TOMM40} have tied GWAS ranks (Table~\ref{tab:replication_genes}).

The pathway layer resolves this tie. GWAS cannot distinguish genes that share a lead variant, but curated pathway membership often can: \textit{APOE} and \textit{CETP} are in the leading pathway, whereas \textit{TOMM40}, \textit{PVRL2}, \textit{HERPUD1}, \textit{NLRC5} and \textit{SLC12A3} are not. Accordingly, \textit{APOE} ranks above \textit{TOMM40} in all five groups and above \textit{PVRL2} in four; \textit{CETP} ranks above \textit{HERPUD1} and \textit{SLC12A3} in all groups and above \textit{NLRC5} in four. This relies on the pathway layer ranking pathways by the calibrated statistic $z_{\mathrm{std}}$ (Section~2.5).
 The approach has limits: genes tied across all three layers cannot be separated (e.g., \textit{APOC1} shares both lead variant and pathway with \textit{APOE} and ranks just behind it), and the layers are complementary rather than individually reliable (e.g., \textit{CETP} has the strongest variant in all groups but MAGMA rank $3$--$72$, while \textit{APOB} is MAGMA first in South Asians and $185$th in Europeans).

This result does not show the score recovers lipid biology: the “established” genes come from the same literature as curated pathways, so prior knowledge appears on both sides. It does show the score can resolve loci left ambiguous by GWAS by using prior knowledge to distinguish genes that association data rank equally. Practically, in replicated loci the combined score usually ranks the known trait gene first, aiding studies with limited validation, but identifying a causal gene still needs evidence not captured by the score (e.g., expression colocalisation or fine-mapping; Section~4.3).

CATFISH also reproduces the same top pathway and the same loci across two related phenotypes and five populations, despite up to 32-fold sample-size differences and differing linkage disequilibrium, using inputs with no overlapping individuals.

% At the gene level, $44.1\%$ of tested genes had $p<0.05$ for LDL-C and $11.2\%$

\subsection*{3.8 A permutation null on sorghum genotypes: genomic control calibrates each pathway, and family-wise error stays above nominal}

The simulations of Sections~3.2--3.6 show that the procedure is calibrated when
gene $z$-scores follow its correlation model. The permutation null of
Section~2.7 asks whether real GWAS output does. The BAP is a demanding case:
with $330$ lines signal is sparse, and LD extends far (each gene has on average
$\nSorNeighbours$ other genes with $|r|\ge0.1$, against $\nLipNeighbours$ in
the human panel). Table~\ref{tab:sorghum_null} and
Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_null} summarise $30{,}400$ null pathway tests ($304$
pathways in each of $100$ permutations).

Without genomic control the calibrated $p$-values were not uniform. The
inflation factor was $0.84$, yet $7.2\%$ of null pathways fell below $0.05$ and
$2.3\%$ below $0.01$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_null}A), and $18$ of the $100$
permutations had at least one pathway significant under family-wise control
(family-wise error $0.18$, $95\%$ CI $0.11$--$0.27$;
Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_null}D). The excess grew with pathway size, from
$6.1\%$ for pathways of $5$--$9$ genes to $12.7\%$ for $50$--$200$ genes
(Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_null}C). A deflated median with an inflated tail is
the mark of a mixture, and here the mixture is over permutations.

Each permutation shifts all of its gene $z$-scores together. The mean gene
$z$-score of a permutation had a standard deviation of $0.24$ across
permutations (range $-0.66$ to $0.66$), against $0.075$ expected under the
correlated null built from MAGMA's correlations and $0.005$ if the $33{,}907$
genes were independent. The shift was shared by all chromosomes: per-chromosome
means were correlated at $0.80$, and genes on different chromosomes, which share
no LD, were correlated at $0.06$ across permutations, a correlation that
disappeared once each permutation's genome-wide mean was removed. The
correlated null cannot reproduce this, because it treats chromosomes as
independent and centres every gene at zero. A self-contained test reads the
shift as signal, and the signal accumulates with pathway size: the number of
pathways below $0.05$ in a permutation tracked its mean gene $z$-score (Pearson
$0.77$, Spearman $0.90$), and $16$ of the $18$ permutations with a family-wise
false positive had a positive shift (Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_null}B). The shift
is a property of each GWAS as a whole, not of linkage disequilibrium; we did not
identify its source within the mixed model.

Genomic control removes it. With gene $z$-scores centred at their median
(Section~2.7), the inflation factor was $1.01$, $5.2\%$ of null pathways fell
below $0.05$ and $1.3\%$ below $0.01$, and the dependence on pathway size
disappeared ($5.0\%$ to $6.0\%$ across the four size classes). The family-wise
error rate fell to $0.12$ ($12$ of $98$ permutations; $95\%$ CI
$0.06$--$0.20$). %% TODO: 100 permutations once the last two GC runs finish.
The $21$ family-wise false positives were $21$ different pathways, so no pathway
is a recurrent false positive in this panel.

The remaining excess is in the extreme tail. After centring, calibrated
$p$-values below $0.01$ were $1.3$ times as frequent as expected and those below
$0.001$ $1.8$ times; gene $p$-values below $10^{-3}$, $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-5}$
were $1.7$, $3.2$ and $6.7$ times as frequent. The family minimum is a tail
statistic, so a modest excess per pathway becomes a larger one family-wise. We
examined two sources. The first is the correlation matrix. Across permutations,
gene pairs that MAGMA reports at $r=0.22$ were almost uncorrelated ($0.03$),
pairs at $r=0.52$ were correlated at $0.32$, and the two agreed only above
about $0.8$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_null}E). MAGMA computes the correlations
from the raw genotypes, which carry the population structure that the mixed
model removes, so moderate correlations are overstated. Overstated correlation
widens the null and should make the test conservative, and replacing MAGMA's
correlations with those observed across permutations did not lower the
family-wise error rate ($0.15$, $95\%$ CI $0.09$--$0.24$; inflation factor
$1.01$; type I error $0.055$ at $0.05$). The second source is the variant-level
test. In five permutations, variant $p$-values below $10^{-4}$ were $4.5$ times
as frequent as expected: $11.5$ times at minor allele frequency $0.01$--$0.02$,
$4.8$ times at $0.02$--$0.05$, and $2.0$ and $1.7$ times at $0.05$--$0.10$ and
above (Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_null}F). The mixed-model Wald test is
heavy-tailed in this panel, most of all for rare variants, and a multivariate
normal null has Gaussian tails by construction. We did not remove variants to
correct this: a filter chosen because it calibrates the null is tuned on the
outcome.

In this panel, then, CATFISH with genomic control gives calibrated per-pathway
$p$-values, and its family-wise $p$-values are anti-conservative by a factor of
about two and a half ($0.12$ against $0.05$). The excess comes from the tail of
the variant-level test, upstream of the pathway procedure.

\section{4 Discussion}

\subsection*{4.1 What the Westfall--Young layer buys, and what it does not}

The contribution of the Westfall--Young layer is narrow and should be stated
narrowly. Reading the null matrix across its rows rather than down its columns
costs nothing---the draws have already been taken---and it makes the family-wise
statement exact under the actual overlap structure of the pathway database being
used, without requiring that structure to be estimated or even described. It
does not change any pathway's marginal evidence, which is what the column-wise
reading gives, and it does not improve power. In the shared-universe simulations with no within-pathway correlation, where
the analytic $p$-value is valid, the Westfall--Young threshold was $\nWyGainLo$
to $\nWyGainHi$ times the Bonferroni threshold $\nBonfThrK$: modestly more
permissive, which is the gain from accounting for overlap at an average of
$\nBrkMembership$ pathways per gene. That is enough to matter for a borderline result and not enough to
rescue an analysis that has gone wrong for other reasons.

The practical value is diagnostic as much as inferential. The ratio of the
Bonferroni threshold to the realized Westfall--Young threshold is a single
number that tells an analyst how far from independent their pathway family
actually is. A ratio near one says the database behaves like a set of
independent tests and Bonferroni is close to right; a ratio far from one says
the correction in common use is substantially miscalibrated for this particular
collection of gene sets. That number is cheap to compute as a by-product of the
procedure and, as far as we are aware, is not routinely reported.

The ratio has to be read on calibrated statistics to mean this. In the same
simulations with within-pathway correlation, the threshold set on the analytic
scale was $\nWyStrictLo$ to $\nWyStrictHi$ times \emph{stricter} than
Bonferroni---not because the pathway family was more dependent, but because the
analytic $p$-value was anti-conservative and the Westfall--Young threshold,
drawn from the same null, corrected for it. On that scale the ratio mixes the
miscalibration of the per-test statistic with the dependence among tests and
cannot separate them.

We were initially wrong about one aspect of this and correct the record here. We
expected the size-standardized Westfall--Young variant of Section~2.3 to remove
the dependence of pathway evidence on pathway size, on the reasoning that
expressing each statistic against its own null bank puts all pathways on a
common scale. It did not: in the maize data the size--evidence correlation moved
from $-0.807$ to $-0.800$ in the human analysis and remained essentially
unchanged elsewhere. The reason is visible once measured. The standard
deviations of the per-size null banks differ by only a few percent across the
pathway sizes we examined, so the standardization divides by a nearly constant
factor and cannot remove a dependence that lives in the numerator. The size
dependence is in the observed statistics, and in a saturated regime it is real
accumulation of signal, not a scaling artefact. Standardization is still worth
reporting because it changes individual rankings, but it is not a correction for
size bias and should not be presented as one.

\subsection*{4.2 Saturation and the self-contained hypothesis}

The LDL-cholesterol result in Section~3.9 is, we think, the most consequential
finding in this paper, and it is not about CATFISH. Self-contained gene-set
tests ask whether the genes in a set show more association than the null
distribution of no association \citep{Goeman2007,Maciejewski2014}. That question
has a definite answer, and at $n\approx1.3$ million for a highly polygenic trait
the answer is yes for almost every set of genes, because $11.2\%$ of all genes
are individually genome-wide significant. A random set of fifty genes rejects
the self-contained null with probability $0.997$. Nothing has malfunctioned; the
hypothesis being tested has simply stopped discriminating.

Three consequences follow. First, as GWAS sample sizes grow, self-contained
testing will fail on more traits, and it will fail silently, producing long
lists of highly significant pathways that look like results. Second, no amount
of multiple-testing sophistication fixes this: we applied strong family-wise
control and $\nLipSigLDL$ of $\nLipNpw$ pathways remained significant for LDL-C
($\nLipSigHDL$ for HDL-C). Reporting a
more careful threshold on a saturated analysis produces a more carefully wrong
answer. Third, the competitive hypothesis---whether the genes in a set are
\emph{more} associated than genes outside it---does not saturate in the same
way, because the comparison is against the observed genome-wide background
rather than against no association. For large, well-powered human GWAS, the
competitive framing is the appropriate one, and the self-contained framing that
CATFISH implements is best suited to the low-signal, strongly polygenic regime
illustrated by the maize analysis, where no gene reaches genome-wide
significance and a ranking is the only available output.

This is a boundary on the applicability of the method we are presenting, and we
would rather state it than discover it in review. It also says how the human
results should be read. Significance is uninformative there, but the ordering
is not: the top of both lists mixes lipoprotein pathways with large generic
sets that accumulate evidence because a saturated genome enriches any large
set, and once each pathway is compared with others of its size, the
lipoprotein pathways are extreme in both traits (Section~3.9). In a saturated
analysis the useful output of a self-contained method is a size-aware ranking
and a gene list, not a list of significant sets.

\subsection*{4.3 What the ranking and the gene score add when significance
saturates}

The lipid analyses show that saturation removes the significance list but not
the ordering, and two things are needed to read the ordering. The first is
pathway size. In a saturated genome every large set accumulates evidence, so
the top of any self-contained ranking is crowded with large generic sets, while
a relevant small set can be extreme for its size and still rank in the
hundreds overall. Comparing each pathway with others of similar size recovered
the plasma-lipoprotein subtree in both traits (Figure~\ref{fig:lipid_size}).
That comparison is descriptive; an inferential version would need a null
distribution for size-matched ranks, which the resampling of Section~2.3 could
supply but which we have not implemented.

The second is that the pathway result is most useful when it is carried down to
genes. Ranked by its strongest variant, a gene inherits the evidence of its
whole locus; ranked by its gene-level test, it can lose a strong signal carried
by one or two variants. The combined score recovered the established genes of
both traits better than either layer alone, across the range of weights we
examined, because genes supported by several layers rise and genes supported by
one fall. The gain is real but modest, and
it is partly circular: the pathway layer rewards membership in curated
pathways, which favours well-studied genes, so recovery of established genes
overstates what the score would achieve for genes not yet in any pathway
database. The score also ranks loci rather than genes. The established genes in
our lists sit beside LD neighbours with almost identical evidence, and only
fine-mapping or functional data can separate them.

\subsection*{4.4 Two regimes, two kinds of validation}

The lipid and sorghum analyses validate different properties of the method.
The lipid traits have abundant signal and short-range LD, and the evidence that
the ordering is meaningful is convergence: two traits whose gene-level signals
are largely disjoint reach the same pathway through different arms of it. They
say little about false positives, because there the null is false nearly
everywhere. The sorghum traits have sparse signal and far denser LD
(Table~\ref{tab:sorghum_regimes}), and the evidence there is calibration: the
independence-based omnibus over-rejected, the correlated null brought it much
closer to nominal, and family-wise control declared nothing. A method that
reported pathway discoveries in this panel would be suspect; this one ranked
pathways and genes without claiming significance it could not support.

Two lessons carry over to other datasets. First, the gene-level model has to be
checked before any pathway output is read. In the BAP, MAGMA's multi-model
output was inflated in proportion to gene size, and a correlated pathway null,
however well it models LD, would have passed that inflation through as pathway
signal. Second, curated annotation cuts both ways. It is what let the pathway
layer lift all three lignin genes, and it is also what lifted a WRKY
transcription factor through an enzymatic annotation it should not carry.
Candidates whose rank depends on the pathway layer should have that annotation
checked; the score makes this straightforward because each layer is reported
separately.

\subsection*{4.5 Calibration on real genotypes}

The simulations and the permutation null answer different questions. The
simulations draw gene $z$-scores from the correlation model that the null is
built on, and there the per-pathway and family-wise error rates were nominal
for every LD structure we examined (Sections~3.2--3.6). The permutation null
pushes real genotypes through the whole pipeline (Section~3.8), and it found
two departures from that model. Neither is a failure to model LD.

The first is a genome-wide shift in gene $z$-scores that differs between GWAS
of the same panel. It is shared across chromosomes, so no gene--gene
correlation matrix, however accurate, contains it; and it is one number per
GWAS, so centring the gene $z$-scores at their median removes it. Centring
brought the per-pathway calibration to nominal (inflation factor $1.01$, type I
error $0.052$) and removed the dependence of false positives on pathway size.
It does not alter the data or the order of genes, and it costs nothing, so we
apply it to every real analysis and recommend it by default. Its cost is in a
saturated GWAS, where the median gene is associated and centring removes real
polygenic signal. There it turns the self-contained question part of the way
towards the competitive one, which Section~4.2 argues is the better question in
that regime.

The second is the tail of the variant-level test. After centring, the
family-wise error rate was $0.12$, and the excess traced to variant $p$-values
far more extreme than their nominal distribution, most of all at minor allele
frequencies below $0.05$. A natural explanation for residual miscalibration in
a densely linked panel is that MAGMA's gene correlations are wrong, and they
are: correlations computed from raw genotypes overstate moderate correlation
once a mixed model has removed population structure. But replacing them did not
lower the family-wise error, and overstated correlation errs in the
conservative direction. The residual is in the input, not in the correlation
model, and a null that is Gaussian by construction cannot absorb it. Removing
rare variants until the null looked right would tune the input to the outcome,
so we report the excess instead. In a panel like the BAP the per-pathway
calibrated $p$-value is the reliable output, and a family-wise $p$-value near
$0.05$ should be read as weaker evidence than its nominal level.

Neither departure changes the conclusions of the real analyses, which use
centred gene $p$-values throughout. In sorghum, no pathway was significant under
family-wise control for either trait. Centring halved the share of pathways with
calibrated $p<0.05$, from $14.5\%$ to $7.2\%$ for dry biomass and from $8.2\%$
to $2.6\%$ for lignin, close to the nominal rate the permutation null predicts;
and a test that errs towards false positives and still declares nothing
supports that conclusion. In the lipid data the ordering is robust to centring.
The plasma lipoprotein pathway ranked first in all ten trait--ancestry sets with
centring and in nine without (European HDL-C, where it ranked third); the
Spearman correlation of pathway statistics with and without centring was
$0.96$--$0.99$; and the number of family-wise significant pathways fell by
$11$--$24\%$. The sets that lost most were the large generic ones that a
saturated genome favours: in the European data the median size of the twenty
leading pathways fell from $159$ to $118$ genes for LDL-C and from $172$ to
$120$ for HDL-C.

\subsection*{4.6 Numerical precision is a correctness issue}

Section~2.4 reads like an implementation note and is not one. Had we not moved
to log space, this paper would have reported a top-pathway list that was
determined by file order, with every leading $p$-value equal to the
double-precision floor, and nothing in the output would have indicated that
anything was wrong. We encountered exactly this failure twice during
development, once from ACAT overflow and once from underflow of the combined
$p$-value, and in both cases the symptom was a plausible-looking table.

The simulations produced a third instance, in a different component and with a
different symptom. The soft TFisher implementation returns the null
distribution function of its statistic, and the $p$-value is one minus that
value; past $1-10^{-16}$ the difference is exactly zero. In the exchangeable
design at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$, about $3.5\%$ of null pathways reached that point
and were reported at the general lower floor. With the floor at $10^{-50}$ the
empirical family-wise error rate in that configuration was $0.24$. Lowering the
floor to $10^{-300}$, in an attempt to recover resolution, raised it to $1.00$:
the spurious value became more extreme than anything another component
produced, so the omnibus family minimum was the same shared number in
essentially every simulated family and the Westfall--Young threshold sat on the
tie. The fix was to cap the component at the precision it has. At $10^{-16}$,
the components with closed-form tails---which in the same configuration resolve
genuinely distinct values below $10^{-100}$---determine the family minimum, and
it is once again a continuous statistic. A floor is a claim about resolution; a
floor set below the resolution a computation actually has manufactures ties that
look like extreme evidence.

We therefore suggest that any pathway method intended for use on modern
large-$n$ GWAS summary statistics should state explicitly how it represents
$p$-values below $10^{-300}$, and that reviewers should ask. The arithmetic is
not difficult; the problem is that the failure is invisible unless specifically
checked for. A simple diagnostic is to count how many pathways share the
smallest reported $p$-value. If that count exceeds one or two, the ranking below
that point is not meaningful. For a family-wise procedure the corresponding
check is the number of distinct values taken by the null family minimum; a
threshold drawn from a distribution with a large atom is not a threshold.

\subsection*{4.7 Practical recommendations}

For users, our recommendations are as follows. Inspect the gene-level null
before running any pathway analysis: a median gene-level $p$-value far from
$0.5$ indicates either genuine saturation or population-structure inflation, and
these require different responses (the former argues for competitive testing,
the latter for a mixed model). Check also that the gene-level null does not
drift with gene size; under strong LD, MAGMA's top-variant and multi-model
$p$-values can be inflated in proportion to the number of variants in a gene
(Section~3.10.1). Centre the gene $z$-scores at their median before the
pathway test (Section~2.7); it removes the per-GWAS shift that otherwise
inflates self-contained tests in proportion to pathway size. Where genotypes
are available, a phenotype-permutation null shows whether the family-wise
$p$-values can be taken at face value in that panel (Section~3.8). Calibrate
against the correlated null whenever
gene--gene correlations are available, and verify that the correlation file
covers all chromosomes; in one of our own runs only chromosome~1
correlations had been parsed, and $85\%$ of the genome was silently treated as
independent, a failure that produced no error and no obviously wrong output.
Report the per-pathway calibrated $p$-values and the Westfall--Young family-wise
ones, together with the Bonferroni threshold and its ratio to the
Westfall--Young threshold computed on calibrated statistics (Section~4.1), since
the comparison is informative about the database. Before trusting any
family-wise threshold, count the distinct values taken by the null family
minimum. When using adaptive soft TFisher, choose a truncation grid that spans
small and large thresholds; a grid confined to small $\tau$ leaves the statistic
empty on most small pathways. Finally, treat the ranking rather than the significance list as the
deliverable, and validate it against independent biology where possible, as we
have attempted to do with the maize, lipid and sorghum analyses. In a saturated
analysis, compare each pathway with others of its size rather than reading its
overall rank, and read a candidate-gene list as a ranking of loci. Check the
annotation behind any candidate whose rank depends on the pathway layer.

\section{5 Limitations and future work}

Several limitations bound what can be concluded from this work, and we list them
explicitly.

\textbf{No simulated GWAS.} The simulations in Section~2.6 operate at the level
of the omnibus statistic and its null, using block-structured and exchangeable
correlation models for gene--gene dependence. We did not simulate genotypes, assign causal
variants, generate phenotypes, and push them through the full
GWAS--MAGMA--CATFISH pipeline. That experiment is the natural next step and is
the one that would establish power against realistic genetic architectures
rather than against latent mean shifts; it was deliberately excluded here rather
than attempted partially. Consequently, the archetype power curves of Section~3.1
are power against stylised signal shapes injected on the gene-level $z$ scale,
not against realistic genetic architectures.

\textbf{Unreported correlations are set to zero.} The correlation matrices of
Section~2.3 are built by filling MAGMA's reported gene pairs into an identity
matrix, so any pair MAGMA does not report is treated as independent. For distant
gene pairs this is close to true and the matrix is banded by construction, but a
nearby pair that goes unreported---because it fell outside a batch boundary, or
because one gene was dropped---becomes a genuine understatement of dependence,
in the anti-conservative direction. Section~3.7 quantifies the cost, and
corrects an assumption we made before measuring it: the two readings do
\emph{not} inherit the limitation equally. The family-wise reading degrades
faster than the marginal one, because the family minimum is a tail
statistic and an error in the correlation structure does its worst damage in
the tail. Verifying chromosome coverage of the correlation file catches the
gross form of this failure; \texttt{10\_pair\_coverage.R} measures the
fine-grained form, and we recommend reporting it alongside any family-wise
$p$-value.

\textbf{Simplified LD models.} Real gene--gene correlation is driven by physical
proximity and decays continuously with distance. The shared-universe simulations
approximate this with independent blocks of exchangeable correlation, so a
pathway spanning several blocks has mostly uncorrelated members; the
exchangeable design instead correlates every member of a pathway, which is far
stronger than block-structured LD produces and is used only as a stress test.
Neither reproduces the gradual decay of correlation with distance. The real-data
analyses use empirical MAGMA correlations and are not subject to this
limitation.

\textbf{What strong control rests on.} The argument of Section~2.3 for strong
family-wise control is exact under its model and inherits that model's limits.
It assumes association acts as a shift in the means of associated genes'
$z$-scores, leaving the correlation among unassociated genes unchanged, and it
defines a null pathway as one containing no associated gene. Under linkage
disequilibrium a gene can be associated at the gene level solely because it is
in LD with a causal variant in a neighbouring gene; a pathway containing it is
then non-null under the self-contained gene-level hypothesis even if it contains
no causal gene, so rejecting it is not a family-wise error, although it may be a
false positive biologically. The argument also inherits the adequacy of the
correlation matrix, and Section~3.7 shows how quickly family-wise calibration
degrades when that matrix is incomplete. We have not separately confirmed strong
control by simulation; under the model it follows from the complete-null
results.

\textbf{Parametric null.} The null draws come from a multivariate normal model
of gene--gene correlation rather than from permuting phenotypes. The procedure
is Westfall--Young in construction, but its validity is that of the correlation
model, which Section~3.7 makes concrete. The sorghum permutation null
(Section~3.8) tests that model directly: after genomic control the per-pathway
calibration was nominal, but the family-wise error rate was $0.12$, because the
variant-level tests are heavier-tailed than a Gaussian null. We tested one
panel and one trait. Panels with less LD or larger samples, and association
tests with better-calibrated tails, may behave differently, and a permutation
null is the check we recommend before relying on family-wise $p$-values in a
new panel.

\textbf{Soft TFisher resolution.} With the current implementation the soft
TFisher $p$-value cannot be resolved below about $10^{-16}$, so among strongly
enriched pathways that component ties. The other components resolve far beyond
this and determine the omnibus ordering there; a log-scale implementation of
the soft-thresholding tail would remove the limitation.

\textbf{No competitive arm.} We were unable to include a direct comparison
against MAGMA's competitive gene-set test in this version, because the
competitive entry point is not currently exported from the CATFISH package
namespace. Given the argument of Section~4.2, that comparison is the single most
important addition to make, and it is in progress.

\textbf{Incomplete cross-species replication.} The Westfall--Young analysis was
run on maize, sorghum and human data. The sorghum stem-volume dataset used in the
original CATFISH paper could not be re-analysed under the new calibration
because the MAGMA \texttt{.genes.raw} file, which the genome-wide correlated
draw requires, was not available in the archived results; its signal-density
summary in Table~\ref{tab:density} is reported from the gene-level output alone.
The sorghum analyses of Section~3.10 use a different panel, the BAP, and with
$330$ lines they are underpowered for pathway discovery; they test calibration
and gene ranking, not the detection of new pathways.

\textbf{Tail resolution.} Monte Carlo calibration resolves $p$-values only to
$1/(B+1)$. CATFISH offers a generalized Pareto tail extrapolation for the
extreme tail \citep{Knijnenburg2009}, but it requires a minimum number of valid
null draws, and a single missing draw can silently disable it; in one of our
human runs this caused $638$ of $1{,}348$ pathways to tie at the Monte Carlo
floor. The threshold is now set to half the number of permutations rather than
to the full number, but we have not yet verified the fix at production scale.

\textbf{Dependence on upstream choices.} Results remain sensitive to the
SNP-to-gene window, the reference panel used for LD, gene model definitions, and
the pathway database, as is true of every method in this class. The $10\,$kb
windows used here are conventional but not optimal for any particular trait.

\section{6 Conclusion}

Pathway analysis of GWAS summary statistics faces two distinct dependence
problems, and the literature has concentrated on the easier one. Correlation
among genes within a pathway is widely acknowledged and routinely modelled;
correlation among pathways, induced by the shared gene membership that is
intrinsic to how pathway databases are built, is usually handled with a
correction that assumes it away. We have added a Westfall--Young max-statistic
layer to CATFISH that controls the family-wise error rate in the strong sense
whatever that dependence, under the same correlation model used for
calibration, requires nothing to be estimated about it beyond
the gene--gene correlation already used for calibration, and yields as a
by-product a direct measure of how far Bonferroni is from correct for any given
database, provided it is read on calibrated statistics.

Two further findings matter beyond this particular method. Computing the omnibus
in log space is a correctness requirement at modern GWAS sample sizes, not an
optimisation, and the failure it prevents is silent. And self-contained pathway
testing saturates: in a GWAS of $1.3$ million individuals, $11.2\%$ of genes are
genome-wide significant, a random fifty-gene set rejects the self-contained null
with probability $0.997$, and strong family-wise error control leaves
$\nLipSigLDL$ of $\nLipNpw$ pathways significant. That is a property of the
hypothesis, not of the correction, and the ranking survives it: plasma
lipoprotein assembly, remodeling and clearance led the LDL-C ordering, the
lipoprotein pathways were extreme for their size in both LDL-C and HDL-C, and
the gene prioritization recovered established lipid genes better than GWAS
association alone. In a sorghum panel with far denser LD and little power, the
same procedure declared no pathway significant and still raised the
established lignin genes through the pathway layer, which is the behaviour a
calibrated method should show when the data are weak. CATFISH, and self-contained pathway testing generally, is best
suited to the low-signal polygenic regime, where the maize grain-nitrogen
analysis recovered L-homoserine biosynthesis as the leading pathway and a known
aspartate kinase isozyme as the leading gene out of $34{,}873$, in agreement
with independent prior work.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note S1: The five pathway signal archetypes}

This note expands the definitions of Section~3.1. Throughout, the
\emph{ranked profile} of a pathway is its gene-level $-\log_{10}p$ values sorted
from largest to smallest, and an archetype is a shape of that profile. The
archetypes are statistical templates: they are not inferred from real
pathways, a real pathway may combine features of several, and none of the
analyses in this paper assigns a real pathway to an archetype.

\subsubsection*{S1.1 Strong Sparse Signal (SSS)}

In an SSS pathway a small subset of genes carries most of the association
evidence and the remaining genes are close to null. The ranked profile shows one
or a few extremely small ordered $p$-values followed by a sharp drop to
null-like values. The pathway-level signal is therefore driven by a limited
number of high-evidence genes rather than by broad enrichment, and the
statistics that read the head of the profile, ACAT and minP, are expected to
detect it most efficiently. Statistics that weight all genes equally dilute
the leading genes with the null majority.

Biologically, SSS is expected when pathway behaviour is controlled by one or a
few bottleneck genes, such as a committed-step enzyme, a rate-limiting regulator
or an essential transporter
\citep{MorenoSanchez2008,Naz2023,Tanner2018,Song2020}. A representative example
is aspartokinase in the aspartate-derived amino-acid pathway, which produces
lysine, threonine, methionine and isoleucine from aspartate \citep{Galili2011}.
Aspartokinase catalyses the entry reaction, the phosphorylation of aspartate to
aspartyl phosphate \citep{Hasenoehrl2019}. Variation at this entry-point enzyme
can generate a strong localised signal while many downstream genes remain
buffered or near null. The leading candidate gene of the maize grain-nitrogen
analysis (Section~3.8) is an aspartate kinase isozyme, and the leading pathway,
L-homoserine biosynthesis, is the branch whose first step it catalyses.

\subsubsection*{S1.2 Single-Gene Proxy (SGP)}

In an SGP pathway the significance is driven primarily by one strongly
associated gene and the remaining genes are near null; the ranked profile is a
single extreme peak followed by an otherwise null-like distribution.
Statistically SGP is a boundary case of SSS, and minP and ACAT are again the
sensitive components. Its interpretation differs, however, because the pathway
result adds little beyond the dominant gene-level association.

Biologically, SGP arises when a pathway is effectively defined by one major
gene, especially in small or overlapping annotations. Phenylalanine metabolism
illustrates the pattern. \textit{PAH} encodes phenylalanine hydroxylase, the
rate-limiting enzyme for the conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine, and
pathogenic \textit{PAH} variation is the main cause of phenylketonuria and of
most PAH deficiency \citep{Flydal2013PAH,Elhawary2022PKU}. Broader
phenylalanine-metabolism pathways also contain genes for tetrahydrobiopterin
synthesis, recycling, chaperoning and related processes, but because of the
dominant role of \textit{PAH} a pathway-level result may largely reflect a
gene-centric signal rather than coordinated enrichment. Pathways that are
significant through minP and ACAT alone should be interpreted cautiously for
this reason, and Section~3.1 shows that this is also the regime in which the
omnibus loses the most power relative to its best component.

\subsubsection*{S1.3 Moderate Dense Signal (MDS)}

In an MDS pathway many genes show modest but consistent association and no
single gene dominates. The ranked profile is a broad shoulder of moderately
small $p$-values rather than a sharp peak, and the pathway-level evidence is the
combined contribution of many genes. Statistics that aggregate consistent
moderate evidence, Fisher's and Stouffer's methods, are expected to be most
sensitive, with soft TFisher also performing well when the enrichment is
concentrated in a moderately sized subset of the pathway.

Biologically, MDS is expected in pathways whose output depends on many
interacting components rather than one key effector. Cytokine-driven immune
signalling is a representative example. In TNF$\alpha$/IL-1$\beta$ to
NF-$\kappa$B signalling, pathway activity depends on receptor engagement,
adaptor recruitment, kinase cascades, feedback and downstream transcriptional
and post-transcriptional control
\citep{Jiang2021CytoSig,Megha2021Inflammation,Verstrepen2008NFkB,vanLoo2011NFkB,Cargnello2011MAPK}.
Genetic variation in such a pathway is more likely to cause many moderate
perturbations across the network than a single large-effect change.

\subsubsection*{S1.4 Weak Diffuse Enrichment (WDE)}

In a WDE pathway most genes are individually non-significant, yet the pathway
as a whole is shifted toward stronger association. The ranked profile shows a
gentle pathway-wide deflection rather than a peak or a shoulder, and the signal
is weak but widespread. Statistics that capture distributed enrichment across
many genes, particularly Stouffer's and Fisher's methods, are expected to be
most sensitive \citep{deLeeuw2015}; the extreme-tail statistics are close to
their null because no gene is extreme.

Biologically, WDE corresponds to highly polygenic architecture, in which
pathway-level signal arises from many small effects. Human height is the
classic example, influenced by thousands of variants with individually small
effects \citep{Guo2018HeightGWAS,Yengo2022HeightSaturation}. In that setting,
coherent pathways such as TGF-$\beta$ signalling, Hedgehog signalling and
growth-plate extracellular-matrix processes may be detectable mainly through
cumulative pathway-level enrichment rather than strong single-gene signals
\citep{LangoAllen2010Height,Guo2018HeightGWAS}.

\subsubsection*{S1.5 Mixed Signal Architecture (MSA)}

In an MSA pathway one or a few genes show strong association while a broader
set of genes provides moderate support. The ranked profile is a sharp leading
peak followed by a moderate shoulder, which places MSA between the sparse SSS
and the coordinated MDS regimes. Statistics that accommodate both the localised
strong signal and the distributed support are expected to be most sensitive,
particularly adaptive soft TFisher and Fisher's method; ACAT responds to the
peak. In the family-wise simulation of Section~3.1, Fisher was the most
powerful component for MSA at every pathway size, with soft TFisher close
behind.

Biologically, MSA is expected in pathways in which a few core regulatory or
structural genes with large, pathway-defining effects are embedded in a wider
network of auxiliary components. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol metabolism
illustrates the pattern. Rare, high-effect variants in \textit{LDLR},
\textit{APOB} and \textit{PCSK9} cause monogenic familial hypercholesterolaemia,
whereas population-level variation in LDL-C also reflects common alleles with
small to moderate effects in many additional lipid-modulating genes
\citep{Umlai2025LDLC,Peloso2022WGSbloodlipids}. GWAS of lipoprotein traits
further implicate genes of lipoprotein metabolism and remodelling, including
\textit{ABCA1}, \textit{LIPC}, \textit{CETP}, \textit{PLTP}, \textit{ANGPTL3}
and \textit{SORT1} \citep{Chasman2009LipoproteinProfiles}. Several of these are
among the established genes used to evaluate the candidate-gene score in
Section~3.9.

\subsubsection*{S1.6 How the archetypes were simulated}

The simulation of Section~3.1 (Section~2.6, item~6) uses the shared-universe
design: $3{,}000$ genes in independent blocks of $50$ with exchangeable
within-block correlation $\rho=0.3$, from the first $600$ of which a family of
$K=150$ pathways of $m\in\{5,20,50\}$ genes is drawn at random, so the pathways
overlap. One pathway in the family carries signal; the other $149$ are null. The
signal is a mean shift on the gene-level $z$ scale applied in layers, each layer
a fixed fraction of the pathway's genes shifted by a fixed amount
(Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}, last column): SSS shifts $15\%$ of genes by
$3.0$; SGP $5\%$ by $4.0$; MDS $60\%$ by $1.1$; WDE $90\%$ by $0.55$; and MSA
$10\%$ by $1.8$ and a further $60\%$ by $1.1$. A layer always contains at least
one gene, so at $m=5$ the sparse archetypes shift exactly one gene and MSA
shifts one gene strongly and three moderately. The shifted genes are chosen
afresh in every replicate, so the signal does not always fall on the same genes
or the same LD blocks. All shifts are multiplied by an effect scale in
$\{0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,2.5,3.0\}$, which takes every archetype from undetectable to
saturated at the pathway sizes considered.

The null bank, the five components, the soft TFisher grid, the undefined-component
rule and the omnibus are those of Section~2. For each archetype, size and effect
scale we record, for every component and for the omnibus, the fraction of
replicates in which the signal pathway clears that statistic's own
Westfall--Young family-wise threshold at $\alpha=0.05$. A cell is informative
when the most powerful component has power above $0.05$ and below $0.98$, so that
cells in which nothing or everything is detected do not count; the observed
winners of Table~\ref{tab:archverify} and the counts of first, second and lower
placings in Section~3.1 are taken over informative cells. Regret in a cell is the power of the best single
component minus the power of the omnibus.
