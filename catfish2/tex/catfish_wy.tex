\abstract{
\textbf{Motivation:} Pathway analysis of genome-wide association study (GWAS)
summary statistics must contend with two distinct sources of dependence. Genes
within a pathway are correlated through linkage disequilibrium (LD), and
pathways are correlated with one another through shared gene membership. Most
pipelines address only the first, calibrating each pathway against its own
LD-aware null and then applying a Bonferroni or Benjamini--Hochberg correction
that assumes the resulting pathway $p$-values are independent. They are not,
and the correction is consequently miscalibrated in a direction that depends on
how strongly the pathway family overlaps.\\
\textbf{Results:} We extend CATFISH, a correlation-aware omnibus pathway
framework combining ACAT, Fisher, adaptive soft TFisher, minP and Stouffer
statistics, with a Westfall--Young-style family-wise calibration. A single
genome-wide correlated null draw, generated from a multivariate normal model of
gene--gene correlation, is propagated through every pathway in the family; read
down its columns it gives each pathway a calibrated marginal $p$-value, and read
across its rows the minimum over pathways gives a family-wise threshold that
accounts for between-pathway dependence without modelling it. Numerical
precision is treated as a correctness requirement: in a GWAS of $n\approx1.3$
million individuals the omnibus statistic underflows double precision for the
majority of pathways, and the resulting ties silently destroy the ranking.
Across simulated null families spanning three LD regimes and three pathway
sizes, independence-based calibration was anti-conservative while per-pathway
and Westfall--Young calibration held type~I and family-wise error near nominal,
and both remained stable when up to four of the five component tests were
deliberately miscalibrated. Under five stylised signal architectures, the
component predicted to be sensitive to each was the best-performing component in
$\nArchCellsIn$ of $\nArchCellsTot$ architecture-by-size combinations. Applied to a maize
grain-nitrogen GWAS, the Westfall--Young ordering recovered L-homoserine
biosynthesis as the leading pathway and placed a known aspartate kinase isozyme
first among 34,873 genes. Applied to GLGC LDL- and HDL-cholesterol GWAS, it
exposed a regime in which the self-contained null is false almost everywhere:
11.2\% of genes reach genome-wide significance for LDL-C and essentially every
pathway is significant, so significance no longer discriminates. The ordering
still does: plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling and clearance ranked first
of $\nLipNpw$ Reactome pathways for both LDL-C and HDL-C, and the combined
gene score ranked \textit{LDLR} and \textit{ABCA1} first and recovered
established lipid genes better than ranking by GWAS association alone. In a
sorghum bioenergy panel of 330 lines, where gene--gene correlation is far
denser than in the human data, no pathway was significant for dry biomass, and
a phenotype-permutation null showed that centring the gene $z$-scores of each
GWAS calibrates every pathway, while the family-wise error stays above nominal
because the variant-level tests are heavy-tailed. We argue
that the saturation is a property of self-contained testing at very large $n$,
not a failure of multiple-testing correction.\\
\textbf{Availability:} CATFISH is an R package available at
\url{https://github.com/nirwan1265/CATFISH} under the MIT licence. All
simulation, analysis and figure code for this paper is in the
\texttt{catfish2/} directory of that repository.\\
\textbf{Contact:} \href{mailto:ntanduk@ncsu.edu}{ntanduk@ncsu.edu}\\
\textbf{Supplementary information:} Supplementary data are available at
\textit{Bioinformatics} online.}

\boxedtext{Key Messages}{
\begin{itemize}
\item Pathway $p$-values are correlated because pathways share genes;
Bonferroni and Benjamini--Hochberg corrections applied to them are therefore
miscalibrated, and the direction of the error depends on the overlap structure
of the particular pathway database used.
\item A Westfall--Young max-statistic null, built by propagating one
genome-wide correlated draw through the whole pathway family, controls the
family-wise error rate in the strong sense without assuming the pathways are
independent; its validity rests instead on the multivariate normal correlation
model used to generate the draw.
\item Implementing the omnibus in log space is a correctness requirement, not an
optimisation: at GWAS sample sizes above roughly one million the analytic
omnibus underflows double precision and the pathway ranking collapses into ties.
\item Self-contained pathway tests saturate at very large $n$. When 11\% of all
genes are genome-wide significant, a random 50-gene set contains several of
them, almost every pathway rejects, and exact FWER control does not restore
discrimination. Saturation is a property of the hypothesis being tested, not of
the correction applied to it.
\end{itemize}}

\section{1 Introduction}

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have transformed complex trait research
by identifying thousands of loci linked to disease, development, and
quantitative phenotypes. Yet most variants explain only a small fraction of
phenotypic variance and often fall in noncoding regions, limiting single-marker
mechanistic interpretation \citep{Manolio2009,Boyle2017}. Complex traits are
often highly polygenic, with relevant signals distributed across many
modest-effect loci rather than concentrated in a few genome-wide significant
variants \citep{Akula2025,Lappalainen2024}. Gene-set analysis, including
pathway-based approaches, is therefore a key post-GWAS strategy for translating
summary statistics into biological hypotheses. By aggregating evidence across
predefined groups of functionally related genes, pathway-based methods enhance
interpretability, reduce the multiple-testing burden, and reveal coordinated
pathway disruptions that single-locus analyses may miss
\citep{Mooney2015,deLeeuw2015}. This is especially valuable for polygenic
traits, where many genes may influence the same phenotype through shared
biological processes. In such settings, a candidate gene becomes more compelling
when locus- or gene-level association evidence is reinforced by pathway-level
enrichment from a biologically relevant process. Pathway support therefore
provides functional context for prioritization, although it is best interpreted
as complementary rather than definitive evidence because pathway annotations
remain incomplete and often overlapping.

A plethora of pathway analysis tools has been developed for GWAS. Classical
over-representation methods test whether predefined pathways are enriched among
significant genes, as implemented in DAVID, g:Profiler, and WebGestalt
\citep{Sherman2022,Kolberg2023,Liao2019}. Ranking-based methods such as Gene Set
Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) assess whether pathway genes are non-randomly
concentrated at the top or bottom of a ranked gene list
\citep{Subramanian2005}. GWAS-focused extensions, including MAGENTA, INRICH, and
MAGMA, add features such as SNP-to-gene mapping, pathway-level modeling, and
adjustment for linkage disequilibrium (LD) or gene size
\citep{Segre2010,Lee2012,deLeeuw2015}. Accounting for LD is essential in GWAS
pathway analysis because nearby variants are often correlated and therefore do
not provide independent evidence of association. If LD is ignored, clusters of
linked SNPs can be effectively counted multiple times, leading to inflated
gene-level aggregation and enrichment biases toward larger genes or regions with
dense marker coverage. Several widely used post-GWAS methods therefore
explicitly account for LD through model-based or reference-based dependence
estimates rather than assuming independence. VEGAS uses multivariate normal
simulation, Pascal uses LD from a reference population to compute gene and
pathway scores, and MAGMA models gene-level dependence through an approximated
correlation structure
\citep{Liu2010,deLeeuw2015,Lamparter2016,Watanabe2017}.

These methods address dependence \emph{within} a pathway. A second source of
dependence operates \emph{between} pathways and is far less often handled.
Curated pathway databases are built from overlapping annotations: a gene that
participates in several metabolic steps is a member of several pathways, and
hierarchical resources such as Reactome place parent and child pathways in the
same family. The pathway-level $p$-values returned by any method are therefore
correlated with one another, sometimes strongly. Nonetheless, the final
multiple-testing step in essentially every published pipeline is a Bonferroni or
Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment of those $p$-values, both of which are derived
under independence or under positive-regression dependence conditions that
overlapping gene sets do not generally satisfy. The resulting family-wise error
rate is not the nominal one, and the size of the discrepancy is a property of
the particular database rather than of the method, which makes it invisible to
the kind of simulation study that generates independent pathways.

The classical solution to exactly this problem is the Westfall--Young
max-statistic procedure \citep{Westfall1993}. Rather than correcting each
$p$-value in isolation, one generates a null realization of the \emph{entire}
family of tests, records the single most extreme statistic observed in that
realization, and repeats. The distribution of that maximum is the reference
distribution for the observed minimum, and the resulting threshold controls the
family-wise error rate under the complete null whatever the dependence structure
among the tests, because the dependence is inherited from the way the null
realizations are generated rather than assumed. In a GWAS pathway setting this means drawing
correlated gene-level null statistics genome-wide, propagating that single draw
through every pathway in the family, and recording the minimum pathway
$p$-value. Nothing about the between-pathway correlation needs to be estimated,
modelled, or even named.

Pathway tests also differ in the question they ask. A self-contained test asks
whether the genes of a pathway are associated with the trait at all; its null
hypothesis is that none of them is. A competitive test asks whether the genes of
a pathway are more strongly associated than genes outside it; its null
hypothesis is that pathway genes are no more associated than the rest of the
genome \citep{Goeman2007,deLeeuw2015}. The two can disagree: a pathway carried
by one strongly associated gene is significant under a self-contained test but
not under a competitive one, and in a highly polygenic GWAS almost every pathway
contains associated genes, so the self-contained null is false nearly
everywhere while a competitive test can still single out the pathways that
stand out from the genome. CATFISH provides both tests, each with family-wise
control, and a joint call for pathways significant under both.

Here we extend CATFISH (Combining \textbf{C}auchy combination test (ACAT),
\textbf{A}daptive \textbf{T}Fisher (soft) test, \textbf{F}isher's test,
m\textbf{I}n-P, and \textbf{S}touffer's method for \textbf{H}olistic pathway
analysis), a correlation-aware pathway analysis framework that integrates
multiple complementary gene-set statistics in a unified omnibus strategy, with
Westfall--Young family-wise calibration. Different pathway tests are sensitive
to different genetic architectures: some are best at detecting broad,
distributed enrichment, whereas others are more sensitive to sparse strong
signals or heterogeneous evidence. Instead of relying on a single summary
statistic, CATFISH evaluates each pathway with several complementary tests and
combines them in an omnibus framework. This improves robustness when the true
signal configuration is unknown while preserving the interpretability of the
component tests. Beyond pathway-level inference, CATFISH prioritizes candidate
genes by integrating locus-level GWAS signals, gene-level MAGMA results, and
pathway-level support.

This paper makes three contributions beyond the original CATFISH framework.
First, we add the Westfall--Young layer described above and characterize how it
behaves: the same correlated null, read down its columns, supplies each
pathway's marginal $p$-value, and read across its rows, the family-wise one. Second, we show that the entire statistic must be computed in log
space. This sounds like an implementation detail and is not: at the sample sizes
now routine in human GWAS consortia, the analytic omnibus $p$-value for a
typical pathway falls below the smallest representable double-precision number,
every such pathway is assigned the same floor value, and the ranking that the
whole analysis exists to produce is destroyed without any error being raised.
Third, we report a result we believe is general. In GLGC LDL- and
HDL-cholesterol GWAS of roughly 1.3 million individuals, the self-contained null
hypothesis is false for almost every gene set that could be constructed and
nearly every pathway is significant, so family-wise significance no longer
discriminates between pathways. We interpret this as saturation of the
self-contained hypothesis rather than as a defect of the correction. The
ordering of pathways, read against their size, still recovers lipoprotein
metabolism in both traits, and the gene-level prioritization recovers the
established genes of each trait better than GWAS association alone. We discuss
what saturation implies for the choice between self-contained and competitive
testing as GWAS sample sizes continue to grow.

CATFISH is implemented as an R package and is freely available at
\url{https://github.com/nirwan1265/CATFISH} under the MIT licence.

\subsection*{1.1 Pathway signal archetypes}

A pathway can be associated with a trait in very different ways. Its evidence
may come from one extreme gene, from a few strong genes, from many genes of
moderate effect, or from a faint shift shared by nearly all of its members, and
the component tests of CATFISH are sensitive to different parts of that range.
We formalise the range as five pathway signal archetypes: Strong Sparse Signal
(SSS), Single-Gene Proxy (SGP), Moderate Dense Signal (MDS), Weak Diffuse
Enrichment (WDE) and Mixed Signal Architecture (MSA). They are statistical
templates for interpreting test behaviour, not empirically learned classes of
real pathways, and they are the reason CATFISH combines complementary components
instead of choosing one in advance. Section~3.1 defines the archetypes, relates
each to a biological setting in which it is expected, and tests the predicted
correspondence between archetype and component under the family-wise decision
rule used throughout this paper. Supplementary Note~S1 gives the expanded
descriptions and the simulation design.

\section{2 Methods}

This section gives the essentials of the method and analyses. Derivations, implementation details, settings and scripts are in the Supplementary Methods, whose sections SM1--SM7 follow Sections~2.1--2.7.

\subsection*{2.1 Gene-level inputs and component tests}

For a pathway $S$ of $m=|S|$ genes, CATFISH takes the gene-level $p$-values $p_g$ and $z$-scores $Z_g$ ($g\in S$) computed by MAGMA \citep{deLeeuw2015}, which summarises the GWAS evidence of the variants assigned to a gene while accounting for LD, gene size and marker density, and also reports the LD-induced correlation between nearby genes. Each pathway is tested with five component tests (Table~\ref{tab:components}): ACAT \citep{Liu2019ACATV,Liu2020ACAT}, Fisher \citep{Fisher1932}, soft TFisher \citep{Zhang2020TFisher}, minP and Stouffer \citep{Stouffer1949}. Each is most sensitive to a different pattern of gene-level evidence, from a single strong gene (minP, ACAT) to a weak shift across most genes (Stouffer) (Section~3.1). Their $p$-values are combined into one omnibus statistic with an ACAT-type operator $\mathcal{O}(\cdot)$. Four components have no tuning. Soft TFisher keeps only genes below a truncation threshold $\tau$ and adapts over $\tau\in\{10^{-2},\,5\times10^{-2},\,5\times10^{-1}\}$, which spans sparse to diffuse signals; $\tau=1$ is Fisher's method and is carried as its own component. When no gene of a pathway falls below any threshold, soft TFisher is treated as undefined and the omnibus is formed from the other components, and its $p$-value is capped at $10^{-16}$, the smallest value it can resolve (Supplementary Methods SM1).

\subsection*{2.2 Two sources of dependence}

Pathway statistics are correlated at two levels. Within a pathway, the five components are functions of the same gene $p$-values, and those $p$-values are correlated through LD, so any analytic reference distribution that assumes independent genes is wrong for every component. Between pathways, databases assign genes to several sets, so a gene shared by several pathways moves them together, and Bonferroni or Benjamini--Hochberg corrections need not hold their nominal error rate. CATFISH handles both with one device: genome-wide null draws of correlated gene $z$-scores, each scored through every pathway at once. The construction assumes that null gene $z$-scores are approximately multivariate normal, that the gene pairs MAGMA reports approximate the gene--gene correlation adequately (unreported pairs are set to zero), and that the gene-level inputs are not driven by gene size or SNP density (Supplementary Methods SM2).

\subsection*{2.3 Calibration and family-wise control from a shared correlated null}

For each chromosome $c$, the gene--gene correlations reported by MAGMA form a correlation matrix $R_c$; genes on different chromosomes are independent, and matrices that are not positive definite are repaired by flooring their eigenvalues. Writing $R_c=U_c^{\top}U_c$, null replicate $b$ draws
\[
\mathbf{Z}^{(b)}_c = U_c^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m_c}),
\]
for every chromosome, giving one genome-wide set of null gene $z$-scores with the LD between genes but no association, and null $p$-values $p_g^{(b)}=1-\Phi(Z_g^{(b)})$. Every pathway $S_k$ of the family $\mathcal{F}=\{S_1,\dots,S_K\}$ is scored on every replicate, giving null omnibus values $Q^{(b)}_k=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S_k))$ for $b=1,\dots,B$ and the observed value $q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k$ from the real data. Two $p$-values are read from this one matrix. The per-pathway $p$-value compares a pathway with its own null values,
\[
\tilde p_k=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(Q^{(b)}_k\le q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k\right)}{B+1},
\]
and asks whether that pathway is enriched. The family-wise $p$-value compares it with the smallest value over all pathways in each replicate, $M^{(b)}=\min_k Q^{(b)}_k$,
\[
\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(M^{(b)}\le q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k\right)}{B+1},
\]
and asks whether evidence this strong would appear anywhere among the $K$ pathways by chance. This is the Westfall--Young procedure \citep{Westfall1993}. Because all pathways are scored on the same draws, the dependence created by shared genes is in the null without being modelled, and rejecting pathways with $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k\le\alpha$ controls the family-wise error rate in the strong sense (Supplementary Methods SM3). Applying $\mathcal{O}$ to the observed components with their analytic reference distributions gives the analytic omnibus, which assumes independent genes; we report it as a baseline. Because Fisher-type statistics grow with pathway size, the family minimum is taken on standardised values $z_k=(q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k-\mu_k)/\sigma_k$, where $\mu_k$ and $\sigma_k$ are the mean and standard deviation of pathway $k$'s null values; $z_k$ (written $z_{\mathrm{std}}$ in the Results) is also the statistic used to rank pathways.

\emph{Competitive test.} The test above is self-contained: its null hypothesis is that no gene in the pathway is associated. The competitive test asks instead whether a pathway's genes are more associated than genes elsewhere in the same GWAS. Its null replicates shift the observed gene scores along the genome, in chromosome order, by one random offset shared by all pathways. This keeps the LD between neighbouring genes and the genome-wide distribution of gene scores, including any polygenic signal; the two $p$-values are read from the null matrix as above. A pathway called by both tests at level $\alpha$ controls the family-wise error of the joint claim (an intersection--union test \citep{Berger1982}).

\subsection*{2.4 Numerical implementation in log space}

In large GWAS, gene $p$-values fall below the smallest positive double-precision number ($\approx2.2\times10^{-308}$); in the GLGC LDL-C data, $473$ chromosome~1 variants have a published $p$-value of exactly zero. Combining such genes makes pathway $p$-values underflow to zero, ties the strongest pathways and silently corrupts their ranking; in our first implementation, the top pathways were in alphabetical order. CATFISH therefore computes every component and the omnibus on $\log p$, with asymptotic forms for ACAT and minP in the extreme tail, so pathway $p$-values keep their order down to about $10^{-324}$ (Supplementary Methods SM4).

\subsection*{2.5 Candidate-gene prioritization}

CATFISH ranks genes by a score over three layers of evidence,
\[
\mathrm{Score}(g)=S_{\mathrm{GWAS}}(g)+S_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}(g)+0.5\,S_{\mathrm{PATH}}(g)+0.3\,B_{\mathrm{hub}}(g),\qquad S_\bullet(g)=-\log_{10}\!\left(r_\bullet(g)/N_\bullet\right),
\]
where $r_{\mathrm{GWAS}}$ is the gene's rank by its strongest variant, $r_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}$ its rank by its MAGMA $p$-value, $r_{\mathrm{PATH}}$ the rank of its best pathway by $z_k$, $N_\bullet$ the number of genes ranked in that layer, and $B_{\mathrm{hub}}(g)=\log_{10}(n_{\mathrm{path}}(g)+1)$ a small bonus for membership in several pathways. Layers enter by rank so that their different tail behaviours are comparable; a gene missing a layer scores zero for it. The pathway layer has half the weight of each association layer because pathway evidence is shared by every member gene and partly encodes prior knowledge. We fixed the weights in advance and then examined them over a grid in a simulation with planted causal genes and in the real data (Section~3.5; Supplementary Methods SM5). To measure what the pathway layer adds, genes were also ranked without it (pathway and hub weights $0$; GWAS+MAGMA). Because a gene's own association contributes to the evidence of its pathways, we also recomputed the pathway evidence of selected genes with each gene left out of its own pathways (leave one gene out), keeping every other gene unchanged. The score is a ranking, not an inferential model: it has no $p$-value.

\subsection*{2.6 Simulation design}

We evaluated calibration and power in two kinds of simulation; settings and scripts are in Supplementary Methods SM6. The first draws null gene $z$-scores directly. In the shared-universe design, $3{,}000$ genes in blocks of $50$ with within-block correlation $\rho$ stand for chromosomes and LD blocks, and $K=150$ pathways of $m$ genes are drawn from them, so that pathways overlap as in real databases. In the exchangeable design, all genes of a pathway are correlated at $\rho$, a stress test of within-pathway correlation. With $\rho\in\{0,0.3,0.6\}$ and $m\in\{5,20,50\}$, and thresholds fitted on one set of draws and evaluated on another, we ran six studies:
\begin{enumerate}
\item null calibration: per-pathway type~I error and family-wise error of the analytic omnibus, per-pathway calibration, Bonferroni and Westfall--Young under the global null (exchangeable design);
\item component breakage: up to four of the five components miscalibrated, by replacing their $p$-values with $p^{\gamma}$ ($\gamma=0.7$ or $1.4$);
\item leave one component out: each component removed in turn and the calibration recomputed;
\item per-component calibration: each component calibrated and used alone;
\item an incomplete correlation file: $0$--$100\%$ of gene pairs missing from the matrix used for calibration;
\item power: one pathway per family carrying one of the five signal types of Section~3.1 (Supplementary Note~S1), the other $149$ null.
\end{enumerate}
The five signal types describe how association can be spread over a pathway's genes, each with a biological example (Supplementary Note~S1). In a strong sparse signal (SSS), a few genes carry strong evidence, as when a committed-step enzyme such as aspartokinase controls a pathway's output. A single-gene proxy (SGP) is its limit, one dominant gene, as \textit{PAH} dominates phenylalanine metabolism. In a moderate dense signal (MDS), many genes are moderately associated, as in cytokine signalling to NF-$\kappa$B. In weak diffuse enrichment (WDE), almost every gene is shifted slightly, as in growth pathways for human height. A mixed signal (MSA) combines a few strong genes with many moderate ones, as \textit{LDLR}, \textit{APOB} and \textit{PCSK9} with many common lipid variants for LDL-C.

The second kind of simulation generated GWAS summary statistics on real LD, so that CATFISH and MAGMA gene-set analysis could be compared on identical data with known truth. SNP $z$-scores were drawn with the LD of the 1000 Genomes European panel ($503$ individuals; chromosomes 1--10; $748{,}822$ HapMap3 SNPs), and causal SNPs were placed in one target pathway following each signal type, on top of a polygenic background in which $2\%$ of genes carried one causal SNP. Each of the $850$ replicates ($17$ scenario--size combinations) was analysed with MAGMA (SNP-wise mean model; $9{,}645$ genes; $1{,}021$ Reactome pathways) and with CATFISH ($B=2{,}000$). Power was the fraction of replicates calling the target pathway, and a false call was a pathway with no gene within $1$~Mb of a causal SNP of the target.

\subsection*{2.7 Real-data analyses}

\textbf{Human lipids.} We used the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium LDL-C and HDL-C meta-analyses \citep{Graham2021GLGC} in five ancestry groups (European, $n\approx1.3$ million; African, East Asian, South Asian and Hispanic; Section~3.4), giving ten trait--ancestry sets. Gene analysis used MAGMA v1.10 (NCBI37.3 gene locations, $\pm10$~kb windows, SNP-wise mean model), run per chromosome and merged to keep the gene--gene correlations. Pathways were Reactome from MSigDB C2:CP ($1{,}390$ pathways after size filtering). We compared CATFISH with MAGMA's competitive and self-contained gene-set tests on the same gene statistics and pathways, with Bonferroni correction for MAGMA.

\textbf{Nine-trait benchmark.} To evaluate the gene score beyond lipids, we added seven European GWAS with GWAS Catalog harmonised summary statistics: coronary artery disease \citep{vanderHarst2018CAD}, breast cancer \citep{Michailidou2017BRCA}, Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis \citep{deLange2017IBD}, atrial fibrillation \citep{Nielsen2018AF}, systemic lupus erythematosus \citep{Bentham2015SLE} and asthma \citep{Valette2021Asthma}, analysed as the European lipid data. Known genes were each trait's Open Targets gold-standard genes from the 2019 and 2023 releases \citep{Mountjoy2021L2G}, curated independently of this study and used only for assessment. We compared six rankings: CATFISH, CATFISH without the pathway layer, the strongest variant, the MAGMA $p$-value, PoPS \citep{Weeks2023PoPS} run with default settings, and the mean of the CATFISH and PoPS ranks, counting gold-standard genes in the genome-wide top 20, 50 and 100. The main analysis excluded the extended MHC (chromosome~6, $25$--$34$~Mb), where long-range LD dominates the rankings of immune traits; results with all genes are also reported. For each trait, we compared the top 50 genes of the gene score with the top 50 by strongest variant, and we checked the literature for each of the ten highest-ranked genes, classifying it as a gene with published genetic or functional evidence, a gene at a reported locus whose causal gene is unresolved, or a gene whose locus has been resolved to a neighbour (Supplementary Methods SM7).

\textbf{Sorghum.} We analysed the sorghum Bioenergy Association Panel \citep{Brenton2016} ($363$ genotyped lines). Dry biomass ($327$ lines) and three stem-composition traits, Brix at maturity (stem-juice soluble solids), neutral detergent fibre (NDF; cell wall) and non-fibre carbohydrate (NFC; mainly sugars and starch), were tested with GEMMA's linear mixed model \citep{Zhou2012GEMMA}. Gene analysis used MAGMA (BTx623 v3 gene models, $\pm25$~kb windows, SNP-wise mean model), and pathways were SorghumBicolorCyc \citep{Hawkins2021CornCyc} ($5$--$200$ genes). To test calibration on real genotypes, we permuted dry biomass across lines $100$ times and ran every permutation through the full pipeline, so that every pathway was null. For the chromosome~4 locus shared by the stem-composition traits, we estimated the effect of the lead allele on each trait and asked how much of its effect on NDF is explained by the sugar--fibre trade-off, by regressing NDF on allele count with and without Brix as a covariate; the share explained is $1-b_{\mathrm{adj}}/b$ \citep{VanderWeele2016Mediation}. Leave one gene out was applied to every pathway-supported gene in each trait's top 100 (Supplementary Methods SM7).

\textbf{Gene-level genomic control.} In all real analyses, gene $p$-values were centred before pathway testing: each was converted to $z_g=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_g)$, the GWAS-wide median $\hat\delta$ was subtracted, and the result converted back. This location genomic control \citep{Devlin1999} uses one constant per GWAS and keeps gene order and gene--gene correlations. We added it because the permutation null showed GWAS-specific shifts of all gene $z$-scores that a self-contained test misreads as pathway signal (Section~3.6). Competitive tests were run alongside the self-contained tests on the same centred inputs ($B=10{,}000$ shifts).

\section{3 Results}

\subsection*{3.1 Five potential pathway signal types, the component tests that detect them, and an omnibus that performs well across all five}

CATFISH is a pathway analysis and gene prioritization method for GWAS summary statistics. It applies five component tests to each pathway (ACAT, Fisher, Stouffer, minP and soft TFisher), each most sensitive to a different pattern of gene-level evidence, and combines their five $p$-values into a single omnibus $p$-value with the Cauchy combination (Section~2.1). Computed from the analytic null of each component, which assumes that the genes of a pathway are independent, this is the analytic omnibus $p$-value. CATFISH instead calibrates the omnibus against correlated null draws: each draw is one simulated set of genome-wide gene $z$-scores that carries the LD correlation between genes but no association, and every pathway is scored on every draw. Comparing a pathway's observed omnibus with its own null values gives its per-pathway $p$-value; comparing it with the smallest value over all pathways in each draw gives its Westfall--Young family-wise $p$-value, which controls the probability of any false positive among all pathways tested (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a; Algorithm~\ref{alg:catfish}; Section~2.3). When a pathway is associated with a trait, its gene-level evidence can be distributed across its genes in different patterns, so we define five signal types by the ranked gene-level profile (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b; Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}; Supplementary Note~S1). Strong sparse signal (SSS) arises when pathway output is driven by a few key genes, such as a committed-step enzyme, a rate-limiting regulator or an essential transporter \citep{MorenoSanchez2008,Naz2023,Tanner2018,Song2020}; single-gene proxy (SGP) is the limiting case in which one gene carries the signal. Moderate dense signal (MDS) arises when output depends on many interacting genes, each with a modest effect: in cytokine signalling to NF-$\kappa$B, receptors, adaptor proteins, a kinase cascade and transcription factors each pass the signal on, so variation in any of them shifts the output a little \citep{Jiang2021CytoSig,Verstrepen2008NFkB}. Weak diffuse enrichment (WDE) matches highly polygenic traits such as height \citep{Yengo2022HeightSaturation}. Mixed signal architecture (MSA) combines a few large-effect genes with many modest ones, e.g., LDL-cholesterol metabolism (Section~3.4) \citep{Chasman2009LipoproteinProfiles,Peloso2022WGSbloodlipids}. Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b shows the expected ranked profile of a $50$-gene pathway of each type against the profile under no signal (dashed). Under SSS a few genes reach $-\log_{10}p$ of about $4$--$5$ and the rest follow the null; under SGP a single gene stands alone; under MDS a broad shoulder of moderately small $p$-values extends over half the pathway; under WDE the profile sits only slightly above the null along its whole length; and MSA combines a strong head with a moderate shoulder. These five types are templates, however: real pathways may be intermediate, and their type is unknown a priori.

Because each component test is built for a particular pattern of gene-level evidence, we predicted which components should detect each signal type best (Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}) and tested these predictions by simulation. We embedded one non-null pathway of each type among $149$ correlated null pathways, varied effect size from undetectable to saturated, and measured family-wise power, the fraction of replicates in which the signal pathway reached a family-wise $p\le0.05$ (Section~2.6, item~6). In $\nArchCellsIn$ of $\nArchCellsTot$ type-by-size cells, the top-powered component matched the prediction (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}c; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:archverify}). Diffuse enrichment favoured Stouffer (extreme-value tests near null); sparse and single-gene signals favoured minP and ACAT (Fisher and Stouffer nearly powerless); and dense or mixed signals favoured Fisher, with soft TFisher close behind. No component dominated across settings: among $\nArchInfCells$ informative cells, Fisher ranked first $\nFisherFirst$ times but fourth or worse $\nFisherFourthPlus$ times; minP finished last $\nMinPLast$ times; and soft TFisher won $\nTFFirst$ cell but never placed below third.

With no dominant component and the type unknown, CATFISH reports all five components and combines them into the omnibus. Figure~\ref{fig:overview}c shows family-wise power against effect scale for each component (coloured lines) and the omnibus (dashed line), by signal type (columns) and pathway size (rows). The components separate most clearly at intermediate effects. With $20$ genes and an effect scale of $1.5$, Fisher, soft TFisher and Stouffer detected the dense pathway in $74\%$, $72\%$ and $66\%$ of replicates, but ACAT and minP in only $24\%$ and $21\%$; for the single-gene pathway the order reversed, with minP and ACAT at $99\%$ and Stouffer at $1\%$; and for the diffuse pathway with $50$ genes, Stouffer ($70\%$) and Fisher ($66\%$) led while ACAT and minP stayed below $10\%$. The omnibus followed the best component in each case rather than the average of the five, reaching $73\%$, $92\%$ and $68\%$ in the same three settings, and its power rose with effect size and pathway size at the same rate as the best component. It fell behind mainly at small effects in the sparse types, most for single-gene signal: with $20$ genes and an effect scale of $1.0$, minP detected the pathway in $62\%$ of replicates and the omnibus in $26\%$, because only minP and ACAT carry the evidence and the other three components dilute it. We quantify the cost of combining as \emph{regret}: the power gap between the best component and the omnibus within a cell. Mean regret ranged from $\nArchRegretBest$ ($\nArchRegretBestA$) to $\nArchRegretWorst$ ($\nArchRegretWorstA$) (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}d; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:archregret}); the bars show mean regret, below $0.03$ for four of the five types, and the crosses the worst single cell. In the same cells the gap between the best and worst component often exceeded $0.5$. Preselecting a component can incur the full gap; the omnibus incurs only the regret. An exception is the single-gene type, where the omnibus lost up to $\nArchMaxRegret$ in one cell: only minP and ACAT detect such pathways, and averaging dilutes them. This reflects the combination, not any component, so such pathways are better treated as gene-level results. Thus, the five components together cover the range of signal types, and the omnibus stays close to the best of them without the signal type being known in advance, losing appreciable power only when a single gene carries the pathway.

%% ---- algorithm ----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{CATFISH self-contained test: per-pathway and family-wise $p$-values from one set of correlated null draws (Section~2.3).}
\label{alg:catfish}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require gene $p$-values $p_g$ from MAGMA for $G$ genes; MAGMA gene--gene correlations $r_{ij}$; pathways $\mathcal{F}=\{S_1,\dots,S_K\}$; number of null draws $B$; omnibus $\mathcal{O}$, the ACAT combination of ACAT, Fisher, Stouffer, minP and soft TFisher (truncation grid fixed in advance)
\Ensure per-pathway $p$-value $\tilde p_k$ and family-wise $p$-value $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k$ for every pathway
\State $z_g \gets \Phi^{-1}(1-p_g)$ computed from $\log p_g$; $\;p_g \gets 1-\Phi\{z_g-\mathrm{median}(z)\}$ \Comment{gene-level genomic control}
\For{each chromosome $c$}
  \State $R_c \gets I + \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}_c} r_{ij}\,(e_ie_j^{\top}+e_je_i^{\top})$; floor eigenvalues at $10^{-6}$ if not positive definite
  \State $R_c = U_c^{\top}U_c$ \Comment{Cholesky factor}
\EndFor
\For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
  \State $q_k \gets \mathcal{O}\{\mathbf{p}(S_k)\}$ \Comment{observed omnibus, in log space}
\EndFor
\For{$b=1,\dots,B$}
  \State $\mathbf{Z}^{(b)}_c \gets U_c^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_c$, $\;\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_c\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, independently for each chromosome \Comment{one genome-wide draw}\label{alg:draw}
  \State $p^{(b)}_g \gets 1-\Phi(Z^{(b)}_g)$ for every gene\label{alg:nullp}
  \For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
    \State $Q^{(b)}_k \gets \mathcal{O}\{\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S_k)\}$ \Comment{every pathway scored on the same draw}
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
  \State $\tilde p_k \gets \bigl\{1+\sum_{b}\mathbf{1}(Q^{(b)}_k\le q_k)\bigr\}/(B+1)$ \Comment{per-pathway $p$-value}
  \State $\mu_k,\sigma_k \gets$ mean and SD of $Q^{(1)}_k,\dots,Q^{(B)}_k$; $\;z_k \gets (q_k-\mu_k)/\sigma_k$; $\;\zeta^{(b)}_k \gets (Q^{(b)}_k-\mu_k)/\sigma_k$
\EndFor
\For{$b=1,\dots,B$}
  \State $M^{(b)} \gets \min_k \zeta^{(b)}_k$ \Comment{family minimum of one draw}
\EndFor
\For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
  \State $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k \gets \bigl\{1+\sum_{b}\mathbf{1}(M^{(b)}\le z_k)\bigr\}/(B+1)$ \Comment{Westfall--Young; ties count as extreme}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\tilde p_k$, $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k$ and $z_k$ (the pathway statistic $z_{\mathrm{std}}$ used by the gene score)
\end{algorithmic}
\vspace{2pt}
\footnotesize Competitive test: lines~\ref{alg:draw}--\ref{alg:nullp} are replaced by one circular shift $s$ of the observed gene $p$-values along the genome order, shared by all pathways ($p^{(b)}_g \gets p_{g+s}$); everything else is unchanged. A pathway is called by both tests when $\max(\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY,self}}_k,\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY,comp}}_k)\le\alpha$.
\end{algorithm}

\subsection*{3.2 CATFISH controls false positives under linkage disequilibrium and detects sparse pathway signals more often than MAGMA}

We first asked whether CATFISH keeps false positives at the nominal rate when the genes of a pathway are correlated, the setting in which the analytic omnibus $p$-value, which assumes independent genes, should fail. To test only this assumption, we simulated independent pathways whose $m$ genes had within-pathway correlation $\rho\in\{0,0.3,0.6\}$ with $m\in\{5,20,50\}$, using $500$ families per setting (Section~2.6, item~1). We compared two per-pathway $p$-values, the analytic $p$-value and the CATFISH per-pathway $p$-value (the same omnibus calibrated against correlated null draws), and two family-wise $p$-values, Bonferroni applied to the analytic $p$-value (the analytic $p$-value multiplied by the number of pathways tested, the usual correction in pathway analysis) and the CATFISH family-wise $p$-value, with the two CATFISH $p$-values read from the same null draws (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}a; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:null_calibration}). The analytic type I error was $\nTAnInd$ at $\rho=0$, $m=5$ (near $0.05$) but increased with $\rho$ and $m$ to $\nTAnStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$, consistent with correlated genes providing less independent information than assumed. In Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}a, the analytic curve climbs with $\rho$ in every panel, more steeply in larger pathways, whereas both CATFISH curves stay flat near the dashed $0.05$ line. CATFISH per-pathway error stayed within $[\nTMvnMin,\nTMvnMax]$ across settings; at $\alpha=0.01$, the analytic rate reached $\nTAnMaxOne$ versus $\nTMvnMaxOne$. The family-wise $p$-value was evaluated out of sample (estimating the null of the family minimum on half the families and applying it to the rest): family-wise error ranged from $\nTWyMin$ to $\nTWyMax$, within Monte Carlo error for $250$ held-out families (SE $\approx 0.014$). Bonferroni inherited the analytic miscalibration, reaching $\nTBonfMod$ at $\rho=0.3$, $m=20$ and $\nTBonfStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$; with independent pathways, the inflation is driven by the per-pathway statistic.

In GWAS, inflation is usually checked with the genomic-control inflation factor $\lambda$, the median of the observed $\chi^2_1$ statistics divided by its expected value, which is close to one for a calibrated test. For the analytic omnibus this check pointed the wrong way: $\lambda$ dropped from $\nLamAnInd$ at $\rho=0$, $m=50$ to $\nLamAnStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$—the case with the largest type I error (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}b). Because $\lambda$ depends on the median null $p$-value while type I error depends on the lower tail, strong within-pathway correlation can increase lower-tail mass even as the median rises above one half. For CATFISH per-pathway $p$-values, $\lambda\in[\nLamMvnMin,\nLamMvnMax]$. In Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}b, the analytic bars (black) fall further below one as LD and pathway size grow, to almost zero in strong LD with $50$ genes, while the CATFISH bars (white) stay near one in every setting. Thus $\lambda\le 1$ does not imply a calibrated tail.

These simulations draw gene-level statistics directly from a correlation model. To test CATFISH on GWAS data with real linkage disequilibrium and to compare it with existing methods, we next simulated GWAS summary statistics from real genotypes with known enriched pathways, and analysed each replicate with CATFISH and MAGMA gene-set analysis (Section~2.6; 50 replicates per setting). Both methods were run as a self-contained and a competitive test, and CATFISH also as the joint call of both (Section~1). With no signal, CATFISH’s self-contained test had 0/50 false calls, vs 8/50 for MAGMA (FWER $0.16$, 95\% CI $0.07$--$0.29$; Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}c; Table~\ref{tab:sim_compare}). Both competitive tests erred in $0.06$ of replicates; the joint call erred in none. With only a polygenic background (where competitive tests should not call), CATFISH’s competitive test and joint call erred in 4/50 ($0.08$, 95\% CI $0.02$--$0.19$), while MAGMA’s did not. In Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}c, which shows each estimate with its $95\%$ interval, MAGMA's self-contained test is the only one whose interval lies above $0.05$.

Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}d,e compares power by signal type in small, medium and large pathways. For the self-contained test (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}d), the gap between CATFISH (black) and MAGMA (grey) was largest for the sparse types in small and medium pathways, and both methods approached full power for most types in large pathways. CATFISH outperformed MAGMA when signal was sparse: it detected strong sparse pathways in $98\%$ of replicates vs $34\%$ for MAGMA, and single-gene pathways in $92$--$100\%$ vs $0\%$, $18\%$, and $78\%$. Performance was similar for dense and mixed signal, while MAGMA was slightly better for weak diffuse signal at medium size ($76\%$ vs $68\%$).

Competitive power was lower than self-contained power for both methods, most in small pathways, because a competitive test must also beat the rest of the genome, but the comparison showed the same pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}e): CATFISH detected strong sparse pathways in $66\%$, $76\%$, and $100\%$ (small/medium/large) vs $4\%$, $12\%$, and $36\%$ for MAGMA. For dense, diffuse, and mixed signal the methods were similar, with MAGMA better for small pathways (MDS $36\%$ vs $20\%$, MSA $50\%$ vs $42\%$). As expected, neither competitive test detected single-gene pathways in more than $4\%$ of replicates, since one gene does not distinguish a pathway from the genome; this is instead addressed by the self-contained test. Thus, CATFISH kept false positives at or near the nominal rate in every null we simulated, matched MAGMA for dense, diffuse and mixed signals and was much more powerful for sparse ones.

\subsection*{3.3 Calibration holds when components are corrupted or removed, and degrades predictably with an incomplete correlation file}

A $p$-value is calibrated when it means what it says: under the null, $p\le0.05$ should occur in $5\%$ of tests. CATFISH calibrates its $p$-values by comparing each observed statistic with the same statistic on null draws (Section~3.1), and here we asked how robust that calibration is. An omnibus helps only if no misbehaving component can dominate it; likewise, family-wise control helps only if this robustness holds at the family level. We tested both in a second simulation in which pathways overlap, as in real databases: $3{,}000$ genes in independent LD blocks of $50$, with correlation $\rho$ within blocks, from which $150$ pathways of $m$ genes were drawn at random, so that each gene belongs to $\nBrkMembership$ pathways on average and one null draw moves every pathway that contains a given gene (Section~2.6). In each setting, one set of null draws calibrated CATFISH and a separate set of null datasets, with no associated genes, measured its error rates.

First, we asked whether a misbehaving component can break the omnibus. We corrupted $k$ of the five component $p$-values, in both the null draws and the evaluated datasets, by replacing $p$ with $p^{\gamma}$: anti-conservative at $\gamma=1.4$ and conservative at $\gamma=0.7$ (Figure~\ref{fig:stress}a,b; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:breakage}). With four anti-conservatively corrupted components, the analytic omnibus increased from $\nBrkAnKzero$ to $\nBrkAnKfour$ and Bonferroni from $\nBrkBonfKzero$ to $\nBrkBonfKfour$, while CATFISH per-pathway and family-wise rates remained $\nBrkMvnKfour$ and $\nBrkWyKfour$. In Figure~\ref{fig:stress}a, the analytic and Bonferroni lines climb with each additional corrupted component under anti-conservative corruption (top row), most steeply in strong LD, while both CATFISH lines stay flat at $0.05$. Conservative corruption reduced power, moving the analytic omnibus to $\nBrkConsAnKfour$, with CATFISH at $\nBrkConsMvnKfour$ and $\nBrkConsWyKfour$. Across settings, the family-wise rate stayed within $[\nBrkWyRangeLo,\nBrkWyRangeHi]$ and the per-pathway rate within $[\nBrkMvnRangeLo,\nBrkMvnRangeHi]$, and the CATFISH per-pathway $\lambda$ stayed near one while the analytic $\lambda$ drifted in the direction of the corruption (Figure~\ref{fig:stress}b). Because the null draws were corrupted in the same way as the data, a uniformly miscalibrated component shifts the null and the observed statistic together and cancels; data-dependent errors would not cancel, and we did not test that case.

Next, we asked whether the omnibus depends on any single component. Leaving out each component in turn and recomputing the null draws and thresholds kept per-pathway type I error within $[\nLooMvnMin,\nLooMvnMax]$ (full omnibus $\nLooFullMvn$) and family-wise error within $[\nLooWyMin,\nLooWyMax]$ (full $\nLooFullWy$; Figure~\ref{fig:stress}c; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:loo}), and the per-pathway $\lambda$ stayed near one. In Figure~\ref{fig:stress}c, the bars for each removed component match the full omnibus (None) in the three CATFISH panels, whereas the Bonferroni panel, on a different scale, rises with LD. Averaged over sizes and variants, the CATFISH family-wise (Westfall--Young) rate stayed nominal across LD ($\nLooWyInd$, $\nLooWyMod$, $\nLooWyStr$), whereas Bonferroni increased from $\nLooBonfInd$ to $\nLooBonfMod$ to $\nLooBonfStr$ because it corrects an analytic $p$-value that is anti-conservative under LD (Section~3.2). Dropping a component affects power, not validity, so users can omit an untrusted component at a cost in sensitivity to its signal type; we run all five by default.

We then asked which components LD inflates. Calibrating components separately showed LD inflation is concentrated in two (Figure~\ref{fig:stress}d; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:components_cal}). Under the independent null all were correctly sized; under strong LD, Fisher ($\nCmpFisherStr$) and Stouffer ($\nCmpStoufferStr$) were inflated, while ACAT ($\nCmpACATStr$) and minP ($\nCmpminPStr$) changed little, and the analytic omnibus was inflated as well ($\nCmpOmnibusStr$). Aggregation methods treat correlated genes as repeated evidence; extreme-value methods depend mainly on the top gene and discount near-duplicates. Calibrating to the correlated null removed inflation for all components (Fisher $\nCmpFisherCal$, Stouffer $\nCmpStoufferCal$, omnibus $\nCmpOmnibusCal$). In Figure~\ref{fig:stress}d, the Fisher and Stouffer bars in the analytic panel rise far above $0.05$ as LD strengthens while ACAT and minP stay near it, and in the calibrated panel every bar sits near $0.05$. The analytic $\lambda$ panel shows again that $\lambda$ is a poor guide: soft TFisher has $\lambda$ near two even under independence, because its uncalibrated statistic is a minimum over thresholds rather than a $p$-value (Section~2.1). When soft TFisher is undefined for a pathway (no gene passes any truncation threshold), CATFISH leaves it out of that pathway's omnibus, in the observed data and in every null draw, rather than setting it to $p=1$, since ACAT treats $p=1$ as evidence against the pathway, lowering $\lambda$ below one while inflating the tail.

Finally, CATFISH depends on the gene--gene correlation file. The correlation matrix uses MAGMA-reported gene pairs and sets unreported pairs to zero, so how complete it is depends on the LD reference panel and on the variants available. We simulated data with the true correlations and calibrated using matrices where a fraction of pairs was zeroed (Figure~\ref{fig:stress}e,f; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:missing}). Under moderate LD, the complete matrix gave per-pathway type I error $\nMissMvnZero$ and family-wise error $\nMissWyZero$; removing half the pairs gave $\nMissMvnHalf$ and $\nMissWyHalf$; removing all gave $\nMissMvnFull$ and $\nMissWyFull$. In Figure~\ref{fig:stress}e, both CATFISH lines start at $0.05$ with the complete file and rise steadily as pairs are removed, the family-wise line faster, while the analytic and Bonferroni lines are flat because they do not use the correlation file. The family-wise rate was more sensitive to missingness ($\nMissRatioWyHalf\times$ nominal at $50\%$ missing vs. $\nMissRatioMvnHalf\times$ for per-pathway), since the family minimum is a tail statistic, but it stayed below Bonferroni ($\nMissBonfMod$, $\nMissBonfStr$) up to $\nMissCrossover\%$ missing. Check the fraction of nearby within-pathway gene pairs with reported correlations. Across the sweep, $\lambda$ ranged from $\nMissLamLo$ to $\nMissLamHi$ and decreased below 1 as pairs were removed, a second case where $\lambda\le1$ coincided with an inflated tail. Thus, CATFISH's calibration held when components were corrupted or removed, and it degraded gradually with an incomplete correlation file, remaining better than Bonferroni until most correlations were missing.

\subsection*{3.4 In human GWAS, CATFISH ranks the expected pathways first across ancestry groups and traits}

Simulations fix the truth but simplify the genome. We next applied CATFISH to real GWAS of two blood lipid traits, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). LDL particles carry cholesterol from the liver to the tissues, and high LDL-C is a major cause of coronary artery disease; HDL particles carry cholesterol from the tissues back to the liver (reverse cholesterol transport). Both traits are highly heritable and polygenic, and the genes of lipoprotein metabolism are among the best characterised in human genetics, so these traits serve as a positive control: a pathway method should recover lipoprotein pathways and known lipid genes. LD in humans also extends over shorter distances than in the sorghum panel of Section~3.6 (on average $\nLipNeighbours$ correlated neighbours per gene in MAGMA's gene--gene correlation, against $\nSorNeighbours$ in sorghum). We used the GLGC 2021 meta-analyses \citep{Graham2021GLGC} in five ancestry groups: European (EUR, $n\approx1.32$ million), African (AFR, $n\approx99{,}400$), East Asian (EAS, $n\approx146{,}500$), South Asian (SAS, $n\approx41{,}000$) and Hispanic (HIS, $n\approx48{,}100$). The ten trait--ancestry sets are independent meta-analyses with distinct allele frequencies and LD, processed identically ($18{,}429$--$18{,}563$ genes, $1{,}390$ Reactome pathways, $B=10{,}000$, gene-level genomic control; Section~2.7).

The European GWAS are so large that most genes carry some association signal, a state we call saturation. In Europeans, $44.1\%$ of genes had $p<0.05$ for LDL-C and $11.2\%$ were genome-wide significant ($52.7\%$ and $14.8\%$ for HDL-C). The gene-level Manhattan plots show genome-wide significant genes on every chromosome, with the strongest at \textit{PCSK9}, \textit{APOB}, \textit{LPA}, \textit{LDLR} and \textit{PVRL2} for LDL-C and at \textit{ABCA1}, \textit{LIPC}, \textit{LPL}, \textit{SCARB1} and \textit{GALNT2} for HDL-C (Figure~\ref{fig:lipids}a,b). A random 50-gene set therefore contains on average $5.6$ and $7.4$ genome-wide significant genes, so almost every pathway is significant under the self-contained test: $\nLipSigLDL$ (LDL-C) and $\nLipSigHDL$ (HDL-C) of $\nLipNpw$ pathways had a family-wise $p\le0.05$. Significance alone no longer separates pathways, and the ranking has to. Larger pathways also tended to rank higher, because a large set collects more associated genes (size--evidence Spearman $\nLipSizeRhoLDL$ and $\nLipSizeRhoHDL$; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:density}): the top 20 pathways had median sizes of $\nLipTopTwentyMedSizeLDL$ (LDL-C) and $\nLipTopTwentyMedSizeHDL$ (HDL-C) genes, against $\nLipMedSizeAll$ for all pathways. We therefore checked whether the lipid pathways rank high only because of their size; they do not (below).

Despite saturation, the ranking recovered lipid biology. Figure~\ref{fig:lipids}c,d shows, for each trait, the five highest-ranked pathways of each ancestry group under the self-contained test, with each pathway's rank in all five groups; rows are ordered by their geometric-mean rank across the groups, which keeps one poor rank from dominating. Plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling and clearance, the Reactome pathway containing the LDL receptor, the apolipoproteins and the lipases, ranked first of $1{,}390$ in all ten sets, despite up to $32$-fold differences in sample size. The other rows fall into two groups. Lipid pathways, in bold, are the plasma-lipoprotein pathways of Reactome: the parent pathway and its sub-pathways (plasma lipoprotein clearance, remodeling and assembly, LDL clearance, HDL remodeling and chylomicron remodeling), which we expected to lead before the analysis. Real GWAS come with no complete list of truly associated pathways, so these pathways are the expected answer rather than a truth set. They ranked in the top ten in most groups. Large generic pathways ranked in the top five only in Europeans: interferon-$\gamma$ signalling ranked second for LDL-C in Europeans but $51$--$314$ in the other groups, and SUMOylation third for HDL-C in Europeans but $31$--$178$ elsewhere, as expected if their European ranks reflect saturation rather than lipid biology. In each non-European set, five or six of the nine plasma-lipoprotein subtree pathways were in the top ten, followed by two LXR-mediated pathways regulating \textit{ABCA1}. To test whether size explains these ranks, Figure~\ref{fig:lipids}e,f plots each pathway's evidence ($z_{\mathrm{std}}$; lower is stronger) against its size in the European data. Evidence strengthens steadily with size (loess line), which is the size effect above, but the lipid pathways (black) lie far below the line. The parent pathway, with $74$ genes, had $z_{\mathrm{std}}=-385$ for LDL-C and $-425$ for HDL-C, against a median of $-55$ and $-61$ for the $161$ pathways within a $1.25$-fold size window and $-170$ and $-236$ for the next strongest of them. The smaller lipoprotein sets ranked behind large generic sets overall but were also extreme for their size: within a 1.25-fold size window, their median percentile was $\nLipFamPctMedLDL\%$ (LDL-C) and $\nLipFamPctMedHDL\%$ (HDL-C), versus $\nLipAllPctMedLipoFreeLDL\%$ and $\nLipAllPctMedLipoFreeHDL\%$ for other pathways (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:lipid_pathways}). The lipid pathways therefore rank high because their genes are unusually associated for the size of the pathway, not because the pathways are large; the competitive test, which removes the size effect, agrees (below). The same pathway ranked first in both saturated and sparse genomes across five populations.

The two traits implicate this pathway via different genes. Their gene-level $Z$ statistics were only weakly correlated ($\nLipGeneRho$) and shared 13 of the top 100 genes (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:supp_lipid}c). Within the pathway, LDL-C mapped to receptor-mediated uptake (\textit{LDLR}, \textit{PCSK9}, \textit{LDLRAP1}, \textit{APOB}, \textit{APOE}) and HDL-C to reverse transport/lipolysis (\textit{ABCA1}, \textit{LIPC}, \textit{SCARB1}, \textit{LPL}, \textit{CETP}); 4 of the 12 leading genes overlapped (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:supp_lipid}d). At the pathway level the traits agree more (Figure~\ref{fig:lipids}g): pathway ranks for LDL-C and HDL-C were correlated ($\nLipCrossRho$), with the parent lipoprotein pathway at rank one for both and the other lipid pathways close to the diagonal. Much of the agreement away from the top reflects the size trend the two traits share, so the stronger evidence for convergence is that two GWAS led by largely different genes reach the same top pathway.

The ranks in Figure~\ref{fig:lipids}c--g come from CATFISH's self-contained test. Because real GWAS have no list of truly associated pathways, the comparison of methods against known truth is the simulation of Section~3.2, in which CATFISH controlled false positives better than MAGMA and detected sparse pathways more often than MAGMA. Here we compared how CATFISH and MAGMA's gene-set analysis rank pathways in the real data, with the self-contained and competitive tests of both methods run on the same gene statistics and pathways. MAGMA's self-contained test called even more pathways in Europeans ($1{,}358$ and $1{,}383$; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:magma_compare}), indicating that saturation reflects the self-contained hypothesis, not CATFISH. Self-contained rankings were similar (Spearman $0.70$--$0.94$), but MAGMA was more size-driven ($0.85$ and $0.86$ vs $0.72$ and $0.73$ for CATFISH) and placed the lipoprotein pathway 1st in six sets and 2nd in four. Competitive tests did not saturate: CATFISH called $3$--$14$ pathways per set and MAGMA $2$--$12$, with little size dependence (Spearman $-0.13$ to $0.22$; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:competitive_real}); all CATFISH competitive calls were also self-contained. CATFISH's competitive test ranked the parent lipoprotein pathway first in six sets and second or third in the South Asian and Hispanic sets, where a sub-pathway ranked first (plasma lipoprotein clearance for LDL-C, plasma lipoprotein remodeling for HDL-C). In Europeans, competitive calls were mainly lipid pathways: for LDL-C, six sets in the plasma-lipoprotein subtree, both LXR-regulated sets, and interferon-$\gamma$ signalling. Conceptually, self-contained tests ask whether a pathway has any signal, whereas competitive tests ask which pathways stand out genome-wide. For candidate genes, the choice had little impact: ranking by the competitive or joint statistic shifted the median rank of the gold-standard genes (Section~3.5) by at most six places in any set and changed the number in the top 20 by at most one. Thus, the pathway ranking does not depend on the test: the self-contained test, saturated in Europeans, and the competitive test, which is not, point to the same lipoprotein pathways and give the same candidate genes.

To check that these results are not specific to lipids, we ran the same pipeline on seven more European GWAS with harmonised summary statistics from the GWAS Catalog (Section~2.7): coronary artery disease (CAD), breast cancer, atrial fibrillation (AF), Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis (UC), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and asthma. In each, the leading pathways matched the known biology of the trait: plasma lipoprotein metabolism for CAD, SUMOylation and homology-directed repair for breast cancer, muscle contraction and cardiac conduction for AF, interferon-$\gamma$ signalling for Crohn's disease, UC and SLE, and T-cell differentiation and interleukin-1 family signalling for asthma. Thus, across two lipid traits in five ancestry groups and seven further traits, CATFISH placed the expected biology at the top of the pathway ranking, and for the lipids it did so more consistently and with less dependence on pathway size than MAGMA.

\subsection*{3.5 Pathway evidence improves candidate genes within loci and across nine human health traits}

We next turned from pathways to genes. Our central hypothesis is that a candidate gene is more credible when it lies in a pathway that is itself associated with the trait. CATFISH therefore ranks genes by a score that adds three layers of evidence, each entered as a rank,
\[
\mathrm{Score}(g)=S_{\mathrm{GWAS}}(g)+S_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}(g)+0.5\,S_{\mathrm{PATH}}(g)+0.3\,B_{\mathrm{hub}}(g),\qquad S_\bullet(g)=-\log_{10}\{r_\bullet(g)/N_\bullet\},
\]
where $r_{\mathrm{GWAS}}$ is the gene's rank by its strongest variant, $r_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}$ its rank by its MAGMA $p$-value, $r_{\mathrm{PATH}}$ the rank of its strongest pathway by $z_{\mathrm{std}}$, $N_\bullet$ the number of genes ranked in that layer, and $B_{\mathrm{hub}}$ a small bonus for genes in several pathways (Section~2.5). To measure what the pathway layer adds, we compared this score with the same score without it ($0$ weight on the pathway layer and the bonus), which ranks genes by GWAS and MAGMA evidence alone. The pathway layer has half the weight of each association layer ($0.5$) and the bonus $0.3$, because pathway evidence is shared by every member gene and partly encodes prior knowledge, so it should support rather than outweigh a gene's own association. We fixed these weights in advance and then tested them over a grid (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:weights}). In a simulation with causal genes concentrated in five of $50$ pathways, the fraction of causal genes in the top twenty rose from $0.71$ without the pathway layer to $0.80$ at the default weights and $0.85$ at the largest pathway weight, and every ranking on the grid correlated at least $0.986$ with the default ranking (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:weights}a,b). In the European lipid data, the median rank of the gold-standard genes (below) improved with the pathway weight in the same way, from $55$ without pathway evidence to $37$ at the defaults and $26$--$27$ at the largest pathway weight for LDL-C, and from $122$ to $41$ and $35$--$36$ for HDL-C (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:weights}c,d). Adding pathway evidence therefore helps, the exact weights matter little, and the defaults are a conservative choice rather than the best value in either test.

To evaluate the score, we used the Open Targets gold-standard genes \citep{Mountjoy2021L2G}: for each trait, genes with curated evidence, from functional studies, Mendelian disease or drug targets, that they are the causal gene at a GWAS locus, assembled independently of this study and never used by the score ($15$ for LDL-C and $18$ for HDL-C; Section~2.7). The list is not complete: genes outside it are not shown to be unrelated to the trait, only not validated for it, and whether they act on it cannot be decided without testing them. Figure~\ref{fig:genes}a,b shows the rank of each gold-standard gene in the European data by its strongest variant, its MAGMA $p$-value and the CATFISH score. No single association layer ranked all of them well: \textit{LDLRAP1} ranked $251$st for LDL-C by its variant but $14$th by MAGMA, and \textit{CETP} first for HDL-C by its variant but $72$nd by MAGMA. The median rank was $99$ by variant, $336$ by MAGMA, $55$ by the two combined and $37$ with the pathway layer for LDL-C, and $78$, $378$, $122$ and $41$ for HDL-C (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:logo_recovery}). For HDL-C the two association layers together ranked these genes worse than the best variant alone, because MAGMA ranked many of them poorly; the pathway layer more than recovered this. The score ranked \textit{LDLR} first for LDL-C and \textit{ABCA1} first for HDL-C (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:lipid_candidates}), and it placed $6$ of the $15$ gold-standard LDL-C genes and $6$ of the $18$ HDL-C genes in the top twenty, against $3$ and $5$ without the pathway layer.

Figure~\ref{fig:genes}c,d shows every gene in the top twenty of the score in at least three of the five ancestry groups. Seven LDL-C genes and eight HDL-C genes were in the top twenty in all five groups: for LDL-C, the gold-standard genes \textit{LDLR}, \textit{PCSK9} and \textit{APOB}, with \textit{APOE}, \textit{APOC1}, \textit{PVRL2} and \textit{TOMM40}; for HDL-C, the gold-standard genes \textit{ABCA1}, \textit{CETP} and \textit{LIPC}, with \textit{LPL}, \textit{APOA5}, \textit{NLRC5}, \textit{SLC12A3} and \textit{HERPUD1}. Three of the genes outside the gold standard, \textit{APOE}, \textit{LPL} and \textit{APOA5}, are well-known lipid genes that the Open Targets list does not include for these traits, a reminder that the list is incomplete. The others lie in the chromosome~19 apolipoprotein cluster next to \textit{APOE} or beside \textit{CETP} on chromosome~16, and rank high mainly because they share the lead variant (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:replication_genes}); whether any of them also acts on lipids cannot be decided from association data.

Within a locus, association alone often cannot distinguish genes, but pathway membership can. \textit{APOE}, \textit{APOC1} and \textit{TOMM40} share the same best variant in every set, so GWAS ranks them equally; only \textit{APOE} and \textit{APOC1} belong to the leading pathway. Accordingly, the gene score ranked \textit{APOE} above \textit{TOMM40} in nine of ten sets, mainly by demoting \textit{TOMM40} and \textit{PVRL2} (Figure~\ref{fig:genes}e; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:apoe_locus}), and ranked \textit{CETP} above \textit{HERPUD1} and \textit{SLC12A3} in all groups. In Figure~\ref{fig:genes}e, each line joins one gene's rank by the GWAS layer and by the gene score in one of the ten sets: \textit{APOE} and \textit{APOC1} kept their ranks (median $5.5$ by the GWAS layer, and $5.5$ and $6$ by the score), while \textit{TOMM40} fell from a median of $5.5$ to $13$ and \textit{PVRL2} from $7.5$ to $17.5$. Genes tied across all three layers cannot be separated: \textit{APOC1} shares both variant and pathway with \textit{APOE} and ranks just behind it.

The improvement comes from other genes in a pathway, not a gene feeding back on itself. Without the pathway layer, the gold-standard genes had median ranks of $55$--$328$ across the ten sets; with it, $30.5$--$131$, with more of them in the top 20 in every set (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:logo_recovery}). Recomputing pathway evidence while leaving each gene out (Section~2.5) changed the medians little ($34$--$135$) and the number in the top 20 by at most one; in $130$ of $165$ gene--set pairs, the gene still ranked higher than without pathway evidence. This does not prove discovery of lipid biology: gold-standard genes and curated pathways draw on the same literature, so removing a gene eliminates its own signal but not shared knowledge.

We then asked whether the pathway layer improves candidate genes in all nine traits of Section~3.4, using the Open Targets gold-standard genes of each trait ($\nBenchNgold$ in total; Section~2.7). Across the nine traits, the genome-wide top 20/50/100 contained $\nBenchNpTwenty$, $\nBenchNpFifty$ and $\nBenchNpHundred$ gold-standard genes without pathways and $\nBenchCatTwenty$, $\nBenchCatFifty$ and $\nBenchCatHundred$ with them ($\nBenchGainTwenty\%$, $\nBenchGainFifty\%$ and $\nBenchGainHundred\%$ more; Figure~\ref{fig:genes}f; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:pathway_gain_all}). Figure~\ref{fig:genes}f shows the gain for each trait at the three cutoffs. It was never negative: the full score matched or exceeded the score without pathways for all nine traits at every cutoff, and was higher at one or more cutoffs for every trait, with the largest gains in the top 20 for LDL-C ($3$ genes) and asthma ($4$) and in the top 100 for breast cancer and Crohn's disease ($4$ each). The best variant alone recovered $\nBenchGwTwenty$, $\nBenchGwFifty$ and $\nBenchGwHundred$, and the MAGMA $p$-value alone $\nBenchMgTwenty$, $\nBenchMgFifty$ and $\nBenchMgHundred$.

To see how the score changes the list of candidates, we compared its genome-wide top~$\nLiftN$ with the top~$\nLiftN$ by the strongest variant alone in each trait (Table~\ref{tab:lifted_dropped}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:lifted_genes}). The ten highest-scoring genes of a trait were not simply its strongest GWAS genes: $\nLiftTopTenOut$ of the $90$ lay outside the top ten of the GWAS layer, some far outside, such as \textit{LDLRAP1} for LDL-C (rank $237$ by its variant, $9$ by the score) and \textit{IRF3} for SLE ($183$ and $8$). Across the nine traits, the score lifted $\nLiftAll$ genes into the top~$\nLiftN$ and dropped $\nDropAll$ from it. None of the dropped genes was a gold-standard gene, whereas $\nLiftGold$ of the lifted genes were, a proportion ($\nLiftGoldPct\%$) similar to that among genes in both top lists ($\nKeepGold$ of $\nKeepAll$, $\nKeepGoldPct\%$). In asthma, for example, the dropped genes include \textit{PGAP3}, \textit{PNMT}, \textit{TCAP} and \textit{LRRC3C}, which share the association of the chromosome~17q12--21 locus with \textit{ORMDL3} and \textit{GSDMB} but have no known role in asthma, and the lifted genes include the gold-standard genes \textit{IL4R} (rank $85$ by its variant, $7$ by the score), \textit{IL4} ($94$ and $14$) and \textit{IL2RA} ($79$ and $28$). Not all of this change comes from the pathway layer. MAGMA, which summarises every variant in a gene rather than its strongest one, lifted many of these genes by itself: \textit{IL4R} ranked $24$th by the score without the pathway layer, and the pathway layer moved it to $7$th. Of the lifted genes, $\nLiftPath$ were also outside the top~$\nLiftN$ of the score without pathways, and so were brought in by the pathway layer. $\nLiftPathGold$ of them were gold-standard genes, such as \textit{NR1H3} for HDL-C and \textit{STAT3} for Crohn's disease, and others are established biology of their trait, such as \textit{IRF3}, \textit{PLCG2} and \textit{CSK} for SLE. The score also dropped some genes with published evidence that are outside the gold standard and in no tested pathway, such as \textit{MST1} and \textit{NKX2-3} for UC and \textit{BANK1} for SLE, because such genes cannot gain pathway support. Thus, combining the three layers re-orders candidates within and across loci: it removes genes that carry only a shared strong variant and lifts genes with consistent gene-level and pathway evidence, without losing a gold-standard gene from the top~$\nLiftN$, but it cannot lift a gene that no pathway contains.

Finally, we compared CATFISH with PoPS \citep{Weeks2023PoPS}, a gene prioritization method that learns, from MAGMA gene statistics, which of thousands of gene features (expression, protein interactions and pathway membership) predict association, and ranks genes by the fitted model. This is a comparison of candidate genes against the same gold-standard genes, not of pathways; PoPS does not test pathways. With its pathway layer, CATFISH matched PoPS overall, identifying $\nBenchCatTwenty$ vs $\nBenchPopsTwenty$ gold-standard genes in the top 20, $\nBenchCatFifty$ vs $\nBenchPopsFifty$ in the top 50 and $\nBenchCatHundred$ vs $\nBenchPopsHundred$ in the top 100 (Figure~\ref{fig:genes}g; Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_topk_all}). Figure~\ref{fig:genes}g shows the top-20 counts by trait for the score without pathways, PoPS, CATFISH and the average of the CATFISH and PoPS ranks. CATFISH led or tied for LDL-C, HDL-C, CAD, breast cancer and AF, where curated metabolism, signalling and ion-transport pathways are informative; PoPS led for the four immune traits (Crohn's disease, UC, SLE and asthma), where its features (e.g., immune-cell expression) capture signals beyond pathway membership. CATFISH achieves this without model training or features beyond pathways. Restricting both rankings to genes in a tested pathway, which favours CATFISH, yielded $\nBenchAnnCatTwenty$ vs $\nBenchAnnPopsTwenty$ (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:supp_bench}). The methods prioritise different genes (Figure~\ref{fig:genes}h, which splits the gold-standard genes in each method's top 50 into those found by both, by CATFISH only and by PoPS only): among gold-standard genes in either top 20, $\nBenchBothTwenty$ were shared, $\nBenchOnlyCatTwenty$ CATFISH-only and $\nBenchOnlyPopsTwenty$ PoPS-only; averaging ranks increased recovery to $\nBenchCombTwenty$, $\nBenchCombFifty$ and $\nBenchCombHundred$ in the top 20, 50 and 100, exceeding either method alone. The leave-one-gene-out check has been run for lipids but not yet for the other seven traits. Thus, adding pathway evidence to GWAS and MAGMA evidence improved candidate genes in every trait we tested, and the resulting ranking matched a trained gene prioritization method while finding partly different genes, so the two are best used together.

\subsection*{3.6 A permutation null in long-LD sorghum confirms per-pathway calibration on real genotypes}

The human analyses test whether CATFISH finds the expected biology, but not whether its $p$-values are calibrated on real genotypes, because no pathway in a real GWAS is known to be null; the simulations of Sections~3.2 and~3.3 instead modelled the statistics. The sorghum Bioenergy Association Panel (BAP; $327$ lines) is a demanding setting for this test: a small, structured panel analysed with a mixed model, with sparse signal and long-range LD (each gene averages $\nSorNeighbours$ neighbours with $|r|\ge0.1$ in MAGMA's gene--gene correlation, against $\nLipNeighbours$ in humans). We had two goals: first, to measure the false-positive rate of the full pipeline on these genotypes with a permutation null, and second, to apply CATFISH pathway analysis and gene prioritization to a real trait of the panel, dry biomass.

Permuting the phenotype across lines breaks every genotype--phenotype link while keeping the genotypes, LD and population structure, so every pathway is null by construction and any rejection is a false positive \citep{Churchill1994,Wang2007Pathway}. We permuted dry biomass $100$ times and ran each permutation through the full GWAS, MAGMA and CATFISH pipeline ($304$ pathways; $30{,}400$ null tests; Section~2.7). Without gene-level genomic control, pathway $p$-values were anti-conservative: $7.2\%$ had $p<0.05$ and $2.3\%$ had $p<0.01$ despite $\lambda=0.84$, and $18/100$ permutations had at least one family-wise false positive (FWER $0.18$, $95\%$ CI $0.11$--$0.27$; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:sorghum_null}). In Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}a, this excess grows with pathway size, from $6.1\%$ for pathways of $5$--$9$ genes to $12.7\%$ for $50$--$200$ genes, and in the quantile--quantile plot of all null per-pathway $p$-values (Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}d) the curve without genomic control lies above the diagonal along its length. This was driven by a genome-wide shift: permutations moved gene $z$-scores together (SD of permutation means $0.24$ vs $0.075$ expected), genes on different chromosomes correlated $0.06$ across permutations, and the count of pathways with $p<0.05$ tracked the mean gene $z$-score (Spearman $0.90$; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:supp_sorghum_null}a). Self-contained tests interpret this shift as pathway signal that grows with size. Applying gene-level genomic control corrected it: $\lambda=1.01$, $5.2\%$ of pathways had $p<0.05$ and $1.3\%$ had $p<0.01$, with no size trend ($5.0$--$6.0\%$ across the size classes in Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}a), the quantile--quantile curve followed the diagonal except in the extreme tail, and the family-wise error rate dropped to $0.12$ ($12/98$; $95\%$ CI $0.06$--$0.20$; Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}b), with all $21$ family-wise false positives occurring in different pathways.

The remaining excess is confined to the extreme tail. After centring, per-pathway $p$-values $<0.001$ occurred $1.8\times$ more often than expected, and because the family minimum is a tail statistic, small per-pathway excesses inflate family-wise error. We checked two possible causes. The first was the gene--gene correlations. MAGMA overstated moderate correlations: in Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}c, the correlation of gene $z$-scores across permutations lies below the diagonal until MAGMA reports about $0.8$, and pairs reported at $r=0.52$ correlated only $0.32$. Replacing them with the correlations observed across permutations, however, did not reduce the family-wise error ($0.15$, $95\%$ CI $0.09$--$0.24$; Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}b). The second was the variant-level test, and this was the cause. CATFISH draws its null gene $z$-scores from a multivariate normal model, which has Gaussian tails. In this panel the mixed-model association test produced more extreme variant $p$-values than a Gaussian null allows: variant $p$-values $<10^{-4}$ were $4.5\times$ too frequent overall and $11.5\times$ at minor allele frequency $0.01$--$0.02$ (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:supp_sorghum_null}b). The real null of this GWAS therefore has heavier tails than the null that CATFISH draws, so the most extreme null pathways are slightly more extreme than the draws predict. The problem lies in this mismatch between the upstream test and the null model, not in the pathway procedure. We did not filter rare variants, because a filter chosen after seeing this result would be tuned to the outcome. In this panel, CATFISH with genomic control gives calibrated per-pathway $p$-values, while its family-wise $p$-values remain about $2.5\times$ anti-conservative ($0.12$ vs $0.05$).

Having established that per-pathway $p$-values are calibrated in this panel after genomic control, we applied CATFISH to the real dry-biomass GWAS, first to pathways and then to genes. No pathway was family-wise significant (Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}e): the ten leading pathways had family-wise $p$-values from $\nSorTopFwerDry$, for the top pathway, proanthocyanidin biosynthesis from flavanols, to $0.96$. Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}f shows the ten highest-ranked genes and each gene's rank in the three layers. Only the first and fourth genes had pathway evidence; the others are ranked on GWAS and MAGMA evidence alone, which often disagree by orders of magnitude (the second gene is first by MAGMA but several hundredth by its best variant). The top gene, Sobic.004G014400 (cycloartenol synthase; first committed step of sterol biosynthesis), is one of the two top-ten genes supported by all three layers: it shares its lead variant with seven neighbours but leads on MAGMA evidence, and the pathway layer moves it from rank $\nSorCasRankNp$ to first. Cycloartenol synthase loss is lethal in \textit{Arabidopsis} and sterol-deficient mutants impair cellulose synthesis \citep{Babiychuk2008,Schrick2012}; in bioenergy sorghum it is highest in young elongating internodes \citep{Kebrom2017}, but a biomass link is untested. The fourth-ranked gene, Sobic.008G029200, has a WRKY DNA-binding domain but gains pathway support from a SorghumBicolorCyc entry mis-annotating it as acyl-ACP thioesterase, so the pathway layer amplified an annotation error. The eight pre-specified flowering-time and height genes are in no tested pathway, so the pathway layer could not elevate them. Thus, in a structured panel with long-range LD, CATFISH with gene-level genomic control gave calibrated per-pathway $p$-values and only modestly anti-conservative family-wise $p$-values, the excess coming from the tail of the mixed-model association test, and for dry biomass it produced a ranked list of candidate genes but no family-wise significant pathway.

\subsection*{3.7 The gene score resolves a new sorghum sugar--fibre locus to two enzymes}

Dry biomass confirmed that per-pathway $p$-values were calibrated in this panel but showed no added signal from gene scores. We therefore analysed three stem-composition traits in the same lines (Section~2.7): Brix at maturity (soluble solids), neutral detergent fibre (NDF; cell wall) and non-fibre carbohydrate (NFC; mainly sugars and starch). Brix correlated with NDF ($r=\nBapRBrixNDF$) and NFC ($r=\nBapRBrixNFC$): higher stem sugar coincides with lower cell wall, the sweet--biomass trade-off in sorghum. With family-wise control, the self-contained test called $\nBapSigBrix$, $\nBapSigNDF$ and $\nBapSigNFC$ of the $\nBapNpw$ pathways for Brix, NDF and NFC, and the competitive test called none. Two pathways were significant for both fibre traits, the thioredoxin pathway (family-wise $p=0.0062$ for NDF and $8.0\times10^{-4}$ for NFC) and xylogalacturonan biosynthesis ($p=0.042$ and $0.032$); $\beta$-alanine biosynthesis I was significant for Brix ($p=0.015$), and triacylglycerol degradation and stachyose biosynthesis for NFC only. The thioredoxin pathway and $\beta$-alanine biosynthesis I are effectively single-gene pathways whose gene lies in the chromosome~4 locus described below; as in the simulation (Section~3.2), the self-contained test detected them but the competitive test did not (family-wise $p=\nBapBalaCompBrix$, $\nBapTrxPwCompNDF$, $\nBapTrxPwCompNFC$). These calls therefore restate the locus through its annotations rather than implicate $\beta$-alanine or thioredoxin metabolism.

Gene scores mapped all three traits to a single locus. Of $\nBapNgenes$ genes, $\nBapNshared$ were in the top 100 for all traits, all within $85\,\mathrm{kb}$ on chromosome~4 ($63.96$--$64.04\,\mathrm{Mb}$; Figure~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}d; Table~\ref{tab:bap_chr4}), a region not previously linked to sugar or fibre. Two neighbouring genes with pathway evidence led the rankings: Sobic.004G300800 (\textit{SbALDH3E1}, ALDH3 fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase; predicted chloroplast-localised \citep{Islam2022SbALDH}) ranked $\nBapAldhBrix$, $\nBapAldhNDF$ and $\nBapAldhNFC$; Sobic.004G300900 (TRXR, NADPH-dependent thioredoxin reductase) ranked $\nBapTrxBrix$, $\nBapTrxNDF$ and $\nBapTrxNFC$. Figure~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}d shows the rank of every gene of the locus, in order of position: the two enzymes form the peak for all three traits, the unannotated Sobic.004G301200--301700 rank $43$--$192$, and the genes at either end of the locus rank $181$ or lower. Among genes supported in all layers, \textit{SbALDH3E1} was first for Brix and TRXR second for both fibre traits. Neither gene contained the top GWAS variants: for Brix the best variant is shared with four neighbours, and for NDF/NFC the strongest variants are about $60\,\mathrm{kb}$ away in unannotated Sobic.004G301200--301700. However, these two enzymes had the smallest MAGMA $p$-values in the locus for all traits and were the only locus genes in any pathway, shifting the fibre signal from unannotated genes to the adjacent enzymes.

Part of the pathway support is the genes' own: the two pathways that contain them are significant largely because of them. To measure this, we gave each gene the evidence its pathways carry without it: for \textit{SbALDH3E1}, every pathway containing it was rescored with \textit{SbALDH3E1} removed, and likewise for TRXR, on the same null draws and with every other gene unchanged (Section~2.5). In Figure~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}e, each grey line follows one pathway-supported gene of a trait's top 100 from its rank without the pathway layer, through its rank with it, to its rank with its own contribution removed; the black lines are \textit{SbALDH3E1} and TRXR, and the dashed line marks rank 20. \textit{SbALDH3E1} still ranked highly for Brix ($\nBapGmAldhBrix$ without pathways; $\nBapAldhBrix$ with pathways; $\nBapLogoAldhBrix$ without its own contribution). TRXR similarly retained most of its pathway lift (its rise in rank when the pathway layer is added) for fibre traits, ranking $\nBapLogoTrxNDF$ (NDF) and $\nBapLogoTrxNFC$ (NFC) without its own contribution, compared with $\nBapTrxNDF$/$\nBapTrxNFC$ with it and $\nBapGmTrxNDF$/$\nBapGmTrxNFC$ without pathways. For the other trait of each gene, the lift was mostly its own: TRXR dropped to $\nBapLogoTrxBrix$ for Brix, and \textit{SbALDH3E1} to $\nBapLogoAldhNDF$ and $\nBapLogoAldhNFC$ for fibre. Across pathway-supported genes in each trait’s top 100, $\nBapLogoKeepBrix$ of $\nBapLogoNBrix$ (Brix), $\nBapLogoKeepNDF$ of $\nBapLogoNNDF$ (NDF) and $\nBapLogoKeepNFC$ of $\nBapLogoNNFC$ (NFC) remained in the top 100 after removing their own contribution; median ranks fell between the with- and without-pathway analyses, suggesting ~half of the lift comes from other genes in the same pathways.

The three traits’ lead variants are in strong LD: the Brix lead at $\nBapLeadPosBrix\,\mathrm{Mb}$ (6 kb from the dehydrogenase) and the shared NDF/NFC lead at $\nBapLeadPosNDF\,\mathrm{Mb}$ have $r^2=\nBapLeadRsq$, and Brix and NDF $z$-scores correlate across $\nBapNwin$ variants ($\nBapZcor$): in Figure~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}c, with each variant's alleles oriented to raise Brix, the variants fall along one diagonal, with the Brix lead (triangle) and the NDF/NFC lead (circle) at its extreme. The signal persisted after three principal components. Each copy of the Brix-increasing allele (\nBapUp; frequency $\nBapFreqUp$) raised Brix by $\nBapEffBrix$ and NFC by $\nBapEffNFC$ SD and lowered NDF by $|\nBapEffNDF|$ (Figure~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}b). In Figure~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}a, lines homozygous for this allele have higher Brix and lower NDF than the other homozygotes, and their mean falls below the line relating NDF to Brix across all lines. After conditioning on Brix, NDF still differed by $\nBapAdjNDF$ between homozygotes ($p=\nBapAdjP$), implying the sugar--fibre trade-off explains ~$\nBapFracVia\%$ of the allele’s fibre effect. The locus does not appear to affect maturity or height: no cloned maturity/height gene is on chromosome~4; the strongest flowering-time signal had $p=1.1\times10^{-4}$; and the Brix allele showed no effect on stalk height ($p=0.29$) or dry biomass ($p=0.09$). In public RNA-seq, \textit{SbALDH3E1} is broadly expressed, highest in leaf sheath at flowering and lowest in stem internodes (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:sbaldh_expression}) \citep{Moreno2022ExpressionAtlas}. Related evidence includes a maize fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase implicated in kernel oil \citep{Zhang2026FALDH} and \textit{Arabidopsis} ALDH3F1 supplying acetate for histone acetylation at \textit{FLOWERING LOCUS C} \citep{Xu2020ALDH3F1}. Thus, starting from three GWAS whose strongest variants fall in unannotated genes, CATFISH pointed to one shared locus and, within it, to two annotated enzymes, ranked partly on the evidence of the other genes in their pathways, as concrete candidates for functional tests such as gene editing.

\section{4 Discussion}

\subsection*{4.1 CATFISH detects different pathway signals and stays calibrated on simulated and real genotypes}

Pathway signals vary, so no single test detects all types. In simulations, each component test excelled in its target signal but could lose $<0.5$ power on others. The omnibus was usually near the best component and lost substantial power only when a single gene drove the pathway (Section~3.1). Thus, combining five tests largely removes the need to pre-specify signal type, at modest cost.

Furthermore, the omnibus $p$-values were well calibrated. CATFISH computes both its per-pathway and its family-wise $p$-values from the same null draws, and these draws carry the correlation between genes. This kept both $p$-values calibrated in every stress test: with strong correlation between the genes of a pathway, with up to four of the five component tests miscalibrated, and with a component removed. The analytic omnibus, which treats genes as independent, and a Bonferroni correction across pathways lost calibration in the same tests (Sections~3.2 and~3.3). Additionally, the method relies on gene--gene correlation. As correlations went missing, family-wise $p$-values degraded faster than per-pathway $p$-values, but still outperformed Bonferroni until most correlations were absent. In GWAS simulations with real LD, CATFISH made no false calls under the null (vs. $16\%$ for MAGMA’s self-contained test) and detected sparse pathways that MAGMA largely missed.

The simulations modelled gene statistics; the sorghum analysis asked whether CATFISH stays calibrated on real genotypes, running the full pipeline from GWAS to pathways. We permuted dry biomass across the lines of a structured panel with long-range LD, so that every pathway was null and every significant pathway a false positive (Section~3.6). This revealed two problems that LD does not explain. First, in each permuted GWAS, all gene $z$-scores moved up or down together. A self-contained test asks whether a pathway's genes are more associated than expected by chance, so a shift shared by every gene makes every pathway look associated, and the more genes a pathway has, the more the shift adds up. Without correction, $18$ of $100$ permutations gave at least one significant pathway (family-wise error $0.18$ instead of $0.05$), and false positives became more frequent as pathways grew. Subtracting each GWAS's median gene $z$-score removed the shift and restored calibration, so CATFISH applies this gene-level genomic control by default. Second, even with the phenotype permuted, the mixed-model GWAS gave rare variants extreme $p$-values far more often than chance allows. CATFISH's null draws assume normally distributed gene statistics, so they underestimate how extreme a null gene can be. This barely affected each pathway's own $p$-value, the per-pathway $p$-value ($5.2\%$ below $0.05$), but the family-wise $p$-value is set by the most extreme of all $304$ pathways, so its error rose to $0.12$. Thus, in small panels analysed with mixed models, per-pathway $p$-values are reliable, a family-wise $p$-value near $0.05$ should be read as borderline, and a phenotype permutation, when genotypes are available, checks it directly.

Human analyses asked whether the method recovers known biology. Plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling and clearance ranked first for LDL-C and HDL-C in all five ancestry groups—ten independent meta-analyses spanning up to 32-fold differences in sample size, allele frequencies and LD (Section~3.4). The traits implicated largely different genes (receptor-mediated uptake for LDL-C; reverse transport and lipolysis for HDL-C), supporting pathway- rather than shared-signal convergence. Across seven additional traits, top pathways matched known biology. For lipids, CATFISH ranked the lipoprotein pathway first more consistently than MAGMA (10/10 vs 6/10) and was less driven by pathway size.

\subsection*{4.2 When significance saturates, the ranking carries the information}

In the European lipid GWAS, $11.2\%$ of genes were genome-wide significant, and a random set of $50$ genes rejected the self-contained null with probability $0.997$. Even with a strong family-wise control, $\nLipSigLDL$ of $\nLipNpw$ pathways remained significant for LDL-C. At this sample size, the self-contained hypothesis (that no gene in a pathway is associated) is false for almost any gene set \citep{Goeman2007,Maciejewski2014}, and MAGMA flagged even more pathways. Tightening the threshold does not fix this, because the issue is the question, not multiple-testing correction.

Nonetheless, the self-contained analysis still separated pathways, because CATFISH ranks them by $z_{\mathrm{std}}$, which compares each pathway with null draws of its own size and correlation. In a saturated genome, large pathways accumulate evidence simply by having more genes, so a ranking by raw $p$-value favours them, as MAGMA's did. The CATFISH ranking depended less on pathway size, and the lipoprotein pathways were extreme for their size in both traits (Section~3.4). The competitive test, which asks whether a pathway's genes are more associated than the rest of the genome, does not saturate and highlighted the same pathways, an independent check. Thus, in large GWAS, the number of significant self-contained pathways says little, while the CATFISH ranking, confirmed by the competitive test, carries the information. When few genes are associated, as in sorghum, self-contained significance remains informative, and it is the only test that detects a pathway driven by a single gene.

\subsection*{4.3 Pathway evidence lets candidate genes be chosen beyond the strength of their GWAS signal}

We hypothesized that a candidate gene is more credible when it is in a trait-relevant pathway and supported by its gene score. The common post-GWAS practice of ranking genes by the strongest variant inflates candidate lists with many genes from the same locus. At 17q12--21 (asthma) or the \textit{APOE} cluster (LDL-C), all genes in the linkage block share the lead variant and are ranked together regardless of function. Using a gene prioritization formula with GWAS, MAGMA, and pathway layers addressed this. Compared with the top~$\nLiftN$ genes ranked by their strongest variant, it removed $\nDropAll$ genes across nine human traits (none was a gold-standard gene) and brought in $\nLiftAll$, including $\nLiftGold$ gold-standard genes and known biology such as \textit{IL4R}/\textit{IL4} for asthma and \textit{IRF3} for SLE (Section~3.5). It also placed $\nBenchGainTwenty\%$ more gold-standard genes in the top 20 than GWAS+MAGMA without pathways, and never fewer in any of the nine human traits. GWAS and MAGMA can disagree substantially (e.g., \textit{CETP} is the top-variant for HDL-C but 72nd by MAGMA; \textit{LDLRAP1} shows the reverse for LDL-C), so candidates need not be the top-variant gene and can be prioritized by concordant gene-level association and pathway evidence rather than by a single variant.

However, this has two limitations. First, the gain may partly reflect prior knowledge. The gold-standard genes are well studied and annotated to more pathways, so some lift may come from shared literature rather than GWAS. Leave-one-gene-out addresses this partly. By removing a gene’s own association from its pathways, the lipid gold-standard ranks (Section~3.5) were barely changed, implying a lift from other pathway genes. But it cannot remove gene–pathway shared knowledge, and it was run only for lipids. Second, the pathway layer helps only genes present in pathways and can propagate annotation errors. Genes with evidence but no pathway (e.g., \textit{MST1} for UC, \textit{BANK1} for SLE) dropped from the top~$\nLiftN$, and in sorghum, a gene with a WRKY transcription-factor domain gained pathway support because SorghumBicolorCyc annotates it as an acyl-ACP thioesterase (Section~3.6). Within these limits, the genes the score places at the top are mostly genes of the trait's known biology: none of the dropped genes was a gold-standard gene, lifted genes were as often gold-standard as genes in both lists, and all $90$ top-ten genes lie at loci reported for their trait (Section~4.5). Thus, the gene score is best used to choose genes for follow-up, reading each gene's rank on every layer, rather than as proof of causality.

With its pathway layer, CATFISH recovered about as many gold-standard genes as PoPS ($\nBenchCatTwenty$ vs $\nBenchPopsTwenty$ in the top 20, $\nBenchCatFifty$ vs $\nBenchPopsFifty$ in the top 50 and $\nBenchCatHundred$ vs $\nBenchPopsHundred$ in the top 100; with the MHC included, $29$ vs $31$, $51$ vs $51$ and $67$ vs $65$; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:supp_bench}). PoPS trains a model on thousands of gene features, including tissue and cell-type expression; CATFISH uses only GWAS, MAGMA and pathway membership, without training. CATFISH led where pathway biology is well curated (lipids, CAD, breast cancer, AF) and trailed in the immune traits, where expression features carry information that pathway membership does not, so an expression layer is a natural addition to the gene score (Section~5). The two methods also found partly different genes, and averaging their ranks recovered more gold-standard genes than either alone, so they complement each other.

\subsection*{4.4 A sorghum locus that moves stem carbon between sugar and cell wall}

The stem-composition traits show what the gene score adds when the strongest GWAS variants fall in unannotated genes. Three correlated traits (Brix, NFC, NDF) mapped to an $\sim85\,\mathrm{kb}$ haplotype on chromosome~4, with lead variants in strong LD ($r^2=\nBapLeadRsq$) and largely interchangeable evidence across $\sim15$ genes. For the two fiber traits, the strongest variants were in unannotated genes. Gene scores narrowed the locus to two adjacent enzymes, which include a fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase \textit{SbALDH3E1} and an NADPH-dependent thioredoxin reductase gene, which ranked in the top $35$ genome-wide for every trait and were the only locus genes in any pathway. For gene editing, this reduces $\sim34{,}000$ genes to two candidates.

A single locus can shift all three traits because they share a carbon budget. NDF reflects cell wall, NFC mainly soluble sugars/starch, and Brix soluble solids in stem juice. Carbon used for cell walls is unavailable as sugar, consistent with the observed Brix–NDF correlation ($\nBapRBrixNDF$; Figure~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}a). The Brix-increasing haplotype raised Brix and NFC and lowered NDF by similar standardized amounts (Figure~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}b), with no detectable effects on flowering, height, or biomass, so the effect is not due to later, taller, or smaller plants. Adjusting for Brix only halved the NDF difference between the two homozygous groups (from $\nBapDiffNDF$ to $\nBapAdjNDF$ percentage points of dry matter), so the sugar–fibre trade-off explains about $\nBapFracVia\%$ of the haplotype's fibre effect. The rest suggests that the locus also alters carbon partitioning or cell-wall deposition directly, not only through sugar accumulation. Both candidates could plausibly act via different mechanisms. The ALDH3 enzymes oxidize lipid-derived aldehydes to acids, connecting aldehyde detoxification to fatty-acid/acetate pools \citep{Islam2022SbALDH}. In \textit{Arabidopsis}, ALDH3F1 supplies acetate for acetyl-CoA used in histone acetylation at a developmental regulator \citep{Xu2020ALDH3F1}, and in maize, a fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase is a candidate for kernel oil content \citep{Zhang2026FALDH}. Thioredoxin reductases keep thioredoxins reduced, and reduced thioredoxins switch many enzymes of carbon metabolism on or off by reducing their disulphide bonds \citep{BuchananBalmer2005,Geigenberger2017Trx}; in \textit{Arabidopsis}, the plastid NADPH-dependent thioredoxin reductase C links sucrose supply to starch synthesis \citep{Michalska2009NTRC}. Neither gene is yet linked to stem sugar or cell walls in sorghum or other species, and we do not claim either is causal.

However, the results have limitations. Much of the pathway evidence for the two genes is effectively single-gene. When each gene was removed from its own pathways, \textit{SbALDH3E1} kept its Brix rank and TRXR kept most of its lift for the fibre traits, the rise in rank the pathway layer gives it, so these ranks rest on other genes of their pathways; for the other traits, the lift came from the genes themselves (Section~3.7). The haplotype occurs in $\nBapNUpHom$ of $\nBapNlines$ lines. Adding three principal components barely changed the association, but biparental or near-isogenic lines are needed to exclude population history. \textit{SbALDH3E1} is expressed mainly in leaves and leaf sheaths and little in stem internodes (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:sbaldh_expression}). This is consistent with an effect on carbon supply rather than on the stem itself, because stem sugars are made in leaves and moved to the stem. Effect sizes for a significance selected variant are also likely inflated in a panel of this size. Future work could test the two genes directly, by measuring allele-specific expression in stem, sheath and leaf, comparing coding and regulatory sequence between the two haplotypes, and creating loss-of-function alleles by mutagenesis or genome editing.

\subsection*{4.5 The top-ranked genes in nine human traits are known genes of those traits}

To assess what the gene score prioritizes, we reviewed the top ten genes for each of nine human traits and checked the literature (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:top10}). All 90 genes map to loci previously reported for the corresponding trait. For 40, genetic or functional evidence implicates the gene itself, including Mendelian lipid-disorder genes (\textit{LDLR}, \textit{LDLRAP1}, \textit{PCSK9} for LDL-C; \textit{ABCA1} for HDL-C \citep{BrownGoldstein1986,Garcia2001LDLRAP1,Abifadel2003PCSK9,BrooksWilson1999ABCA1}), genes with trait-altering loss-of-function or coding variants (\textit{SCARB1}, \textit{GALNT2} for HDL-C; \textit{NOD2}, \textit{IL23R} for Crohn’s disease; \textit{IL33} for asthma \citep{Zanoni2016SCARB1,Khetarpal2016GALNT2,Hugot2001NOD2,Duerr2006IL23R,Smith2017IL33}), and genes whose expression/splicing is affected by risk variants (\textit{ESR1} for breast cancer; \textit{COL4A1}, \textit{CDKN2A}, \textit{CDKN2B} for CAD; \textit{IRF5}, \textit{UBE2L3} for SLE; \textit{GSDMB} for asthma \citep{Dunning2016ESR1,Yang2016COL4A1,Visel2010,Graham2006IRF5,Lewis2015UBE2L3,Panganiban2018GSDMB}). For 43, the gene lies at a known locus but the causal gene is unresolved, e.g., \textit{SLC22A3} in the \textit{SLC22A3}--\textit{LPAL2}--\textit{LPA} CAD cluster \citep{Tregouet2009}, \textit{NLRC5} and \textit{SLC12A3} near \textit{CETP} and \textit{MYO5B} near \textit{LIPG} for HDL-C, and \textit{STAT1} for SLE (risk variant in \textit{STAT4} but alters \textit{STAT1} expression in B cells \citep{Patel2018STAT1}). For the remaining 7, evidence points to a nearby gene. The \textit{PHACTR1} CAD variant regulates \textit{EDN1} \citep{Gupta2017PHACTR1}; breast cancer variants near \textit{ZNF365} regulate \textit{NRBF2} \citep{Darabi2015ZNF365}; the 1p13 LDL-C signal acts through \textit{SORT1} rather than \textit{KIAA1324} \citep{Musunuru2010SORT1}; and \textit{SNAPC4}, \textit{SDCCAG3}, and \textit{SAG} for Crohn’s disease lie at the \textit{CARD9} and \textit{ATG16L1} loci \citep{Franke2010CD}. Overall, the score recapitulates established trait genetics, and none of the top genes comes from a previously unreported locus.

Two points help in reading these lists. First, several top genes of a trait share a locus, such as \textit{APOE}, \textit{APOC1} and \textit{PVRL2} for LDL-C or \textit{GPX1}, \textit{RHOA} and \textit{APEH} on chromosome~3p21 for UC. They are not separate discoveries, but they show where the score helps most: at the \textit{APOE} locus, the pathway layer ranked \textit{APOE} above \textit{TOMM40}, although both carry the same lead variant (Section~3.5). Second, a high rank does not by itself name the causal gene. At the seven neighbour loci, the causal gene was identified by experiments showing which gene the risk variant switches on or off, such as \textit{EDN1} at \textit{PHACTR1}, and the score does not use such data. The top lists also contain established drug targets, \textit{PCSK9} and \textit{CETP} among them, consistent with the value of genetic evidence for choosing drug targets \citep{Nelson2015DrugGenetics,Sabatine2017FOURIER}. Thus, the score recovers the known genes of each trait and, where a locus holds several genes, points to those with pathway support.


\subsection*{4.6 Recommendations for use}

Before pathway analysis, inspect the gene-level null: a median gene $p$-value far from $0.5$ suggests saturation or population structure. Under strong LD, MAGMA’s top-variant and multi-model $p$-values inflate with gene size (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:sorghum_pcol}), so use the SNP-wise mean model. Centre gene $z$-scores at the median. Verify the gene--gene correlation file covers all chromosomes and most nearby within-pathway pairs; we once parsed only chromosome~1, implicitly treating $85\%$ of the genome as independent. Compute statistics on the log scale: with large samples many gene $p$-values underflow double precision, silently corrupting pathway ranks (Section~2.4). Report per-pathway and family-wise $p$-values alongside the competitive test; in saturated GWAS interpret rankings against pathway size, not “significant” pathways. If genotypes are available, use a phenotype-permutation null to validate family-wise $p$-values. For candidate genes, check each gene’s rank at every layer, and verify annotations when the rank depends on the pathway layer.

\section{5 Limitations and future work}

Several limitations constrain our conclusions.

\textbf{No simulated GWAS.} The simulations in Section~2.6 model only the omnibus statistic and its null, using block-structured and exchangeable gene--gene correlation. We did not simulate genotypes, assign causal variants, generate phenotypes, or run the full GWAS--MAGMA--CATFISH pipeline. This natural next step would assess power under realistic genetic architectures rather than latent mean shifts and was intentionally omitted. Therefore, the power curves in Section~3.1 reflect stylised gene-level $z$-score signals, not realistic genetic architectures.

\textbf{Unreported correlations are set to zero.} The correlation matrices in Section~2.3 are formed by inserting MAGMA-reported gene pairs into an identity matrix, so any unreported pair is treated as independent. This is usually reasonable for distant genes and yields a banded matrix, but nearby unreported pairs (e.g., crossing batch boundaries or involving a dropped gene) understate dependence and can be anti-conservative. Section~3.3 quantifies this effect and shows it is not shared equally: the family-wise reading degrades faster than the marginal one because the family minimum is a tail statistic, where correlation misspecification is most damaging. Chromosome-coverage checks catch gross failures; \texttt{10\_pair\_coverage.R} measures finer gaps, which we recommend reporting alongside any family-wise $p$-value.

\textbf{Simplified LD models.} True gene--gene correlation decays with distance. The shared-universe simulations approximate LD with independent exchangeable blocks, so pathways spanning blocks are mostly uncorrelated; the exchangeable design correlates all pathway members, exaggerating LD and serving only as a stress test. Neither captures gradual distance-based decay. Real-data analyses use empirical MAGMA correlations and avoid this limitation.

\textbf{What strong control rests on.} The strong family-wise control in Section~2.3 is exact only under its model. It assumes association shifts mean gene $z$-scores while leaving correlations among unassociated genes unchanged, and it defines a null pathway as one with no associated genes. Under linkage disequilibrium, a gene may appear associated only because it tags a causal variant in a nearby gene; a pathway containing such a gene is non-null under the self-contained gene-level hypothesis even if it contains no causal gene. Rejecting it is therefore not a family-wise error, though it may be a biological false positive. Strong control also depends on an adequate correlation matrix; Section~3.3 shows family-wise calibration degrades quickly when the matrix is incomplete. We did not separately verify strong control by simulation; under the model it follows from the complete-null results.

\textbf{Parametric null.} Null draws use a multivariate normal correlation model rather than phenotype permutations. Although Westfall--Young in form, validity depends on the correlation model (Section~3.3). The sorghum permutation null (Section~3.6) directly checks this: after genomic control, per-pathway calibration was nominal but the family-wise error rate was $0.12$, likely because variant-level tests have heavier tails than a Gaussian null. We tested one panel and one trait; results may differ with weaker LD, larger samples, or better-calibrated association tests. We recommend a permutation null before relying on family-wise $p$-values in a new panel.

\textbf{Soft TFisher resolution.} Soft TFisher cannot resolve $p$-values below $\sim 10^{-16}$, causing ties among highly enriched pathways. Other components resolve smaller $p$-values and can dominate the omnibus ranking; implementing the soft-threshold tail on the log scale would remove this limit.

\textbf{Partial comparison with other methods.} We compared CATFISH with MAGMA gene-set analysis on lipid data (Section~3.4) and in simulations with known truth (Section~3.2) assessing calibration and power. Simulations used one LD panel, ten chromosomes, and five signal architectures, and did not include other gene-set methods.

\textbf{Incomplete cross-species replication.} Westfall--Young was run on maize, sorghum, and human data. The sorghum stem-volume dataset from the original CATFISH paper could not be re-analysed under the new calibration because the MAGMA \texttt{.genes.raw} file was missing; Table~\ref{tab:density} therefore reports signal density from gene-level output only. The Section~3.6 sorghum analysis uses the BAP panel and, with $330$ lines, is underpowered for pathway discovery, focusing on calibration and gene ranking rather than new pathway detection.

\textbf{Tail resolution.} Monte Carlo calibration resolves $p$-values to $1/(B+1)$. CATFISH can extrapolate extreme tails via a generalized Pareto fit \citep{Knijnenburg2009}, but needs enough valid null draws; a single missing draw can disable it. In one human run, $638$ of $1{,}348$ pathways tied at the Monte Carlo floor. We now require only half (not all) permutations, but have not validated this at production scale.

\textbf{Dependence on upstream choices.} Results depend on the SNP-to-gene window, LD reference panel, gene model, and pathway database, as with similar methods. The $10\,$kb windows used here are standard but may not be trait-optimal.

\section{6 Conclusion}

Pathway analysis of GWAS summary statistics has two dependence problems, but most work addresses only the easier one. Gene–gene correlation within pathways is routinely modeled; pathway–pathway correlation from shared gene membership is often “corrected” by assuming it away. We add a Westfall–Young max-statistic layer to CATFISH that provides strong family-wise error control under this dependence, uses the same gene–gene correlation model already needed for calibration (no extra estimation), and quantifies how far Bonferroni deviates from the truth for a given database when applied to calibrated statistics.

Two broader results follow. First, computing the omnibus in log space is required at modern GWAS sample sizes; otherwise a silent failure can occur. Second, self-contained pathway testing saturates: in a GWAS of $1.3$ million individuals, $11.2\%$ of genes are genome-wide significant, a random 50-gene set rejects with probability $0.997$, yet strong FWER control leaves $\nLipSigLDL$ of $\nLipNpw$ pathways significant. This reflects the hypothesis, not the correction, and ranking remains informative: plasma lipoprotein assembly/remodeling/clearance leads the LDL-C ordering, lipoprotein pathways are extreme for size in both LDL-C and HDL-C, and gene prioritization recovers known lipid genes better than GWAS association alone. In a sorghum panel with much denser LD and low power, the same procedure finds no significant pathways—as expected for a calibrated method—and a genotype-permutation null shows that centering gene $z$-scores calibrates each pathway while FWER remains above nominal. When few genes are associated, CATFISH’s self-contained question is natural; whether it outperforms competitive tests for weak signals remains open.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note S1: The five pathway signal archetypes}

This note extends Section~3.1. A pathway’s \emph{ranked profile} is its gene-level $-\log_{10}p$ values sorted from largest to smallest; an archetype is the profile’s shape. Archetypes are statistical templates: they are not inferred from real pathways, real pathways may mix features of several, and this paper does not assign real pathways to archetypes.

\subsubsection*{S1.1 Strong Sparse Signal (SSS)}

In an SSS pathway, most association evidence comes from a small subset of genes, while the rest are near null. The ranked profile has one or a few very small ordered $p$-values, then quickly drops to null-like values. Thus the pathway signal is driven by a few high-evidence genes rather than broad enrichment, so head-focused statistics such as ACAT and minP should be most efficient. Methods that weight all genes equally dilute these leading genes with the null majority.

SSS is expected when pathway behaviour is controlled by one or a few bottleneck genes (e.g., a committed-step enzyme, rate-limiting regulator, or essential transporter) \citep{MorenoSanchez2008,Naz2023,Tanner2018,Song2020}. For example, aspartokinase initiates the aspartate-derived amino-acid pathway that produces lysine, threonine, methionine and isoleucine \citep{Galili2011} by phosphorylating aspartate to aspartyl phosphate \citep{Hasenoehrl2019}. Variation in this entry enzyme can create a strong localised signal while downstream genes remain buffered or near null. In the maize grain-nitrogen analysis (Section~2.7), the top candidate gene is an aspartate kinase isozyme, and the top pathway, L-homoserine biosynthesis, is the branch whose first step it catalyses.

\subsubsection*{S1.2 Single-Gene Proxy (SGP)}

In an SGP pathway, significance is driven by one strongly associated gene while the rest are near null; the ranked profile shows a single extreme peak followed by an otherwise null-like distribution. Statistically, SGP is a boundary case of SSS, with minP and ACAT again the most sensitive components. Interpretively, however, the pathway adds little beyond the dominant gene-level signal.

Biologically, SGP occurs when a pathway is effectively defined by one major gene, often in small or overlapping annotations. Phenylalanine metabolism illustrates this. \textit{PAH} encodes phenylalanine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme converting phenylalanine to tyrosine, and pathogenic \textit{PAH} variation is the main cause of phenylketonuria and most PAH deficiency \citep{Flydal2013PAH,Elhawary2022PKU}. Broader phenylalanine-metabolism pathways include genes for tetrahydrobiopterin synthesis, recycling, chaperoning, and related processes, but the dominance of \textit{PAH} means pathway significance may reflect a gene-centric signal rather than coordinated enrichment. Accordingly, pathways significant only by minP and ACAT should be interpreted cautiously; Section~3.1 also shows this is where the omnibus loses the most power relative to its best component.

\subsubsection*{S1.3 Moderate Dense Signal (MDS)}

In an MDS pathway, many genes show modest but consistent association, with no single dominant gene. The ranked profile shows a broad shoulder of moderately small $p$-values rather than a sharp peak, and pathway-level evidence reflects the combined effects of many genes. Methods that aggregate consistent moderate signals (Fisher’s and Stouffer’s) should be most sensitive; soft TFisher should also perform well when enrichment is concentrated in a moderately sized subset.

Biologically, MDS is expected in pathways whose output depends on many interacting components rather than a single effector, such as cytokine-driven immune signalling. In TNF$\alpha$/IL-1$\beta$ to NF-$\kappa$B signalling, activity depends on receptor engagement, adaptor recruitment, kinase cascades, feedback, and downstream transcriptional and post-transcriptional control \citep{Jiang2021CytoSig,Megha2021Inflammation,Verstrepen2008NFkB,vanLoo2011NFkB,Cargnello2011MAPK}. Variation in such pathways is therefore more likely to produce many moderate perturbations than one large-effect change.

\subsubsection*{S1.4 Weak Diffuse Enrichment (WDE)}

In a WDE pathway, most genes are individually non-significant, but the pathway shows a modest shift toward stronger association. The ranked profile displays a gentle, pathway-wide deflection rather than a peak, indicating a weak but widespread signal. Methods that capture distributed enrichment across many genes, especially Stouffer’s and Fisher’s, should be most sensitive \citep{deLeeuw2015}; extreme-tail statistics stay near the null because no gene is extreme.

Biologically, WDE reflects a highly polygenic architecture in which pathway signal comes from many small effects. Human height is a classic example, shaped by thousands of variants with small individual effects \citep{Guo2018HeightGWAS,Yengo2022HeightSaturation}. In such settings, pathways such as TGF-$\beta$ signalling, Hedgehog signalling, and growth-plate extracellular-matrix processes may be detectable mainly through cumulative pathway enrichment rather than strong single-gene signals \citep{LangoAllen2010Height,Guo2018HeightGWAS}.

\subsubsection*{S1.5 Mixed Signal Architecture (MSA)}

In an MSA pathway, one or a few genes show strong association while many others provide moderate support. The ranked profile has a sharp leading peak and a moderate shoulder, placing MSA between sparse SSS and coordinated MDS. Methods that capture both features should be most sensitive, especially adaptive soft TFisher and Fisher’s method; ACAT mainly tracks the peak. In the family-wise simulation of Section~3.1, Fisher was the most powerful for MSA at all pathway sizes, with soft TFisher close behind.

Biologically, MSA arises when a few core regulatory or structural genes with large effects sit within a broader network of auxiliary components. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol metabolism illustrates this: rare, high-effect variants in \textit{LDLR}, \textit{APOB} and \textit{PCSK9} cause monogenic familial hypercholesterolaemia, while population-level LDL-C variation also reflects many common alleles with small to moderate effects in other lipid genes \citep{Umlai2025LDLC,Peloso2022WGSbloodlipids}. GWAS further implicate genes involved in lipoprotein metabolism and remodelling, including \textit{ABCA1}, \textit{LIPC}, \textit{CETP}, \textit{PLTP}, \textit{ANGPTL3} and \textit{SORT1} \citep{Chasman2009LipoproteinProfiles}. Several are among the established genes used to evaluate the candidate-gene score in Section~3.5.

\subsubsection*{S1.6 How the archetypes were simulated}

The simulation in Section~3.1 (Section~2.6, item~6) uses a shared-universe design: $3{,}000$ genes in independent blocks of $50$ with exchangeable within-block correlation $\rho=0.3$. From the first $600$ genes, we randomly draw a family of $K=150$ overlapping pathways of size $m\in\{5,20,50\}$. Exactly one pathway carries signal; the other $149$ are null.

Signal is a layered mean shift on the gene-level $z$ scale (Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}, last column): SSS shifts $15\%$ of genes by $3.0$; SGP $5\%$ by $4.0$; MDS $60\%$ by $1.1$; WDE $90\%$ by $0.55$; and MSA shifts $10\%$ by $1.8$ plus $60\%$ by $1.1$. Each layer contains at least one gene, so at $m=5$ the sparse archetypes shift exactly one gene, and MSA shifts one gene strongly and three moderately. Shifted genes are resampled each replicate, so signal does not consistently hit the same genes or LD blocks. All shifts are multiplied by an effect scale in $\{0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,2.5,3.0\}$, spanning undetectable to saturated regimes at the considered pathway sizes.

The null bank, five components, soft TFisher grid, undefined-component rule, and omnibus follow Section~2. For each archetype, $m$, and effect scale, we record (for each component and the omnibus) the fraction of replicates in which the signal pathway exceeds its own Westfall--Young FWER threshold at $\alpha=0.05$. A cell is informative if the best component’s power is in $(0.05,0.98)$; Table~\ref{tab:archverify} winners and the first/second/lower-place counts in Section~3.1 are computed over informative cells. Cell regret is the best single-component power minus the omnibus power.
