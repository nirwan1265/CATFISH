\abstract{
\textbf{Motivation:} Pathway analysis of genome-wide association study (GWAS)
summary statistics must contend with two distinct sources of dependence. Genes
within a pathway are correlated through linkage disequilibrium (LD), and
pathways are correlated with one another through shared gene membership. Most
pipelines address only the first, calibrating each pathway against its own
LD-aware null and then applying a Bonferroni or Benjamini--Hochberg correction
that assumes the resulting pathway $p$-values are independent. They are not,
and the correction is consequently miscalibrated in a direction that depends on
how strongly the pathway family overlaps.\\
\textbf{Results:} We extend CATFISH, a correlation-aware omnibus pathway
framework combining ACAT, Fisher, adaptive soft TFisher, minP and Stouffer
statistics, with a Westfall--Young-style family-wise calibration. A single
genome-wide correlated null draw, generated from a multivariate normal model of
gene--gene correlation, is propagated through every pathway in the family; read
down its columns it gives each pathway a calibrated marginal $p$-value, and read
across its rows the minimum over pathways gives a family-wise threshold that
accounts for between-pathway dependence without modelling it. Numerical
precision is treated as a correctness requirement: in a GWAS of $n\approx1.3$
million individuals the omnibus statistic underflows double precision for the
majority of pathways, and the resulting ties silently destroy the ranking.
Across simulated null families spanning three LD regimes and three pathway
sizes, independence-based calibration was anti-conservative while per-pathway
and Westfall--Young calibration held type~I and family-wise error near nominal,
and both remained stable when up to four of the five component tests were
deliberately miscalibrated. Under five stylised signal architectures, the
component predicted to be sensitive to each was the best-performing component in
$\nArchCellsIn$ of $\nArchCellsTot$ architecture-by-size combinations. Applied to a maize
grain-nitrogen GWAS, the Westfall--Young ordering recovered L-homoserine
biosynthesis as the leading pathway and placed a known aspartate kinase isozyme
first among 34,873 genes. Applied to GLGC LDL- and HDL-cholesterol GWAS, it
exposed a regime in which the self-contained null is false almost everywhere:
11.2\% of genes reach genome-wide significance for LDL-C and essentially every
pathway is significant, so significance no longer discriminates. The ordering
still does: plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling and clearance ranked first
of $\nLipNpw$ Reactome pathways for both LDL-C and HDL-C, and the combined
gene score ranked \textit{LDLR} and \textit{ABCA1} first and recovered
established lipid genes better than ranking by GWAS association alone. In a
sorghum bioenergy panel of 330 lines, where gene--gene correlation is far
denser than in the human data, no pathway was significant for dry biomass, and
a phenotype-permutation null showed that centring the gene $z$-scores of each
GWAS calibrates every pathway, while the family-wise error stays above nominal
because the variant-level tests are heavy-tailed. We argue
that the saturation is a property of self-contained testing at very large $n$,
not a failure of multiple-testing correction.\\
\textbf{Availability:} CATFISH is an R package available at
\url{https://github.com/nirwan1265/CATFISH} under the MIT licence. All
simulation, analysis and figure code for this paper is in the
\texttt{catfish2/} directory of that repository.\\
\textbf{Contact:} \href{mailto:ntanduk@ncsu.edu}{ntanduk@ncsu.edu}\\
\textbf{Supplementary information:} Supplementary data are available at
\textit{Genome Biology} online.}

\boxedtext{Key Messages}{
\begin{itemize}
\item Pathway $p$-values are correlated because pathways share genes;
Bonferroni and Benjamini--Hochberg corrections applied to them are therefore
miscalibrated, and the direction of the error depends on the overlap structure
of the particular pathway database used.
\item A Westfall--Young max-statistic null, built by propagating one
genome-wide correlated draw through the whole pathway family, controls the
family-wise error rate in the strong sense without assuming the pathways are
independent; its validity rests instead on the multivariate normal correlation
model used to generate the draw.
\item Implementing the omnibus in log space is a correctness requirement, not an
optimisation: at GWAS sample sizes above roughly one million the analytic
omnibus underflows double precision and the pathway ranking collapses into ties.
\item Self-contained pathway tests saturate at very large $n$. When 11\% of all
genes are genome-wide significant, a random 50-gene set contains several of
them, almost every pathway rejects, and exact FWER control does not restore
discrimination. Saturation is a property of the hypothesis being tested, not of
the correction applied to it.
\end{itemize}}

\section{1 Introduction}

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have transformed complex trait research
by identifying thousands of loci linked to disease, development, and
quantitative phenotypes. Yet most variants explain only a small fraction of
phenotypic variance and often fall in noncoding regions, limiting single-marker
mechanistic interpretation \citep{Manolio2009,Boyle2017}. Complex traits are
often highly polygenic, with relevant signals distributed across many
modest-effect loci rather than concentrated in a few genome-wide significant
variants \citep{Akula2025,Lappalainen2024}. Gene-set analysis, including
pathway-based approaches, is therefore a key post-GWAS strategy for translating
summary statistics into biological hypotheses. By aggregating evidence across
predefined groups of functionally related genes, pathway-based methods enhance
interpretability, reduce the multiple-testing burden, and reveal coordinated
pathway disruptions that single-locus analyses may miss
\citep{Mooney2015,deLeeuw2015}. This is especially valuable for polygenic
traits, where many genes may influence the same phenotype through shared
biological processes. In such settings, a candidate gene becomes more compelling
when locus- or gene-level association evidence is reinforced by pathway-level
enrichment from a biologically relevant process. Pathway support therefore
provides functional context for prioritization, although it is best interpreted
as complementary rather than definitive evidence because pathway annotations
remain incomplete and often overlapping.

A plethora of pathway analysis tools has been developed for GWAS. Classical
over-representation methods test whether predefined pathways are enriched among
significant genes, as implemented in DAVID, g:Profiler, and WebGestalt
\citep{Sherman2022,Kolberg2023,Liao2019}. Ranking-based methods such as Gene Set
Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) assess whether pathway genes are non-randomly
concentrated at the top or bottom of a ranked gene list
\citep{Subramanian2005}. GWAS-focused extensions, including MAGENTA, INRICH, and
MAGMA, add features such as SNP-to-gene mapping, pathway-level modeling, and
adjustment for linkage disequilibrium (LD) or gene size
\citep{Segre2010,Lee2012,deLeeuw2015}. Accounting for LD is essential in GWAS
pathway analysis because nearby variants are often correlated and therefore do
not provide independent evidence of association. If LD is ignored, clusters of
linked SNPs can be effectively counted multiple times, leading to inflated
gene-level aggregation and enrichment biases toward larger genes or regions with
dense marker coverage. Several widely used post-GWAS methods therefore
explicitly account for LD through model-based or reference-based dependence
estimates rather than assuming independence. VEGAS uses multivariate normal
simulation, Pascal uses LD from a reference population to compute gene and
pathway scores, and MAGMA models gene-level dependence through an approximated
correlation structure
\citep{Liu2010,deLeeuw2015,Lamparter2016,Watanabe2017}.

These methods address dependence \emph{within} a pathway. A second source of
dependence operates \emph{between} pathways and is far less often handled.
Curated pathway databases are built from overlapping annotations: a gene that
participates in several metabolic steps is a member of several pathways, and
hierarchical resources such as Reactome place parent and child pathways in the
same family. The pathway-level $p$-values returned by any method are therefore
correlated with one another, sometimes strongly. Nonetheless, the final
multiple-testing step in essentially every published pipeline is a Bonferroni or
Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment of those $p$-values, both of which are derived
under independence or under positive-regression dependence conditions that
overlapping gene sets do not generally satisfy. The resulting family-wise error
rate is not the nominal one, and the size of the discrepancy is a property of
the particular database rather than of the method, which makes it invisible to
the kind of simulation study that generates independent pathways.

The classical solution to exactly this problem is the Westfall--Young
max-statistic procedure \citep{Westfall1993}. Rather than correcting each
$p$-value in isolation, one generates a null realization of the \emph{entire}
family of tests, records the single most extreme statistic observed in that
realization, and repeats. The distribution of that maximum is the reference
distribution for the observed minimum, and the resulting threshold controls the
family-wise error rate under the complete null whatever the dependence structure
among the tests, because the dependence is inherited from the way the null
realizations are generated rather than assumed. In a GWAS pathway setting this means drawing
correlated gene-level null statistics genome-wide, propagating that single draw
through every pathway in the family, and recording the minimum pathway
$p$-value. Nothing about the between-pathway correlation needs to be estimated,
modelled, or even named.

Pathway tests also differ in the question they ask. A self-contained test asks
whether the genes of a pathway are associated with the trait at all; its null
hypothesis is that none of them is. A competitive test asks whether the genes of
a pathway are more strongly associated than genes outside it; its null
hypothesis is that pathway genes are no more associated than the rest of the
genome \citep{Goeman2007,deLeeuw2015}. The two can disagree: a pathway carried
by one strongly associated gene is significant under a self-contained test but
not under a competitive one, and in a highly polygenic GWAS almost every pathway
contains associated genes, so the self-contained null is false nearly
everywhere while a competitive test can still single out the pathways that
stand out from the genome. CATFISH provides both tests, each with family-wise
control, and a joint call for pathways significant under both.

Here we extend CATFISH (Combining \textbf{C}auchy combination test (ACAT),
\textbf{A}daptive \textbf{T}Fisher (soft) test, \textbf{F}isher's test,
m\textbf{I}n-P, and \textbf{S}touffer's method for \textbf{H}olistic pathway
analysis), a correlation-aware pathway analysis framework that integrates
multiple complementary gene-set statistics in a unified omnibus strategy, with
Westfall--Young family-wise calibration. Different pathway tests are sensitive
to different genetic architectures: some are best at detecting broad,
distributed enrichment, whereas others are more sensitive to sparse strong
signals or heterogeneous evidence. Instead of relying on a single summary
statistic, CATFISH evaluates each pathway with several complementary tests and
combines them in an omnibus framework. This improves robustness when the true
signal configuration is unknown while preserving the interpretability of the
component tests. Beyond pathway-level inference, CATFISH prioritizes candidate
genes by integrating locus-level GWAS signals, gene-level MAGMA results, and
pathway-level support.

This paper makes three contributions beyond the original CATFISH framework.
First, we add the Westfall--Young layer described above and characterize how it
behaves: the same correlated null, read down its columns, supplies each
pathway's marginal $p$-value, and read across its rows, the family-wise one. Second, we show that the entire statistic must be computed in log
space. This sounds like an implementation detail and is not: at the sample sizes
now routine in human GWAS consortia, the analytic omnibus $p$-value for a
typical pathway falls below the smallest representable double-precision number,
every such pathway is assigned the same floor value, and the ranking that the
whole analysis exists to produce is destroyed without any error being raised.
Third, we report a result we believe is general. In GLGC LDL- and
HDL-cholesterol GWAS of roughly 1.3 million individuals, the self-contained null
hypothesis is false for almost every gene set that could be constructed and
nearly every pathway is significant, so family-wise significance no longer
discriminates between pathways. We interpret this as saturation of the
self-contained hypothesis rather than as a defect of the correction. The
ordering of pathways, read against their size, still recovers lipoprotein
metabolism in both traits, and the gene-level prioritization recovers the
established genes of each trait better than GWAS association alone. We discuss
what saturation implies for the choice between self-contained and competitive
testing as GWAS sample sizes continue to grow.

CATFISH is implemented as an R package and is freely available at
\url{https://github.com/nirwan1265/CATFISH} under the MIT licence.

\subsection*{1.1 Pathway signal archetypes}

A pathway can be associated with a trait in very different ways. Its evidence
may come from one extreme gene, from a few strong genes, from many genes of
moderate effect, or from a faint shift shared by nearly all of its members, and
the component tests of CATFISH are sensitive to different parts of that range.
We formalise the range as five pathway signal archetypes: Strong Sparse Signal
(SSS), Single-Gene Proxy (SGP), Moderate Dense Signal (MDS), Weak Diffuse
Enrichment (WDE) and Mixed Signal Architecture (MSA). They are statistical
templates for interpreting test behaviour, not empirically learned classes of
real pathways, and they are the reason CATFISH combines complementary components
instead of choosing one in advance. Section~3.1 defines the archetypes, relates
each to a biological setting in which it is expected, and tests the predicted
correspondence between archetype and component under the family-wise decision
rule used throughout this paper. Supplementary Note~S1 gives the expanded
descriptions and the simulation design.

\section{2 Methods}

This section gives the essentials of the method and analyses. Derivations, implementation details, settings and scripts are in the Supplementary Methods, whose sections SM1--SM7 follow Sections~2.1--2.7.

\subsection*{2.1 Gene-level inputs and component tests}

For a pathway $S$ with $m=|S|$ genes, CATFISH uses MAGMA \citep{deLeeuw2015} gene-level $p$-values $p_g$ and $z$-scores $Z_g$ ($g\in S$), which summarise GWAS evidence while accounting for LD, gene size and marker density, and provide LD-induced gene–gene correlations. Each pathway is tested with five component tests (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:components}): ACAT \citep{Liu2019ACATV,Liu2020ACAT}, Fisher \citep{Fisher1932}, soft TFisher \citep{Zhang2020TFisher}, minP and Stouffer \citep{Stouffer1949}. These target different signal patterns, from a single strong gene (minP, ACAT) to a weak shift across many genes (Stouffer) (Section~3.1). Component $p$-values are combined into an omnibus statistic via an ACAT-type operator $\mathcal{O}(\cdot)$. Only soft TFisher is tuned: it keeps genes with $p_g\le\tau$ and adapts over $\tau\in\{10^{-2},\,5\times10^{-2},\,5\times10^{-1}\}$; $\tau=1$ corresponds to Fisher and is included as a separate component. If no gene meets any threshold, soft TFisher is undefined and the omnibus uses the remaining components; its $p$-value is capped at $10^{-16}$ (Supplementary Methods SM1).



\subsection*{2.2 Two sources of dependence}

Pathway statistics are correlated at two levels. Within a pathway, the five components are functions of the same gene $p$-values, and those $p$-values are correlated through LD, so any analytic reference distribution that assumes independent genes is wrong for every component. Between pathways, databases assign genes to several sets, so a gene shared by several pathways moves them together, and Bonferroni or Benjamini--Hochberg corrections need not hold their nominal error rate. CATFISH handles both with one device: genome-wide null draws of correlated gene $z$-scores, each scored through every pathway at once. The construction assumes that null gene $z$-scores are approximately multivariate normal, that the gene pairs MAGMA reports approximate the gene--gene correlation adequately (unreported pairs are set to zero), and that the gene-level inputs are not driven by gene size or SNP density (Supplementary Methods SM2).

\subsection*{2.3 Calibration and family-wise control from a shared correlated null}

For each chromosome $c$, the gene--gene correlations reported by MAGMA form a correlation matrix $R_c$; genes on different chromosomes are independent, and matrices that are not positive definite are repaired by flooring their eigenvalues. Writing $R_c=U_c^{\top}U_c$, null replicate $b$ draws
\[
\mathbf{Z}^{(b)}_c = U_c^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m_c}),
\]
for every chromosome, giving one genome-wide set of null gene $z$-scores with the LD between genes but no association, and null $p$-values $p_g^{(b)}=1-\Phi(Z_g^{(b)})$. Every pathway $S_k$ of the family $\mathcal{F}=\{S_1,\dots,S_K\}$ is scored on every replicate, giving null omnibus values $Q^{(b)}_k=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S_k))$ for $b=1,\dots,B$ and the observed value $q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k$ from the real data. Two $p$-values are read from this one matrix. The per-pathway $p$-value compares a pathway with its own null values,
\[
\tilde p_k=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(Q^{(b)}_k\le q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k\right)}{B+1},
\]
and asks whether that pathway is enriched. The family-wise $p$-value compares it with the smallest value over all pathways in each replicate, $M^{(b)}=\min_k Q^{(b)}_k$,
\[
\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{1}\!\left(M^{(b)}\le q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k\right)}{B+1},
\]
and asks whether evidence this strong would appear anywhere among the $K$ pathways by chance. This is the Westfall--Young procedure \citep{Westfall1993}. Because all pathways are scored on the same draws, the dependence created by shared genes is in the null without being modelled, and rejecting pathways with $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k\le\alpha$ controls the family-wise error rate in the strong sense (Supplementary Methods SM3). Applying $\mathcal{O}$ to the observed components with their analytic reference distributions gives the analytic omnibus, which assumes independent genes; we report it as a baseline. Because Fisher-type statistics grow with pathway size, the family minimum is taken on standardised values $z_k=(q^{\mathrm{obs}}_k-\mu_k)/\sigma_k$, where $\mu_k$ and $\sigma_k$ are the mean and standard deviation of pathway $k$'s null values; $z_k$ (written $z_{\mathrm{std}}$ in the Results) is also the statistic used to rank pathways.

\emph{Competitive test.} The self-contained test’s null is that no gene in the pathway is associated. The competitive test asks whether pathway genes are more associated than other genes in the same GWAS. Its null shifts observed gene scores along the genome (in chromosome order) by a single random offset shared across pathways, preserving LD between neighbouring genes and the genome-wide score distribution, including polygenic signal. The two $p$-values are then read from the null matrix as above. Calling a pathway by both tests at level $\alpha$ controls the family-wise error of the joint claim (an intersection--union test \citep{Berger1982}).

\subsection*{2.4 Numerical implementation in log space}

In large GWAS, gene $p$-values fall below the smallest positive double-precision number ($\approx2.2\times10^{-308}$); in the GLGC LDL-C data, $473$ chromosome~1 variants have a published $p$-value of exactly zero. Combining such genes makes pathway $p$-values underflow to zero, ties the strongest pathways and silently corrupts their ranking; in our first implementation, the top pathways were in alphabetical order. CATFISH therefore computes every component and the omnibus on $\log p$, with asymptotic forms for ACAT and minP in the extreme tail, so pathway $p$-values keep their order down to about $10^{-324}$ (Supplementary Methods SM4).

\subsection*{2.5 Candidate-gene prioritization}

CATFISH ranks genes by a score over three layers of evidence,
\[
\mathrm{Score}(g)=S_{\mathrm{GWAS}}(g)+S_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}(g)+S_{\mathrm{PATH}}(g),\qquad S_\bullet(g)=-\log_{10}\!\left(r_\bullet(g)/N_\bullet\right),
\]
where $r_{\mathrm{GWAS}}$ is the gene's rank by its strongest variant, $r_{\mathrm{MAGMA}}$ its rank by its MAGMA $p$-value, $r_{\mathrm{PATH}}$ the rank of its best pathway by $z_k$, and $N_\bullet$ the number of genes ranked in that layer; a gene missing a layer scores zero for it. Each term is $-\log_{10}$ of a rank-based empirical $p$-value, the fraction of genes ranked at least as high on that layer, so the score combines the three layers with equal weight, as Fisher's method combines $p$-values. It has no tuned parameter and applies unchanged to any species or pathway database, which matters because most species have no curated causal genes on which layer weights could be learned. We examined other pathway weights in a simulation with planted causal genes and in the real data (Section~3.5; Supplementary Methods SM5). To measure what the pathway layer adds, genes were also ranked without it (pathway weight $0$; GWAS+MAGMA). Because a gene's own association contributes to the evidence of its pathways, we also recomputed the pathway evidence of selected genes with each gene left out of its own pathways (leave one gene out), keeping every other gene unchanged; a gene's pathway evidence without itself was never taken as stronger than with itself (Supplementary Methods SM5). The score is a ranking, not an inferential model: it has no $p$-value.

\subsection*{2.6 Simulation design}

We evaluated calibration and power in two simulation designs (settings and scripts in Supplementary Methods SM6). First, we drew null gene $z$-scores directly. In the shared-universe design, $3{,}000$ genes were arranged in blocks of $50$ with within-block correlation $\rho$ (mimicking chromosomes/LD blocks), and $K=150$ pathways of $m$ genes were sampled to induce realistic overlap. In the exchangeable design, all genes within a pathway had correlation $\rho$, providing a stress test for within-pathway correlation. For $\rho\in\{0,0.3,0.6\}$ and $m\in\{5,20,50\}$, thresholds were fit on one set of draws and evaluated on another, yielding six studies:
\begin{enumerate}
\item null calibration: per-pathway type~I error and family-wise error of the analytic omnibus, per-pathway calibration, Bonferroni and Westfall--Young under the global null (exchangeable design);
\item component breakage: up to four of the five components miscalibrated, by replacing their $p$-values with $p^{\gamma}$ ($\gamma=0.7$ or $1.4$);
\item leave one component out: each component removed in turn and the calibration recomputed;
\item per-component calibration: each component calibrated and used alone;
\item an incomplete correlation file: $0$--$100\%$ of gene pairs missing from the matrix used for calibration;
\item power: one pathway per family carrying one of the five signal types of Section~3.1 (Supplementary Note~S1), the other $149$ null.
\end{enumerate}
Five signal types describe how association spreads across a pathway’s genes, with biological examples (Supplementary Note~S1). Strong sparse signal (SSS) involves a few strongly associated genes, e.g., a committed-step enzyme such as aspartokinase. Single-gene proxy (SGP) is the extreme: one dominant gene, e.g., \textit{PAH} in phenylalanine metabolism. Moderate dense signal (MDS) has many moderately associated genes, e.g., cytokine signalling to NF-$\kappa$B. Weak diffuse enrichment (WDE) shifts nearly all genes slightly, e.g., growth pathways for human height. Mixed signal (MSA) combines a few strong genes with many moderate ones, e.g., \textit{LDLR}, \textit{APOB} and \textit{PCSK9} plus many common lipid variants for LDL-C.

A second simulation generated GWAS summary statistics using real LD to compare CATFISH with MAGMA gene-set analysis and preranked gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) under known truth. SNP $z$-scores were simulated from 1000 Genomes European LD ($503$ individuals; chromosomes 1--10; $748{,}822$ HapMap3 SNPs). For each signal type, causal SNPs were placed in one target pathway, plus a polygenic background where $2\%$ of genes contained one causal SNP. Across $850$ replicates ($17$ scenario--size combinations), we ran MAGMA (SNP-wise mean; $9{,}645$ genes; $1{,}021$ Reactome pathways), CATFISH ($B=2{,}000$) and preranked GSEA \citep{Subramanian2005} on the MAGMA gene $z$-scores (fgsea \citep{Korotkevich2021fgsea}), with Bonferroni correction for MAGMA and GSEA. Power was the fraction of replicates detecting the target pathway; false calls were pathways with no gene within $1$~Mb of a target causal SNP.

\subsection*{2.7 Real-data analyses}

\textbf{Human lipids.} We used Global Lipids Genetics Consortium LDL-C and HDL-C meta-analyses \citep{Graham2021GLGC} across five ancestry groups (European, $n\approx1.3$ million; African, East Asian, South Asian and Hispanic; Section~3.4), yielding ten trait--ancestry datasets. Gene analysis used MAGMA v1.10 (NCBI37.3 genes, $\pm10$~kb windows, SNP-wise mean), run per chromosome and merged to preserve gene--gene correlations. Pathways were Reactome from MSigDB C2:CP ($1{,}390$ after size filtering). We compared CATFISH with MAGMA competitive and self-contained gene-set tests and with preranked GSEA on the same gene statistics and pathways; MAGMA and GSEA results used Bonferroni correction.

\textbf{Nine-trait benchmark.} To assess the gene score beyond lipids, we added seven European GWAS with GWAS Catalog-harmonised summary statistics: coronary artery disease \citep{vanderHarst2018CAD}, breast cancer \citep{Michailidou2017BRCA}, Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis \citep{deLange2017IBD}, atrial fibrillation \citep{Nielsen2018AF}, systemic lupus erythematosus \citep{Bentham2015SLE} and asthma \citep{Valette2021Asthma}, analysed as for European lipids. Known genes were Open Targets gold-standard genes (2019 and 2023 releases) \citep{Mountjoy2021L2G}, curated independently and used only for evaluation. We compared six rankings: CATFISH, CATFISH without pathways, strongest variant, MAGMA $p$-value, PoPS \citep{Weeks2023PoPS} (default), and the mean CATFISH+PoPS rank, counting gold-standard genes in the top 20/50/100. All rankings were compared within the same genes: those scored by both CATFISH and PoPS (matched by gene symbol), outside the extended MHC (chr6, $25$--$34$~Mb), which PoPS leaves out and whose long-range LD fills the top of every list in immune traits. The lipid gold-standard comparisons and the leave-one-gene-out check (Section~3.5) use the same genes; analyses including all genes are also reported. For each trait, we compared the gene-score top 50 with the strongest-variant top 50, and reviewed the literature for the top 10 genes, classifying each as supported by genetic/functional evidence, at a reported locus with unresolved causal gene, or resolved to a neighbouring gene (Supplementary Methods SM7).

\textbf{Sorghum.} We analysed the sorghum Bioenergy Association Panel \citep{Brenton2016} (363 genotyped lines). Dry biomass (327 lines) and stem traits (Brix at maturity, NDF, and NFC) were tested with GEMMA’s linear mixed model \citep{Zhou2012GEMMA}. Gene analysis used MAGMA (BTx623 v3 models; \(\pm25\) kb windows; SNP-wise mean), and pathways came from SorghumBicolorCyc \citep{Hawkins2021CornCyc} (5–200 genes). For calibration on real genotypes, we permuted dry biomass across lines 100 times and ran the full pipeline so all pathways were null. For the shared chromosome 4 locus, we estimated the lead-allele effect on each trait and quantified how much of its NDF effect is explained by the sugar–fibre trade-off by regressing NDF on allele count with and without Brix; the share explained was \(1-b_{\mathrm{adj}}/b\) \citep{VanderWeele2016Mediation}. Leave-one-gene-out was applied to each pathway-supported gene in each trait’s top 100 (Supplementary Methods SM7).

\textbf{Gene-level genomic control.} Before pathway testing, we centred gene \(p\)-values: \(p_g \rightarrow z_g=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_g)\), subtract the GWAS-wide median \(\hat\delta\), then convert back. This location genomic control \citep{Devlin1999} applies one constant per GWAS while preserving gene ranks and gene–gene correlations. We included it because permutation nulls showed GWAS-specific shifts in gene \(z\)-scores that self-contained tests can misinterpret as pathway signal (Section 3.6). Competitive tests were also run on the same centred inputs (\(B=10{,}000\) shifts).

\section{3 Results}

\subsection*{3.1 Five potential pathway signal types, the component tests that detect them, and an omnibus that performs well across all five}

CATFISH is a pathway analysis and gene prioritization tool using GWAS summary statistics. It applies five component tests per pathway (ACAT, Fisher, Stouffer, minP, soft TFisher), each tuned to different gene-level evidence patterns, and combines their $p$-values into a single omnibus $p$-value using the Cauchy combination. The resulting analytic omnibus $p$-value is based on component analytic nulls that assume genes within a pathway are independent. CATFISH instead calibrates the omnibus using correlated null draws, which are simulated genome-wide gene $z$-scores that preserve LD-induced gene correlations and contain no associations, and every pathway is scored in each draw. A pathway's $p$-value compares its observed omnibus to its own null distribution, and its Westfall–Young family-wise $p$-value compares it to the minimum omnibus across pathways in each draw, controlling the probability of any false positives among all tested pathways (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a, Algorithm~\ref{alg:catfish}).

We define five pathway signal types from ranked gene-level profiles (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b, Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}, Supplementary Note~S1). Strong sparse signal (SSS) is driven by a few key genes, such as those encoding committed-step enzymes, rate-limiting regulators, or essential transporters \citep{MorenoSanchez2008,Naz2023,Tanner2018,Song2020}. Single-gene proxy (SGP) is the extreme case, with one gene carrying the signal. Moderate dense signal (MDS) reflects modest contributions from many interacting genes, as in cytokine signaling to NF-$\kappa$B \citep{Jiang2021CytoSig,Verstrepen2008NFkB}. Weak diffuse enrichment (WDE) reflects highly polygenic traits such as height \citep{Yengo2022HeightSaturation}. Mixed signal architecture (MSA) combines a few large-effect genes with many modest-effect genes, as in LDL-cholesterol metabolism (Section~3.4) \citep{Chasman2009LipoproteinProfiles,Peloso2022WGSbloodlipids}. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b compares expected profiles for a $50$-gene pathway with the dashed null. SSS has a few genes at $-\log_{10}p\approx4$--$5$ and the rest near null. SGP has one standout, while MDS has a broad shoulder of moderately small $p$-values across about half the genes. WDE lies slightly above null throughout. Finally, MSA pairs a strong head with a moderate shoulder. These profiles act as templates. Real pathways can fall between types, and the type is unknown a priori.

Next, we tested whether the components we predicted to detect each signal type do so (Table~\ref{tab:archetype_summary}). We placed one signal pathway of each type among $149$ correlated null pathways, varied its effect from undetectable to saturated, and measured family-wise power, the fraction of replicates in which the signal pathway reached a family-wise $p\le0.05$. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c shows this power for each of the five components and for the omnibus, by signal type and pathway size. In $\nArchCellsIn$ of $\nArchCellsTot$ combinations of type and size, the most powerful component was the predicted one (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:archverify}). These were minP and ACAT for sparse and single-gene signals, Fisher, with soft TFisher close behind, for dense and mixed signals, and Stouffer for diffuse signals. No component was best for every type. The differences were largest at intermediate effects. With $20$ genes and an effect scale of $1.5$, Fisher, soft TFisher, and Stouffer detected dense signals in $74\%$, $72\%$, and $66\%$ of replicates, against $24\%$ for ACAT and $21\%$ for minP. For single-gene signals at the same setting, minP and ACAT reached $99\%$, and Stouffer $1\%$. For diffuse signals across $50$ genes, Stouffer ($70\%$) and Fisher ($66\%$) led, while ACAT and minP stayed below $10\%$. The omnibus tracked the best component rather than the average, reaching $73\%$, $92\%$, and $68\%$ in these three settings, and its power rose with effect size and pathway size as the best component's did. It fell behind mainly for weak single-gene signals. With $20$ genes and an effect scale of $1.0$, minP detected $62\%$ of single-gene signals and the omnibus $26\%$, because only minP and ACAT carried the signal, and the other three components diluted it.

Finally, we define regret as the within-cell power gap between the best component and the omnibus. Mean regret ranged from $\nArchRegretBest$ ($\nArchRegretBestA$) to $\nArchRegretWorst$ ($\nArchRegretWorstA$) and was below $0.03$ for four of the five signal types (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}d, where crosses mark the worst-cell regret, and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:archregret}). The gap between the best and the worst component often exceeded $0.5$, so choosing one component in advance risks losing that entire gap, while the omnibus loses only the regret. Thus, the five components cover diverse signals, and the omnibus stays near the best without knowing the signal type in advance. The main exception is single-gene signals, where the omnibus lost up to $\nArchMaxRegret$ in one cell, so these pathways are better reported as gene-level results.

%% ---- algorithm ----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{CATFISH self-contained test: per-pathway and family-wise $p$-values from one set of correlated null draws (Section~2.3).}
\label{alg:catfish}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require gene $p$-values $p_g$ from MAGMA for $G$ genes; MAGMA gene--gene correlations $r_{ij}$; pathways $\mathcal{F}=\{S_1,\dots,S_K\}$; number of null draws $B$; omnibus $\mathcal{O}$, the ACAT combination of ACAT, Fisher, Stouffer, minP and soft TFisher (truncation grid fixed in advance)
\Ensure per-pathway $p$-value $\tilde p_k$ and family-wise $p$-value $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k$ for every pathway
\State $z_g \gets \Phi^{-1}(1-p_g)$ computed from $\log p_g$; $\;p_g \gets 1-\Phi\{z_g-\mathrm{median}(z)\}$ \Comment{gene-level genomic control}
\For{each chromosome $c$}
  \State $R_c \gets I + \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}_c} r_{ij}\,(e_ie_j^{\top}+e_je_i^{\top})$; floor eigenvalues at $10^{-6}$ if not positive definite
  \State $R_c = U_c^{\top}U_c$ \Comment{Cholesky factor}
\EndFor
\For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
  \State $q_k \gets \mathcal{O}\{\mathbf{p}(S_k)\}$ \Comment{observed omnibus, in log space}
\EndFor
\For{$b=1,\dots,B$}
  \State $\mathbf{Z}^{(b)}_c \gets U_c^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_c$, $\;\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_c\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, independently for each chromosome \Comment{one genome-wide draw}\label{alg:draw}
  \State $p^{(b)}_g \gets 1-\Phi(Z^{(b)}_g)$ for every gene\label{alg:nullp}
  \For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
    \State $Q^{(b)}_k \gets \mathcal{O}\{\mathbf{p}^{(b)}(S_k)\}$ \Comment{every pathway scored on the same draw}
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
  \State $\tilde p_k \gets \bigl\{1+\sum_{b}\mathbf{1}(Q^{(b)}_k\le q_k)\bigr\}/(B+1)$ \Comment{per-pathway $p$-value}
  \State $\mu_k,\sigma_k \gets$ mean and SD of $Q^{(1)}_k,\dots,Q^{(B)}_k$; $\;z_k \gets (q_k-\mu_k)/\sigma_k$; $\;\zeta^{(b)}_k \gets (Q^{(b)}_k-\mu_k)/\sigma_k$
\EndFor
\For{$b=1,\dots,B$}
  \State $M^{(b)} \gets \min_k \zeta^{(b)}_k$ \Comment{family minimum of one draw}
\EndFor
\For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
  \State $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k \gets \bigl\{1+\sum_{b}\mathbf{1}(M^{(b)}\le z_k)\bigr\}/(B+1)$ \Comment{Westfall--Young; ties count as extreme}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\tilde p_k$, $\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY}}_k$ and $z_k$ (the pathway statistic $z_{\mathrm{std}}$ used by the gene score)
\end{algorithmic}
\vspace{2pt}
\footnotesize Competitive test: lines~\ref{alg:draw}--\ref{alg:nullp} are replaced by one circular shift $s$ of the observed gene $p$-values along the genome order, shared by all pathways ($p^{(b)}_g \gets p_{g+s}$); everything else is unchanged. A pathway is called by both tests when $\max(\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY,self}}_k,\tilde p^{\mathrm{WY,comp}}_k)\le\alpha$.
\end{algorithm}

\subsection*{3.2 CATFISH controls false positives under linkage disequilibrium and detects sparse pathway signals more often than MAGMA}

We next examined whether CATFISH keeps the nominal false positive rate when genes within a pathway are correlated, a setting in which the analytic omnibus $p$-value is expected to fail because it assumes the genes are independent. We simulated independent pathways of $m\in\{5,20,50\}$ genes with within-pathway correlation $\rho\in\{0,0.3,0.6\}$, using $500$ families per setting. We compared two per-pathway $p$-values, the analytic $p$-value and the CATFISH per-pathway $p$-value, and two family-wise $p$-values, the analytic $p$-value with Bonferroni correction and the CATFISH family-wise $p$-value, with both CATFISH values taken from the same correlated null draws. Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}a shows the error rate of each at $\alpha=0.05$, by $\rho$ and $m$, as type~I error for the per-pathway $p$-values and family-wise error for the family-wise $p$-values (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:null_calibration}). The analytic error was $\nTAnInd$ at $\rho=0$, $m=5$, close to $0.05$, but rose with $\rho$ in every panel and most in larger pathways, reaching $\nTAnStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$. Correlated genes carry less independent evidence than the analytic test assumes, so it overstates the evidence. Bonferroni correction, the usual family-wise correction, made this much worse. Its family-wise error was $\nTBonfInd$ at $\rho=0$, $m=5$, but at least $0.68$ in every setting with correlation, reaching $\nTBonfMod$ at $\rho=0.3$, $m=20$ and $\nTBonfStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, $m=50$. Bonferroni corrects for the number of pathways but not for the miscalibrated $p$-value it is applied to, and with $200$ pathways per family even a small excess of small null $p$-values makes at least one false call almost certain. Both CATFISH curves stayed near the dashed $0.05$ line. The CATFISH per-pathway error stayed within $[\nTMvnMin,\nTMvnMax]$, and at $\alpha=0.01$ the analytic rate reached $\nTAnMaxOne$ against $\nTMvnMaxOne$ for CATFISH. The CATFISH family-wise error, estimated out of sample by learning the null distribution of the family minimum on half the families and applying it to the other half, ranged from $\nTWyMin$ to $\nTWyMax$, within Monte Carlo error for $250$ held-out families (SE $\approx 0.014$).

GWAS inflation is often measured by the genomic-control $\lambda$, the observed median $\chi^2_1$ statistic divided by its null expectation ($\lambda\approx 1$ suggests calibration). Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}b shows $\lambda$ for the analytic (black) and CATFISH per-pathway (white) $p$-values. The analytic $\lambda$ declined as correlation and pathway size increased, falling at $m=50$ from $\nLamAnInd$ at $\rho=0$ to $\nLamAnStr$ at $\rho=0.6$, the setting with the highest type~I error, whereas the CATFISH $\lambda$ stayed near one ($[\nLamMvnMin,\nLamMvnMax]$). Strong within-pathway correlation makes small null $p$-values more frequent while pushing their median above $0.5$. Because $\lambda$ reflects the median rather than the lower tail, it can miss type~I error inflation. Hence, $\lambda\le 1$ does not guarantee calibration in the tail, where pathways are called.

The simulations so far drew gene $z$-scores from a correlation model. To evaluate CATFISH with real LD and known enriched pathways, we simulated GWAS summary statistics on real genotypes, specifically those of $503$ European individuals from the 1000 Genomes Project (chromosomes 1--10, $748{,}822$ HapMap3 SNPs), placing causal SNPs in one target pathway and a polygenic background in which $2\%$ of genes carried one causal SNP (Section~2.6). In $50$ replicates per setting, we ran self-contained and competitive tests with CATFISH and MAGMA, preranked GSEA on the MAGMA gene $z$-scores, and the CATFISH joint call, which calls a pathway only when both CATFISH tests do. Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}c shows, for the two settings with no enriched pathway, the proportion of replicates with at least one false call and its exact $95\%$ binomial interval (Table~\ref{tab:sim_compare}). With no signal, the CATFISH self-contained test made false calls in $0/50$ replicates compared to $8/50$ for MAGMA (family-wise error $0.16$, $95\%$ CI $0.07$--$0.29$). Both competitive tests had a false-call rate of $0.06$, and the joint call made none. With only a polygenic background, where competitive tests should make no calls, the CATFISH competitive test and joint call each erred in $4/50$ replicates ($0.08$, $95\%$ CI $0.02$--$0.19$), while the MAGMA competitive test made no false calls. GSEA, whose random-gene-set null treats genes as independent, made false calls in $15/50$ replicates with no signal ($0.30$, $95\%$ CI $0.18$--$0.45$, with a mean of $5.5$ false pathways per replicate) and $33/50$ with only a polygenic background ($0.66$). Only the MAGMA self-contained test and GSEA had $95\%$ intervals entirely above $0.05$.

We then compared power by signal type across small, medium, and large pathways. Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}d shows the power of the CATFISH and MAGMA self-contained tests to call the enriched pathway. Overall, CATFISH matched or exceeded MAGMA in $14$ of the $15$ combinations of signal type and size, with its largest gains for sparse signals in small and medium pathways. In large pathways, both methods were near full power for most types. CATFISH detected strong sparse pathways of small size in $98\%$ of replicates against $34\%$ for MAGMA, and single-gene pathways in $92$--$100\%$ against $0\%$, $18\%$, and $78\%$ (small, medium, and large). Results were similar for dense and mixed signals, and MAGMA was slightly better only for weak diffuse signals at medium size ($76\%$ against $68\%$).

Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}e shows the power of the competitive tests. Power was lower for both methods, especially in small pathways, but the same pattern held. CATFISH detected strong sparse pathways in $66\%$, $76\%$, and $100\%$ of replicates (small, medium, and large) against $4\%$, $12\%$, and $36\%$ for MAGMA. For dense, diffuse, and mixed signals, the two were similar, with MAGMA performing better in small pathways ($36\%$ against $20\%$ for dense and $50\%$ against $42\%$ for mixed signals). Neither competitive test detected single-gene pathways in more than $4\%$ of replicates, because one gene is too few to make a pathway stand out from the rest of the genome. The self-contained test captures these pathways instead. GSEA had high power for dense and diffuse signals in medium and large pathways (for example, weak diffuse enrichment in large pathways in $96\%$ of replicates, against $32\%$ for CATFISH), but it detected strong sparse pathways in only $0\%$, $42\%$, and $100\%$ of replicates (small, medium, and large) and falsely identified a pathway in about half of all signal replicates, so its power cannot be separated from its false calls. Overall, CATFISH kept false positives at or near the nominal level in every null setting, matched MAGMA for dense, diffuse, and mixed signals, and was substantially more powerful for sparse signals, while GSEA, which ignores LD between genes, did not control false positives.

\subsection*{3.3 Calibration holds when components are corrupted or removed, and degrades predictably with an incomplete correlation file}

CATFISH is calibrated when its five components behave as expected. Here we asked whether it stays calibrated when they do not, using four tests on overlapping pathways (Fig.~\ref{fig:stress}). We made some component tests deliberately wrong, removed each component in turn, measured how much LD inflates each component on its own, and removed part of the gene--gene correlation file used to build the null draws. A $p$-value is calibrated at $0.05$ if $p\le 0.05$ in $5\%$ of tests with no true signal. For all four tests, we simulated $3{,}000$ genes in LD blocks of $50$ with within-block correlation $\rho$ and sampled $150$ pathways of $m$ genes, so that each gene belonged to $\nBrkMembership$ pathways on average, and pathways overlapped as they do in real databases. CATFISH learned its thresholds from one set of null draws, and error rates were measured on separate simulated datasets with no associated genes, so CATFISH was never scored on the draws it was calibrated on.

First, we made $k$ of the five components wrong on purpose ($k=0$ to $4$) by replacing their $p$-values with $p^{\gamma}$, in both the null draws and the evaluated datasets (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:breakage}). With $\gamma=1.4$ a corrupted component reports $p$-values that are too small, claiming more evidence than it has (anti-conservative), and with $\gamma=0.7$ $p$-values that are too large (conservative). This mimics a component whose analytic null does not fit the data. Fig.~\ref{fig:stress}a shows the error rates at $0.05$ as $k$ increases, by LD level (columns) and direction of corruption (rows), with line type for pathway size. With four anti-conservative components, the error of the analytic omnibus rose from $\nBrkAnKzero$ to $\nBrkAnKfour$, increasing with each corrupted component and most under strong LD (Fig.~\ref{fig:stress}a, top row), while the CATFISH per-pathway and family-wise errors held at $\nBrkMvnKfour$ and $\nBrkWyKfour$. With four conservative components, the analytic error fell to $\nBrkConsAnKfour$, and the CATFISH errors stayed at $\nBrkConsMvnKfour$ and $\nBrkConsWyKfour$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:stress}a, bottom row). Across all settings, the CATFISH family-wise and per-pathway errors stayed within $[\nBrkWyRangeLo,\nBrkWyRangeHi]$ and $[\nBrkMvnRangeLo,\nBrkMvnRangeHi]$. Fig.~\ref{fig:stress}b shows $\lambda$ for the same runs. The CATFISH per-pathway $\lambda$ stayed near one, while the analytic $\lambda$ shifted with the corruption. Because the same corruption is applied to the null draws and to the data, CATFISH cancels a component that is wrong by the same amount everywhere. It would not cancel an error that depends on the data, which we did not test.

Second, we removed each component in turn and rebuilt the null draws and thresholds without it ($m=50$, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:loo}). Fig.~\ref{fig:stress}c shows, for each removed component and LD level, the CATFISH per-pathway type I error, family-wise error, and per-pathway $\lambda$, with None marking the full omnibus. The per-pathway type~I error stayed within $[\nLooMvnMin,\nLooMvnMax]$ (full omnibus $\nLooFullMvn$), the family-wise error within $[\nLooWyMin,\nLooWyMax]$ (full omnibus $\nLooFullWy$), and $\lambda$ near one, so every removal matched the full omnibus. Averaged over pathway sizes and removed components, the family-wise error was $\nLooWyInd$, $\nLooWyMod$, and $\nLooWyStr$ under no, moderate, and strong LD. A user can therefore drop a component they do not trust without losing calibration, although dropping it costs power for the signal types that component detects best. We use all five by default.

Third, we asked which components LD inflates. Fig.~\ref{fig:stress}d shows each component on its own ($m=50$), with its type~I error under the independence assumption, its type~I error after calibration against the correlated null, and its analytic $\lambda$ (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:components_cal}). Under independence, all components except soft TFisher were near $0.05$. As LD strengthened, Fisher ($\nCmpFisherStr$) and Stouffer ($\nCmpStoufferStr$) exceeded the nominal rate and inflated the analytic omnibus ($\nCmpOmnibusStr$), while ACAT ($\nCmpACATStr$) and minP ($\nCmpminPStr$) stayed near $0.05$. Fisher and Stouffer add up evidence across genes and count correlated genes as repeated evidence, whereas ACAT and minP are driven by the top gene and give little extra weight to near-duplicates. Calibration against the correlated null brought all rates near $0.05$ (Fisher $\nCmpFisherCal$, Stouffer $\nCmpStoufferCal$, omnibus $\nCmpOmnibusCal$). The analytic $\lambda$ shows why $\lambda$ can mislead. Soft TFisher has $\lambda\approx 2$ even under independence because its uncalibrated statistic is a minimum over thresholds, not a $p$-value. A related detail pushes $\lambda$ the other way. When no gene in a pathway passes any truncation threshold, soft TFisher is undefined, and CATFISH leaves it out of that pathway's omnibus in both the observed data and every null draw. Assigning it $p=1$ instead would count in ACAT as evidence against the pathway, pushing $\lambda$ below $1$ while inflating the tail.

Finally, CATFISH builds its null draws from the gene--gene correlation file. MAGMA reports correlations only for nearby gene pairs with variants in the LD reference panel, and CATFISH treats any pair it does not report as uncorrelated, so part of the true correlation can be missing from the file. We generated data with all correlations and calibrated CATFISH after setting increasing fractions of gene pairs to zero (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:missing}). Fig.~\ref{fig:stress}e shows the error rates as pairs are removed. Both CATFISH curves start at $0.05$ and rise as pairs are removed, while the analytic rate remains flat because it does not use correlation. Under moderate LD, the per-pathway type~I and family-wise errors were $\nMissMvnZero$ and $\nMissWyZero$ with the complete correlations, $\nMissMvnHalf$ and $\nMissWyHalf$ with half the pairs removed, and $\nMissMvnFull$ and $\nMissWyFull$ with all pairs removed. At half the pairs missing, the family-wise error was $\nMissRatioWyHalf$ times nominal and the per-pathway error $\nMissRatioMvnHalf$ times nominal. Fig.~\ref{fig:stress}f shows the CATFISH per-pathway $\lambda$ for the same runs. It ranged from $\nMissLamLo$ to $\nMissLamHi$ and fell below $1$ as pairs were removed, again showing that $\lambda\le1$ can coexist with inflation in the tail. Overall, calibration was robust to corrupting or removing components and degraded gradually as correlations were lost. Users should therefore check what fraction of nearby gene pairs within their pathways have a reported correlation.

\subsection*{3.4 CATFISH ranks lipoprotein pathways first for blood lipids in five ancestry groups and recovers trait-relevant pathways in seven other human traits}

We applied CATFISH to GWAS summary statistics for two blood lipid traits, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), from the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium (GLGC) 2021 meta-analyses \citep{Graham2021GLGC}. These cover five ancestry groups, European (EUR, $n\approx1.32$ million), African (AFR, $n\approx99{,}400$), East Asian (EAS, $n\approx146{,}500$), South Asian (SAS, $n\approx41{,}000$) and Hispanic (HIS, $n\approx48{,}100$), giving ten trait--ancestry sets that differ in allele frequencies and LD. All ten were analyzed in the same way, with MAGMA gene statistics for $18{,}429$–$18{,}563$ genes, $1{,}390$ Reactome pathways, $B=10{,}000$ null draws, and gene-level genomic control. LDL carries cholesterol from the liver to peripheral tissues, where it is taken up through the LDL receptor \citep{BrownGoldstein1986}, and high LDL-C causes atherosclerotic coronary artery disease \citep{Ference2017LDL}. HDL carries cholesterol from peripheral tissues back to the liver \citep{Rosenson2012RCT}. Both traits are heritable \citep{Beekman2002Lipids} and highly polygenic, with hundreds of associated loci \citep{Graham2021GLGC}, and many of their genes encode lipoprotein components, receptors, and lipases of well-characterised function \citep{Chasman2009LipoproteinProfiles,Peloso2022WGSbloodlipids}. They therefore test whether CATFISH ranks lipoprotein pathways and known lipid genes highly. LD in human populations also extends over much shorter distances than in self-pollinating crops such as sorghum \citep{Reich2001LD,Morris2013Sorghum}, so gene--gene correlations in these data are comparatively weak.

CATFISH runs two tests on each pathway. The self-contained test asks whether the genes of a pathway are associated with the trait, and the competitive test asks whether they are more strongly associated than genes outside the pathway. We first describe the self-contained ranking. In Europeans, the GWAS were so large that association was widespread across the genome, a state known as saturation \citep{Yengo2022HeightSaturation}. For LDL-C, $44.1\%$ of genes had $p<0.05$ and $11.2\%$ were genome-wide significant, and for HDL-C, $52.7\%$ and $14.8\%$. Significant genes lay on every chromosome, led by \textit{PCSK9}, \textit{APOB}, \textit{LPA}, \textit{LDLR}, and \textit{PVRL2} for LDL-C and by \textit{ABCA1}, \textit{LIPC}, \textit{LPL}, \textit{SCARB1}, and \textit{GALNT2} for HDL-C (Fig.~\ref{fig:lipids}a,b). A random set of $50$ genes therefore contained, on average, $5.6$ (LDL-C) and $7.4$ (HDL-C) genome-wide significant genes, and almost every pathway was significant, with $\nLipSigLDL$ (LDL-C) and $\nLipSigHDL$ (HDL-C) of $\nLipNpw$ pathways at a family-wise $p\le0.05$. When nearly every pathway is significant, significance no longer separates pathways, and their ranking becomes what matters. Larger pathways also tended to rank higher because they contain more associated genes (Spearman correlation between pathway size and evidence $\nLipSizeRhoLDL$ for LDL-C and $\nLipSizeRhoHDL$ for HDL-C, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:density}). The top $20$ pathways had median sizes of $\nLipTopTwentyMedSizeLDL$ (LDL-C) and $\nLipTopTwentyMedSizeHDL$ (HDL-C) genes, against $\nLipMedSizeAll$ for all pathways.

Despite saturation, the top of the ranking reflected lipid biology. Plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling and clearance, a Reactome pathway that includes \textit{LDLR}, the apolipoproteins and the lipases, ranked first of $1{,}390$ pathways in all ten sets, although sample sizes differed up to $32$-fold (Fig.~\ref{fig:lipids}c,d). Its sub-pathways, plasma lipoprotein clearance, plasma lipoprotein remodeling, plasma lipoprotein assembly, LDL clearance, HDL remodeling and chylomicron remodeling, generally ranked in the top ten in every ancestry group (Fig.~\ref{fig:lipids}c,d, bold rows). In the non-European sets, five or six of the nine pathways in this group ranked in the top ten, followed by two LXR-mediated pathways that regulate \textit{ABCA1}. Large general pathways reached the top five only in Europeans. Interferon-$\gamma$ signalling ranked second for LDL-C in Europeans but $51$st to $314$th elsewhere, and SUMOylation ranked third for HDL-C in Europeans but $31$st to $178$th elsewhere, which fits saturation of the European data rather than lipid biology.

Because larger pathways tended to rank higher in Europeans, the lipoprotein pathways could have ranked first simply because they are large. To test this, we compared the evidence of each pathway with that of pathways of similar size. Evidence ($z_{\mathrm{std}}$, more negative is stronger) increased with pathway size, but the lipoprotein pathways lay far below this trend in both traits (Fig.~\ref{fig:lipids}e,f). The parent pathway, with $74$ genes, had $z_{\mathrm{std}}=-385$ for LDL-C and $-425$ for HDL-C, against medians of $-55$ and $-61$ for the $161$ pathways within $1.25$-fold of its size, and $-170$ and $-236$ for the strongest of those $161$. The smaller lipoprotein pathways also ranked high for their size, with a median percentile of $\nLipFamPctMedLDL\%$ (LDL-C) and $\nLipFamPctMedHDL\%$ (HDL-C) among pathways within $1.25$-fold of their size, against $\nLipAllPctMedLipoFreeLDL\%$ and $\nLipAllPctMedLipoFreeHDL\%$ for all other pathways (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:lipid_pathways}). The lipoprotein pathways therefore rank first because their genes are unusually strongly associated for pathways of their size, not because the pathways are large.

LDL-C and HDL-C reflect different steps of lipoprotein metabolism, so we compared which genes drove the shared top pathway in each trait. The gene-level $Z$ statistics of the two traits were only weakly correlated ($\nLipGeneRho$), and the two traits shared $13$ of their $100$ top genes (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_lipid}c). Within the lipoprotein pathway, LDL-C was led by genes of receptor-mediated uptake (\textit{LDLR}, \textit{PCSK9}, \textit{LDLRAP1}, \textit{APOB}, \textit{APOE}) and HDL-C by genes of reverse cholesterol transport and lipolysis (\textit{ABCA1}, \textit{LIPC}, \textit{SCARB1}, \textit{LPL}, \textit{CETP}), and only $4$ of the $12$ leading genes were shared (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_lipid}d). Pathway ranks agreed more closely than gene ranks. The pathway ranks of the two traits were correlated ($\nLipCrossRho$), with the parent lipoprotein pathway first for both and the other lipid pathways near the diagonal (Fig.~\ref{fig:lipids}g). Much of the agreement below the top reflects the shared trend with pathway size, but two GWAS whose leading genes largely differ identified the same top pathway.

Real GWAS have no known set of truly associated pathways, so the accuracy of CATFISH was measured in simulation, where it controlled false positives better than MAGMA and detected sparse pathways more often. On the lipid data we instead compared how CATFISH and MAGMA behave when both run self-contained and competitive tests on the same gene statistics and pathways. MAGMA's self-contained test called even more pathways in Europeans ($1{,}358$ for LDL-C and $1{,}383$ for HDL-C, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:magma_compare}), so saturation comes from the self-contained question itself rather than from CATFISH. The two self-contained rankings were similar (Spearman $0.70$--$0.94$), but MAGMA's depended more on pathway size (Spearman correlation between size and rank $0.85$ and $0.86$, against $0.72$ and $0.73$ for CATFISH), and MAGMA ranked the lipoprotein pathway first in six sets and second in four. The competitive tests did not saturate. CATFISH called $3$--$14$ pathways per set and MAGMA $2$--$12$, with little dependence on size (Spearman $-0.13$ to $0.22$, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:competitive_real}), and every CATFISH competitive call was also a self-contained call. The CATFISH competitive test ranked the parent lipoprotein pathway first in six sets and second or third in the South Asian and Hispanic sets, where one of its sub-pathways ranked first (plasma lipoprotein clearance for LDL-C and plasma lipoprotein remodeling for HDL-C). In Europeans, most competitive calls were lipid pathways, and for LDL-C they were six pathways of the plasma lipoprotein group, both LXR-regulated pathways and interferon-$\gamma$ signalling. Preranked GSEA also ranked the lipoprotein pathway first in all ten sets but called $6$--$65$ pathways per set, $40$ for LDL-C and $65$ for HDL-C in Europeans, in line with its false calls in simulation. The choice between the two CATFISH tests barely affected candidate genes. Using the competitive or joint statistic in the gene score instead of the self-contained one moved the median rank of the Open Targets gold-standard genes by at most $21$ places per set and changed their number in the top $20$ by at most one. Both tests therefore point to the same lipoprotein pathways and the same candidate genes, even though the self-contained test saturates in Europeans.

To test whether these results extend beyond lipids, we applied the same pipeline to seven further European GWAS, using summary statistics from the NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog in its harmonised format, which standardises variant identifiers, alleles and effect directions across studies \citep{Sollis2023GWASCatalog}. The traits were coronary artery disease (CAD) \citep{vanderHarst2018CAD}, breast cancer \citep{Michailidou2017BRCA}, atrial fibrillation (AF) \citep{Nielsen2018AF}, Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis (UC) \citep{deLange2017IBD}, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) \citep{Bentham2015SLE} and asthma \citep{Valette2021Asthma}. In each trait the top pathways matched the known biology of the trait, with plasma lipoprotein metabolism for CAD, SUMOylation and homology-directed repair for breast cancer, muscle contraction and cardiac conduction for AF, interferon-$\gamma$ signalling for Crohn's disease, UC and SLE, and T-cell differentiation and interleukin-1 family signalling for asthma. Across two lipid traits in five ancestry groups and these seven traits, CATFISH ranked pathways of the known biology of each trait highly, and for the lipids it did so more consistently and with less dependence on pathway size than MAGMA.

\subsection*{3.5 Pathway evidence improves candidate genes within loci and across nine human health traits}


We next turned from pathways to genes. Our central hypothesis is that a candidate gene is more credible when it lies in a pathway. CATFISH therefore ranks every gene by three layers of evidence, its strongest variant in the GWAS, its MAGMA gene $p$-value, and the strongest tested pathway that contains it. Each layer is converted to the gene's rank among all genes and scored as $-\log_{10}(\text{rank}/N)$, and the three scores are added with equal weight, so the score needs no tuning and applies to any species. It also works with any pathway database, and we used Reactome \citep{Liao2019} for the human traits and SorghumBicolorCyc, a curated database of sorghum metabolic pathways \citep{Hawkins2021CornCyc}, for sorghum. To assess the pathway layer’s contribution, we compared the full score with a GWAS-and-MAGMA-only version. We judged each ranking by how many known causal genes it placed in its genome-wide top $20$, $50$, and $100$, because genes for follow-up are chosen from the top of the list. We used Open Targets gold-standard genes as known causal genes at GWAS loci, based on curated evidence from functional studies, Mendelian diseases, or drug targets \citep{Mountjoy2021L2G}. There are $15$ such genes for LDL-C, $18$ for HDL-C, and $\nBenchNgold$ across the nine traits. These lists are incomplete, so a known trait gene missing from them is unvalidated rather than unrelated.

In the European lipid data, no single layer ranked the gold-standard genes well, and the layers failed on different genes. \textit{LDLRAP1} was $226$th by its strongest variant but $12$th by MAGMA for LDL-C, whereas \textit{CETP} was first by variant but $66$th by MAGMA for HDL-C (Fig.~\ref{fig:genes}a,b). The strongest variant alone placed $3$ of the $15$ LDL-C gold-standard genes in the top $20$, MAGMA alone $5$, and GWAS and MAGMA together $3$, and adding the pathway layer raised this to $6$. For HDL-C, the counts were $3$, $4$, and $5$ of $18$, rising to $8$ with the pathway layer (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:logo_recovery}). The score ranked \textit{LDLR} first for LDL-C and \textit{LIPC} first for HDL-C, with \textit{ABCA1} second (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:lipid_candidates}).

A gene that ranks high because of its biology should do so in every ancestry group, whereas a gene that ranks high only because it shares a strong variant with a causal neighbor depends on the LD of each population. Five LDL-C genes and eight HDL-C genes were in the top $20$ in all five ancestry groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:genes}c,d). For LDL-C, these were the gold-standard genes \textit{LDLR}, \textit{PCSK9} , and \textit{APOB}, together with \textit{APOE} and \textit{APOC1}; for HDL-C, the gold-standard genes \textit{were ABCA1}, \textit{CETP}, \textit{LIPC} , and \textit{LIPG}, together with \textit{LPL}, \textit{APOA5}, \textit{APOC1} , and \textit{APOE}. Three of the four genes outside the gold standard have well-established roles in lipoprotein metabolism. \textit{APOE} genotype is associated with coronary risk \citep{Song2004APOE}, coding variants in the lipase gene \textit{LPL} alter plasma lipids and coronary risk \citep{Stitziel2016LPL}, and the apolipoprotein gene \textit{APOA5} regulates plasma triglycerides \citep{Pennacchio2001APOA5}. The fourth, \textit{APOC1}, lies next to \textit{APOE} and shares its lead variant, so its independent effect is unresolved \citep{GordilloMaranon2021}. That these genes are missing from the Open Targets lists shows that the lists are incomplete. Genes that ranked high mainly because they share a lead variant with these genes, such as \textit{TOMM40} and \textit{PVRL2} next to \textit{APOE} and \textit{HERPUD1} and \textit{SLC12A3} next to \textit{CETP}, did not reach the top $20$ in every group (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:replication_genes}). Pathway membership also separated genes within a locus where associations could not. \textit{APOE}, \textit{APOC1} and \textit{TOMM40} share the same best variant in every set, so the GWAS layer ranks them equally, but only \textit{APOE} and \textit{APOC1} are in the lipoprotein pathway. Across the ten lipid sets, \textit{APOE} and \textit{APOC1} maintained the high ranks they had by GWAS (median rank across the ten sets $5.5$ by GWAS, and $6.5$ and $4.5$ by the gene score), whereas \textit{TOMM40} fell from $5.5$ to $19$, and \textit{PVRL2} from $7.5$ to $20.5$, so \textit{APOE} ranked above \textit{TOMM40} in all ten sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:genes}e, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:apoe_locus}). The score likewise ranked \textit{CETP} above \textit{HERPUD1} and \textit{SLC12A3} in all five groups. Genes that share both the variant and the pathway, such as \textit{APOC1} with \textit{APOE}, remain indistinguishable and rank next to each other.

To test whether the gain extends beyond lipids, we compared the two scores in all nine European traits. Across the nine traits, the genome-wide top $20$, $50$ and $100$ contained $\nBenchNpTwenty$, $\nBenchNpFifty$ and $\nBenchNpHundred$ gold-standard genes without the pathway layer and $\nBenchCatTwenty$, $\nBenchCatFifty$ and $\nBenchCatHundred$ with it, $\nBenchGainTwenty\%$, $\nBenchGainFifty\%$ and $\nBenchGainHundred\%$ more (Fig.~\ref{fig:genes}f, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:pathway_gain_all}). The full score matched or exceeded the score without pathways in every trait at every cutoff and improved at least one cutoff in each trait. The largest gains were in the top $20$ for asthma ($5$ genes) and for LDL-C and HDL-C ($3$ each), and in the top $100$ for Crohn's disease ($6$) and for breast cancer and HDL-C ($4$ each). For comparison, the strongest variant alone recovered $\nBenchGwTwenty$, $\nBenchGwFifty$ and $\nBenchGwHundred$ gold-standard genes, and MAGMA alone $\nBenchMgTwenty$, $\nBenchMgFifty$ and $\nBenchMgHundred$. Because a gene's own association contributes to the evidence of its pathways, part of this gain could be the gene's own signal returned to it through its pathway. We therefore recomputed the pathway evidence of each gold-standard gene with that gene removed from its pathways, keeping every other gene unchanged. In the ten lipid sets, the pathway layer raised the number of gold-standard genes in the top $20$ from $35$ to $63$, and $59$ of the $63$ remained with each gene left out. In the seven other traits, it raised the number from $11$ to $22$, and $19$ remained (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:logo_recovery} and \ref{tab:logo_recovery_bench}). In $220$ of $321$ gene--trait pairs, the gene still ranked higher without its own contribution than without the pathway layer, so most of the gain comes from the other genes of its pathways. This does not exclude a contribution of prior knowledge, because gold-standard genes and curated pathways draw on the same literature, and removing a gene removes its association but not that shared knowledge.

To see which genes the score changes, we compared its top $\nLiftN$ genes in each trait with the top $\nLiftN$ by strongest variant (Table~\ref{tab:lifted_dropped}, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:lifted_genes}). Across nine traits, the score moved $\nLiftAll$ genes into the top $\nLiftN$ and dropped $\nDropAll$. None of the dropped genes met the gold standard, versus $\nLiftGold$ lifted genes ($\nLiftGoldPct\%$), a share similar to that among genes on both lists ($\nKeepGold$ of $\nKeepAll$, $\nKeepGoldPct\%$). Many dropped genes share a strong variant with a better candidate. For example, \textit{PGAP3}, \textit{PNMT}, \textit{TCAP}, and \textit{LRRC3C} share the 17q12--21 signal with \textit{ORMDL3} and \textit{GSDMB} \citep{Moffatt2010Asthma} but have no known role in asthma. Gold-standard genes that rose from variant to score ranking included \textit{IL4R} ($85$th to $4$th), \textit{IL4} ($94$th to $11$th), and \textit{IL2RA} ($79$th to $19$th) for asthma, and \textit{LDLRAP1} ($237$th to $8$th) for LDL-C. The pathway layer alone moved $\nLiftPath$ genes into the top $\nLiftN$, including $\nLiftPathGold$ gold-standard genes such as \textit{NR1H3} for HDL-C and \textit{STAT3} for Crohn's disease. The score cannot lift genes outside the tested pathways, so it dropped some with published evidence, including \textit{MST1} and \textit{NKX2-3} for UC and \textit{BANK1} for SLE.

We compared CATFISH with PoPS, a widely used method that ranks genes genome-wide by learning which of thousands of features, including tissue expression, protein interactions, and pathway membership, predict MAGMA gene statistics \citep{Weeks2023PoPS}. We ran PoPS with default settings on the same GWAS and MAGMA results. Without model training or features beyond pathways, CATFISH recovered more gold-standard genes at every cutoff. $\nBenchCatTwenty$ vs.\ $\nBenchPopsTwenty$ in the top $20$, $\nBenchCatFifty$ vs.\ $\nBenchPopsFifty$ in the top $50$, and $\nBenchCatHundred$ vs.\ $\nBenchPopsHundred$ in the top $100$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:genes}g, Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_topk_all}). CATFISH led for LDL-C, HDL-C, CAD, breast cancer, and AF. The methods tied for UC and SLE, and PoPS led for Crohn's disease and asthma, where immune-cell expression may provide information not captured by pathways (Fig.~\ref{fig:genes}g). The advantage held whether ranking all genes or only those in tested pathways (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_bench}). Of the gold-standard genes in either top $20$, $\nBenchBothTwenty$ appeared in both, $\nBenchOnlyCatTwenty$ only in CATFISH and $\nBenchOnlyPopsTwenty$ only in PoPS (Fig.~\ref{fig:genes}h). Averaging the ranks recovered $\nBenchCombTwenty$, $\nBenchCombFifty$, and $\nBenchCombHundred$ gold-standard genes in the top $20$, $50$, and $100$, respectively, outperforming either method alone. Thus, adding pathway evidence to GWAS and MAGMA improved gene prioritization for every trait, recovered more known genes overall than PoPS, and identified complementary candidates.

\subsection*{3.6 A permutation null in long-LD sorghum confirms per-pathway calibration on real genotypes}

LD in human populations extends over short distances \citep{Reich2001LD}, and our simulations drew gene $z$-scores from a statistical model rather than from real genotypes. We therefore asked whether CATFISH stays calibrated on real genotypes with long-range LD, running the full pipeline from GWAS to pathways. We used the sorghum Bioenergy Association Panel (BAP), $327$ lines \citep{Brenton2016}, a small, structured panel analyzed with a mixed model, in which LD extends much further than in humans \citep{Morris2013Sorghum}. In MAGMA's gene--gene correlations, each gene had on average $\nSorNeighbours$ neighbors with $|r|\ge0.1$, against $\nLipNeighbours$ in the human lipid data. Because no pathway in a real GWAS is known to be null, we made every pathway null by permuting the phenotype across lines, which breaks the link between genotype and phenotype while keeping the genotypes, LD, and population structure \citep{Churchill1994,Wang2007Pathway}. Any pathway called significant is then a false positive. We permuted dry biomass $100$ times and ran GWAS, MAGMA, and CATFISH on each permutation, giving $304$ pathways and $30{,}400$ null tests. Pathways came from SorghumBicolorCyc, the curated metabolic pathway database for sorghum from the Plant Metabolic Network \citep{Hawkins2021CornCyc}, and we tested those with $5$ to $200$ genes, so the size classes run from $5$--$9$ genes upward.

Without any correction, the pathway $p$-values were anti-conservative, although the genomic-control $\lambda$ was $0.84$. In total, $7.2\%$ had $p<0.05$ and $2.3\%$ had $p<0.01$, against $5\%$ and $1\%$ expected, and the family-wise error was $0.18$ ($18$ of $100$ permutations, $95\%$ CI $0.11$--$0.27$, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:sorghum_null}). The excess grew with pathway size, from $6.1\%$ with $p<0.05$ among pathways of $5$--$9$ genes to $12.7\%$ among those of $50$--$200$ genes, and the QQ plot lay above the diagonal (Fig.~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}a,d). The cause was a shift of all gene $z$-scores together. In each permuted GWAS the mean gene $z$-score moved away from zero, varying across permutations far more than expected (SD $0.24$ against $0.075$), genes on different chromosomes were correlated ($r=0.06$), and the number of pathways with $p<0.05$ followed the mean gene $z$-score (Spearman $0.90$, Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_sorghum_null}a). A self-contained test reads such a shift as signal in every pathway, and more so in larger pathways. Gene-level genomic control removes the shift by subtracting the genome-wide median gene $z$-score before pathway testing, the gene-level analogue of genomic control for variants \citep{Devlin1999}. With this correction, $\lambda$ was $1.01$, $5.2\%$ of pathways had $p<0.05$ and $1.3\%$ had $p<0.01$, and the excess no longer depended on size ($5.0$--$6.0\%$ with $p<0.05$ in every size class, Fig.~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}a). The QQ plot followed the diagonal except in the extreme tail, and the family-wise error fell to $0.12$ ($12$ of $98$, $95\%$ CI $0.06$--$0.20$, Fig.~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}b), with the $21$ family-wise false positives spread over different pathways. We therefore apply gene-level genomic control in all real-data analyzes.

The remaining excess lay in the extreme tail, where per-pathway $p<0.001$ occurred $1.8$ times as often as expected, and because the family-wise error depends on the smallest $p$-value in each permutation, even a small excess there raises it. We checked two possible causes. MAGMA overstated moderate gene--gene correlations ($r=0.52$ against $0.32$ observed across permutations, Fig.~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}c), but replacing its correlations with those observed across permutations did not lower the family-wise error ($0.15$, $95\%$ CI $0.09$--$0.24$, Fig.~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}b). The cause was instead the variant test. The mixed-model GWAS gave heavier null tails than the multivariate normal model of gene $z$-scores assumes, with variant $p<10^{-4}$ occurring $4.5$ times as often as expected overall and $11.5$ times as often at minor allele frequencies of $0.01$--$0.02$ (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_sorghum_null}b). We did not filter rare variants after seeing this result, because that would tune the analysis to its outcome. In this panel, gene-level genomic control therefore calibrated the per-pathway $p$-values, but the family-wise error remained about $2.5$ times nominal ($0.12$ against $0.05$), because of the GWAS model rather than the pathway method.

With the per-pathway $p$-values calibrated on these genotypes, we applied CATFISH to the dry-biomass GWAS itself. No pathway reached family-wise significance (Fig.~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}e). The top-ranked pathway was proanthocyanidin biosynthesis from flavanols, with a family-wise $p$-value of $\nSorTopFwerDry$, and the family-wise $p$-values of the top ten pathways, which the mixed-model tail makes slightly too small, reached up to $0.96$. Five of the top ten genes, ranked $1$, $3$, $5$, $6$ and $7$, had pathway support (Fig.~\ref{fig:sorghum_dry}f). The top gene, Sobic.004G014400, encodes cycloartenol synthase, which catalyses the first committed step of plant sterol biosynthesis \citep{Babiychuk2008}. It shares its lead variant with seven neighbouring genes and ranks first by MAGMA, and the pathway layer raised it from $\nSorCasRankNp$ to first. Loss of cycloartenol synthase is lethal in \textit{Arabidopsis}, and sterol deficiency impairs cellulose synthesis \citep{Babiychuk2008,Schrick2012}. In bioenergy sorghum the gene is most highly expressed in young elongating internodes \citep{Kebrom2017}, although its role in biomass has not been tested. Thus, on real genotypes with long-range LD, CATFISH gave calibrated per-pathway $p$-values once gene-level genomic control was applied, and its gene score nominated a sterol biosynthesis gene as the leading candidate for dry biomass, although no single pathway was significant.

\subsection*{3.7 The gene score resolves a new sorghum sugar--fibre locus to two enzymes}

No pathway was significant for dry biomass, so we analyzed three stem-composition traits measured in the same lines, Brix at maturity (soluble solids), neutral detergent fiber (NDF, the cell wall fraction), and non-fibre carbohydrate (NFC, mainly sugars and starch). Brix was correlated with NDF ($r=\nBapRBrixNDF$) and NFC ($r=\nBapRBrixNFC$), so lines with more sugar had less cell wall. With family-wise control, the self-contained test called $\nBapSigBrix$, $\nBapSigNDF$ and $\nBapSigNFC$ of $\nBapNpw$ pathways for Brix, NDF, and NFC, while the competitive test called none. Two pathways were significant for both fiber traits, the thioredoxin pathway (family-wise $p=0.0062$ for NDF and $8.0\times10^{-4}$ for NFC) and xylogalacturonan biosynthesis ($p=0.042$ and $0.032$). $\beta$-alanine biosynthesis I was significant for Brix ($p=0.015$), and triacylglycerol degradation and stachyose biosynthesis for NFC only. The thioredoxin and $\beta$-alanine biosynthesis I pathways are effectively single-gene pathways at the chromosome~4 locus described next, and as in the simulation, only the self-contained test detected them (competitive family-wise $p=\nBapBalaCompBrix$, $\nBapTrxPwCompNDF$, and $\nBapTrxPwCompNFC$). These calls therefore point to the genes of the locus rather than to $\beta$-alanine or thioredoxin metabolism as a whole.

The gene scores of all three traits pointed to one locus on chromosome~4. Of the $\nBapNgenes$ genes, only two were in the top $30$ for all three traits: the adjacent genes Sobic.004G300800 and Sobic.004G300900. Widening the cutoff to the top $100$ added only two more genes from the same $85\,\mathrm{kb}$ ($63.96$--$64.04\,\mathrm{Mb}$, Fig.~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}d, Table~\ref{tab:bap_chr4}), a region in which, to our knowledge, no sugar or fiber locus has been reported. Sobic.004G300800 encodes \textit{SbALDH3E1}, an ALDH3 fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase predicted to localise to the chloroplast \citep{Islam2022SbALDH}, and Sobic.004G300900 encodes an NADPH-dependent thioredoxin reductase (TRXR). \textit{SbALDH3E1} ranked $\nBapAldhBrix$, $\nBapAldhNDF$ and $\nBapAldhNFC$ for Brix, NDF, and NFC, and TRXR $\nBapTrxBrix$, $\nBapTrxNDF$ and $\nBapTrxNFC$. Neither gene stood out by GWAS or MAGMA alone.Their strongest variants ranked only $\nBapAldhGwasBrix$th to $\nBapAldhGwasNDF$th genome-wide. The best Brix variant is shared with four neighbouring genes, and the strongest NDF and NFC variants lie about $60\,\mathrm{kb}$ away in the unannotated genes Sobic.004G301200 to Sobic.004G301700. By MAGMA, they were the best genes of the locus but ranked only $121$st to $138$th genome-wide for the fiber traits ($13$th and $16$th for Brix). Without the pathway layer, TRXR ranked $\nBapGmTrxNDF$th for NDF and $\nBapGmTrxNFC$th for NFC, and the pathway layer raised it to $\nBapTrxNDF$th and $\nBapTrxNFC$rd, because the two enzymes were the only genes of the locus in any tested pathway. In positional order, the two enzymes formed the shared peak of the gene score, whereas the unannotated genes Sobic.004G301200 to Sobic.004G301700 ranked $52$nd to $215$th and the other genes of the locus no higher than $33$rd (Fig.~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}d).

Because the thioredoxin and $\beta$-alanine pathways are nearly single-gene pathways at this locus, part of the pathway support for these two genes could come from their own association. Therefore, we recomputed each gene's pathway evidence with that gene removed from its pathways, using the same null draws and keeping every other gene unchanged. \textit{SbALDH3E1} stayed near the top for Brix ($\nBapGmAldhBrix$ without pathways, $\nBapAldhBrix$ with pathways, and $\nBapLogoAldhBrix$ without its own contribution), and TRXR kept most of its lift for the fiber traits, ranking $\nBapLogoTrxNDF$ for NDF and $\nBapLogoTrxNFC$ for NFC without its own contribution, against $\nBapTrxNDF$ and $\nBapTrxNFC$ with it, and $\nBapGmTrxNDF$ and $\nBapGmTrxNFC$ without pathways (Fig.~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}e, black lines). For each gene's other trait, much or all of the lift came from the gene itself. TRXR fell to $\nBapLogoTrxBrix$ for Brix, and \textit{SbALDH3E1} fell to $\nBapLogoAldhNFC$ for NFC, no better than their ranks without pathways ($\nBapGmTrxBrix$ and $\nBapGmAldhNFC$), and \textit{SbALDH3E1} kept only part of its lift for NDF ($\nBapLogoAldhNDF$, against $\nBapAldhNDF$ with and $\nBapGmAldhNDF$ without pathways). Across all pathway-supported genes in the top $100$, $\nBapLogoKeepBrix$ of $\nBapLogoNBrix$ (Brix), $\nBapLogoKeepNDF$ of $\nBapLogoNNDF$ (NDF), and $\nBapLogoKeepNFC$ of $\nBapLogoNNFC$ (NFC) stayed in the top $100$ without their own contribution (Fig.~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}e, gray lines). The pathway support for TRXR in the fiber traits and for \textit{SbALDH3E1} in Brix, therefore, came mainly from other genes in their pathways.

The lead variants of the three traits are in strong LD. The Brix lead at $\nBapLeadPosBrix\,\mathrm{Mb}$, $6\,\mathrm{kb}$ from \textit{SbALDH3E1}, and the shared NDF and NFC lead at $\nBapLeadPosNDF\,\mathrm{Mb}$ have $r^2=\nBapLeadRsq$. Brix and NDF $z$-scores were also correlated across the $\nBapNwin$ variants of the region ($\nBapZcor$), and after orienting alleles to increase Brix, the variants fell along one diagonal, with the Brix lead and the NDF and NFC lead at its two ends (Fig.~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}c). The association persisted with three principal components as covariates. Each copy of the Brix-increasing allele (\nBapUp, frequency $\nBapFreqUp$) increased Brix by $\nBapEffBrix$ and NFC by $\nBapEffNFC$ SD and decreased NDF by $|\nBapEffNDF|$ SD (Fig.~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}b). Lines homozygous for this allele had higher Brix and lower NDF than the other homozygotes, and their mean NDF lay below the trend of NDF on Brix across all lines (Fig.~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}a). After adjusting for Brix, NDF still differed by $\nBapAdjNDF$ between the two homozygotes ($p=\nBapAdjP$), so the relationship between sugar and fiber explains about $\nBapFracVia\%$ of the effect of the allele on fiber. The locus showed no clear effect on maturity or height. The cloned sorghum maturity and height genes lie on other chromosomes \citep{Murphy2011PRR37,Murphy2014Ghd7,Childs1997PHYB,Casto2019Ma2,Hilley2016Dw1,Hilley2017Dw2,Multani2003Dw3}, the strongest flowering-time association in the region had $p=1.1\times10^{-4}$, and the Brix-increasing allele had no effect on stalk height ($p=0.29$) or dry biomass ($p=0.09$). In public RNA-seq data, \textit{SbALDH3E1} is expressed in most tissues, most highly in the leaf sheath at flowering and least in stem internodes (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:sbaldh_expression}) \citep{Moreno2022ExpressionAtlas}. Related enzymes in other species point to roles in lipid and acetate metabolism. A maize fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase has been implicated in kernel oil content \citep{Zhang2026FALDH}, and the \textit{Arabidopsis} ALDH3F1 supplies acetate for histone acetylation at \textit{FLOWERING LOCUS C} \citep{Xu2020ALDH3F1}. Overall, from three GWAS whose top variants fall in unannotated genes, CATFISH identified one locus shared by stem sugar and fiber, and within it, two annotated enzymes that the GWAS alone ranked no higher than $\nBapAldhGwasBrix$th as candidates for functional tests.

\section{4 Discussion}

\subsection*{4.1 CATFISH detects different pathway signals and stays calibrated on simulated and real genotypes}

Pathway signals vary, so no single test detects all types. In simulations, each component test excelled in its target signal but could have less than half the power of the best component on other signal types. The omnibus was usually near the best component and lost substantial power only when a single gene drove the pathway. Thus, combining five tests largely removes the need to pre-specify the signal type, at modest cost.

Furthermore, the omnibus $p$-values were well calibrated. CATFISH computes both its per-pathway and its family-wise $p$-values from the same null draws, and these draws carry the correlation between genes. This kept both $p$-values calibrated in every stress test, including strong correlation between the genes of a pathway, up to four of the five component tests miscalibrated, and the removal of a component. The analytic omnibus, which treats genes as independent, lost calibration in the same tests, and a Bonferroni correction of it failed under within-pathway correlation, with a family-wise error close to one. The method also relies on the gene--gene correlation file. As correlations went missing, calibration degraded gradually, and family-wise $p$-values degraded faster than per-pathway $p$-values. In GWAS simulations with real LD, the CATFISH self-contained test made no false calls under the null, against $16\%$ for the MAGMA self-contained test, and detected sparse pathways that MAGMA largely missed.

The simulations modelled gene statistics, so the sorghum analysis asked whether CATFISH stays calibrated on real genotypes, running the full pipeline from GWAS to pathways. We permuted dry biomass across the lines of a structured panel with long-range LD, so that every pathway was null and every significant pathway a false positive. This revealed two problems that LD does not explain. First, in each permuted GWAS, all gene $z$-scores moved up or down together. A self-contained test asks whether the genes of a pathway are more associated than expected by chance, so a shift shared by every gene makes every pathway look associated, and the more genes a pathway has, the more the shift adds up. Without correction, $18$ of $100$ permutations gave at least one significant pathway (family-wise error $0.18$ instead of $0.05$), and false positives became more frequent as pathways grew. Subtracting the median gene $z$-score of each GWAS removed the shift and restored calibration, so CATFISH applies this gene-level genomic control by default. Second, even with the phenotype permuted, the mixed-model GWAS gave rare variants extreme $p$-values far more often than chance allows. The CATFISH null draws assume normally distributed gene statistics, so they underestimate how extreme a null gene can be. This barely affected the per-pathway $p$-value ($5.2\%$ below $0.05$), but the family-wise $p$-value is set by the most extreme of all $304$ pathways, so its error rose to $0.12$. Thus, in small panels analysed with mixed models, per-pathway $p$-values are reliable, a family-wise $p$-value near $0.05$ should be read as borderline, and a phenotype permutation, when genotypes are available, checks it directly.

The human analyses asked whether the method recovers known biology. Plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodeling and clearance ranked first for LDL-C and HDL-C in all five ancestry groups, in ten analyses spanning up to $32$-fold differences in sample size and different allele frequencies and LD. The two traits reached this pathway through largely different genes, receptor-mediated uptake for LDL-C and reverse cholesterol transport and lipolysis for HDL-C, so the shared pathway does not simply reflect shared genes. Across seven additional traits, the top pathways matched the known biology of each trait. For the lipids, CATFISH ranked the lipoprotein pathway first more consistently than MAGMA ($10$ of $10$ sets against $6$ of $10$) and depended less on pathway size. Together, these results show that CATFISH detects sparse, dense and mixed pathway signals without knowing their type in advance, stays calibrated on simulated and real genotypes once a genome-wide shift in gene statistics is removed, and recovers the expected biology in human traits.

\subsection*{4.2 When significance saturates, the ranking carries the information}

In the European lipid GWAS, $11.2\%$ of genes were genome-wide significant, so a random set of $50$ genes would contain at least one genome-wide significant gene with probability $0.997$. Even with family-wise control, $\nLipSigLDL$ of $\nLipNpw$ pathways remained significant for LDL-C, and MAGMA called even more. At this sample size, the self-contained hypothesis, that no gene in a pathway is associated with the trait, is false for almost any gene set \citep{Goeman2007,Maciejewski2014}. A stricter threshold does not fix this, because the problem lies in the question the test asks, not in the correction for multiple testing.

Nonetheless, the self-contained analysis still separated pathways, because CATFISH ranks them by $z_{\mathrm{std}}$, a size-standardised statistic that compares each pathway with null draws of its own size and gene correlation. In a saturated genome, large pathways accumulate evidence simply by containing more genes, so a ranking by raw $p$-value favours them, as the MAGMA ranking did (Spearman correlation between pathway size and rank $0.85$ and $0.86$, against $0.72$ and $0.73$ for CATFISH). The lipoprotein pathways stood out against pathways of similar size in both traits, so their top rank did not come from their size. The competitive test, which asks whether the genes of a pathway are more associated than the rest of the genome, does not saturate and highlighted the same pathways, a second check that asks a different question. Thus, in large GWAS, the number of significant self-contained pathways says little, while the CATFISH ranking, confirmed by the competitive test, carries the information. When few genes are associated, as in sorghum, self-contained significance remains informative, and the self-contained test is the only one that detects a pathway driven by a single gene, both in simulation and at the sorghum chromosome~4 locus.

\subsection*{4.3 Pathway evidence lets candidate genes be chosen beyond the strength of their GWAS signal}

We hypothesized that a candidate gene is more credible when it lies in a pathway that is itself associated with the trait. The common practice after a GWAS of ranking genes by their strongest variant fills candidate lists with many genes from the same locus. At 17q12--21 for asthma or the \textit{APOE} cluster for LDL-C, all genes in the linkage block share the lead variant and are ranked together regardless of their function. The CATFISH gene score, which adds MAGMA and pathway evidence to the strongest variant, addressed this. Compared with the top~$\nLiftN$ genes ranked by their strongest variant, it removed $\nDropAll$ genes across the nine human traits, none of them a gold-standard gene, and brought in $\nLiftAll$, including $\nLiftGold$ gold-standard genes and genes of known trait biology such as \textit{IL4R} and \textit{IL4} for asthma and \textit{IRF3} for SLE. It also placed $\nBenchGainTwenty\%$ more gold-standard genes in the top 20 than the same score without pathways, and never fewer in any of the nine traits. GWAS and MAGMA can disagree substantially. \textit{CETP} has the strongest variant for HDL-C but ranks $66$th by MAGMA, whereas \textit{LDLRAP1} ranks $226$th by its strongest variant but $12$th by MAGMA for LDL-C. Candidates therefore need not be the gene with the strongest variant, and can be chosen by concordant gene-level and pathway evidence rather than by a single variant.

This approach has two limitations. First, the gain may partly reflect prior knowledge. Gold-standard genes are well studied and annotated to more pathways, so some of their lift may come from the literature shared by genes and pathways rather than from the GWAS. Leaving each gene out of its own pathways addresses this in part. The pathway layer placed $85$ gold-standard genes in the top 20 across the ten lipid sets and the seven other traits, and $78$ remained there with each gene's own association removed from its pathways, so most of the lift came from other genes in the same pathways. This check removes a gene's association but not the knowledge that genes and curated pathways share. Second, the pathway layer helps only genes that are in a pathway, and it can propagate annotation errors, particularly from computationally predicted databases such as SorghumBicolorCyc. Genes with published evidence but no tested pathway, such as \textit{MST1} for UC and \textit{BANK1} for SLE, dropped out of the top~$\nLiftN$. Within these limits, the genes the score places at the top are mostly genes of the known biology of each trait. None of the dropped genes was a gold-standard gene, lifted genes were gold-standard genes as often as genes in both lists were, and all $90$ top-ten genes lie at loci previously reported for their trait. Thus, the gene score is best used to choose genes for follow-up, reading each gene's rank on every layer, rather than as proof of causality.

CATFISH also recovered more gold-standard genes than PoPS at every cutoff, $\nBenchCatTwenty$ against $\nBenchPopsTwenty$ in the top 20, $\nBenchCatFifty$ against $\nBenchPopsFifty$ in the top 50 and $\nBenchCatHundred$ against $\nBenchPopsHundred$ in the top 100, and it led at every cutoff whether genes were ranked within the genes scored by both methods, among all genes, or among genes in a tested pathway (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_bench}). PoPS trains a model on thousands of gene features, including tissue and cell-type expression, whereas CATFISH uses only GWAS, MAGMA and pathway membership, with equal weights and no training. CATFISH led for the lipids, CAD, breast cancer and AF, where pathway biology is well curated, and trailed for Crohn's disease and asthma, where expression features may carry information that pathway membership does not, so an expression layer is a natural addition to the gene score in future versions. The two methods also found partly different genes, and averaging their ranks recovered more gold-standard genes than either alone, so they complement each other.

\subsection*{4.4 A sorghum locus that moves stem carbon between sugar and cell wall}

The stem-composition traits show what the gene score adds when the strongest GWAS variants fall in unannotated genes. Three correlated traits, Brix, NFC and NDF, mapped to one haplotype of about $85\,\mathrm{kb}$ on chromosome~4, with lead variants in strong LD ($r^2=\nBapLeadRsq$) and similar evidence across about $15$ genes. For the two fibre traits, the strongest variants lay in unannotated genes. The gene scores narrowed the locus to two adjacent enzymes, the fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase \textit{SbALDH3E1} and an NADPH-dependent thioredoxin reductase, which ranked in the top $30$ genome-wide for every trait and were the only genes of the locus in any pathway. This reduces the roughly $15$ genes of the locus to two candidates for gene editing.

A single locus can shift all three traits because they draw on the same carbon. NDF measures cell wall, NFC mainly soluble sugars and starch, and Brix the soluble solids in stem juice, so carbon used for cell walls is not available as sugar, consistent with the negative correlation between Brix and NDF ($r=\nBapRBrixNDF$). The Brix-increasing haplotype raised Brix and NFC and lowered NDF by similar standardised amounts (Fig.~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}b), with no detectable effect on flowering, height or biomass, so its effect is unlikely to come from later, taller or smaller plants. Adjusting for Brix halved the NDF difference between the two homozygous groups, from $\nBapDiffNDF$ to $\nBapAdjNDF$ percentage points of dry matter (Fig.~\ref{fig:bap_chr4}a), so the relationship between sugar and fibre accounts for about $\nBapFracVia\%$ of the effect of the haplotype on fibre. The remainder could mean that the locus also acts on cell wall deposition directly, but it could also reflect measurement error in Brix, because adjusting for a noisy measure removes only part of its effect. Both candidates have plausible but different mechanisms. ALDH3 enzymes oxidise aldehydes derived from lipids to the corresponding fatty acids \citep{Islam2022SbALDH}. In \textit{Arabidopsis}, ALDH3F1 supplies acetate for the acetyl-CoA used to acetylate histones at the flowering regulator \textit{FLOWERING LOCUS C} \citep{Xu2020ALDH3F1}, and in maize, a fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase is a candidate gene for kernel oil content \citep{Zhang2026FALDH}. Thioredoxin reductases keep thioredoxins reduced, and reduced thioredoxins switch many enzymes of carbon metabolism on or off by reducing their disulphide bonds \citep{BuchananBalmer2005,Geigenberger2017Trx}. In \textit{Arabidopsis}, the plastid NADPH-dependent thioredoxin reductase C links the supply of sucrose to starch synthesis \citep{Michalska2009NTRC}. To our knowledge, neither gene has been linked to stem sugar or cell walls in sorghum or any other species, and we do not claim that either is causal.

These results have limitations. Much of the pathway evidence for the two genes comes from pathways that contain essentially only that gene. When each gene was removed from its own pathways, \textit{SbALDH3E1} kept its high rank for Brix and TRXR kept most of the rise in rank that the pathway layer had given it for the fibre traits, so these ranks rest on other genes in their pathways. For the remaining traits, the rise came mainly from each gene's own association. Only $\nBapNUpHom$ of the $\nBapNlines$ lines are homozygous for the Brix-increasing haplotype. Adding three principal components barely changed the association, but biparental or near-isogenic lines are needed to rule out population history. \textit{SbALDH3E1} is expressed in most tissues, most highly in the leaf sheath and least in stem internodes (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:sbaldh_expression}). This pattern would fit an effect on the supply of sugar from leaves to the stem rather than an effect within the stem, although expression alone cannot show where a gene acts. Effect sizes estimated at the lead variant of a GWAS also tend to be overestimated, especially in a panel of this size, because the variant was selected for its strong association \citep{Xu2003Beavis}. Future work could test the two genes directly, by measuring allele-specific expression in stem, sheath and leaf, comparing the coding and regulatory sequences of the two haplotypes, and creating loss-of-function alleles by mutagenesis or genome editing.

\subsection*{4.5 The top-ranked genes in nine human traits are known genes of those traits}

To assess what the gene score prioritizes, we reviewed the top ten genes for each of the nine human traits against the literature (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:top10}). All $90$ genes lie at loci previously reported for the corresponding trait. For $41$ of them, genetic or functional evidence implicates the gene itself. These include genes of Mendelian lipid disorders, \textit{LDLR}, \textit{LDLRAP1} and \textit{PCSK9} for LDL-C and \textit{ABCA1} for both lipid traits \citep{BrownGoldstein1986,Garcia2001LDLRAP1,Abifadel2003PCSK9,BrooksWilson1999ABCA1}. They also include genes with loss-of-function or coding variants that alter the trait, \textit{SCARB1} and \textit{GALNT2} for HDL-C, \textit{LPL} for HDL-C and CAD, \textit{NOD2} and \textit{IL23R} for Crohn's disease and \textit{IL33} for asthma \citep{Zanoni2016SCARB1,Khetarpal2016GALNT2,Stitziel2016LPL,Hugot2001NOD2,Duerr2006IL23R,Smith2017IL33}, and genes whose expression or splicing is changed by the risk variants, \textit{ESR1} for breast cancer, \textit{COL4A1} for CAD and \textit{IRF5} for SLE \citep{Dunning2016ESR1,Yang2016COL4A1,Graham2006IRF5}. For $45$ genes, the locus is known but the causal gene is unresolved. Examples are \textit{SLC22A3} in the \textit{SLC22A3}, \textit{LPAL2} and \textit{LPA} cluster for CAD \citep{Tregouet2009}, \textit{NLRC5} next to \textit{CETP} for HDL-C, and \textit{STAT1} for SLE, where the risk variant lies in \textit{STAT4} but changes \textit{STAT1} expression in B cells \citep{Patel2018STAT1}. For the remaining $4$, the evidence points to a nearby gene. The \textit{PHACTR1} variant for CAD regulates \textit{EDN1} \citep{Gupta2017PHACTR1}, breast cancer variants near \textit{ZNF365} regulate \textit{NRBF2} \citep{Darabi2015ZNF365}, \textit{SNAPC4} for Crohn's disease lies at the \textit{CARD9} locus \citep{Franke2010CD}, and \textit{HSPA6} for UC lies at the \textit{FCGR2A} locus \citep{Anderson2011UC}. Overall, the score recovers established trait genetics. It does not create new loci, but it orders the genes within known ones.

Two points help in reading these lists. First, several top genes of a trait share a locus, such as \textit{APOE}, \textit{APOC1} and \textit{PVRL2} for LDL-C, or \textit{GPX1} and \textit{RHOA} on chromosome~3p21 for UC. They are not separate discoveries, but they show where the score helps most. At the \textit{APOE} locus, the pathway layer ranked \textit{APOE} above \textit{TOMM40}, although both carry the same lead variant. Second, a high rank does not by itself identify the causal gene. At the four loci where a neighbouring gene is causal, that gene was identified by experiments showing which gene the risk variant switches on or off, such as \textit{EDN1} at \textit{PHACTR1}, and the score does not use such data. The top lists also contain established drug targets, among them \textit{PCSK9} and \textit{CETP}, consistent with the value of genetic evidence for choosing drug targets \citep{Nelson2015DrugGenetics,Sabatine2017FOURIER}. Thus, the score recovers the known genes of each trait and, where a locus holds several genes, points to those with pathway support.

\subsection*{4.6 Limitations}

Beyond the gene score and sorghum locus, the pathway analysis has four limitations. First, CATFISH controls family-wise error under a null in which no pathway gene is associated with the trait. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) with a nearby causal gene can therefore make a pathway significant even if it contains no causal gene. Significance indicates association, not causation. Second, CATFISH draws null gene statistics from a multivariate normal model rather than permuting phenotypes. In the single sorghum panel and trait tested, per-pathway $p$-values were calibrated, but the family-wise error rate was $0.12$. Use phenotype permutations to check calibration before relying on family-wise $p$-values in a new panel. Third, CATFISH treats gene pairs not reported by MAGMA as uncorrelated. Missing correlations between nearby genes degrade family-wise calibration, so check the correlation file’s coverage. Fourth, simulations used one LD reference panel, ten chromosomes, and five signal types, and compared CATFISH only with MAGMA and preranked GSEA. Results also depend on upstream choices: the variant-to-gene window, LD reference panel, MAGMA gene model, and pathway database \citep{deLeeuw2015}.

\section{6 Conclusion}

Pathway analysis of GWAS summary statistics has two dependence problems, but most work addresses only the easier one. Gene–gene correlation within pathways is routinely modeled; pathway–pathway correlation from shared gene membership is often “corrected” by assuming it away. We add a Westfall–Young max-statistic layer to CATFISH that provides strong family-wise error control under this dependence, uses the same gene–gene correlation model already needed for calibration (no extra estimation), and quantifies how far Bonferroni deviates from the truth for a given database when applied to calibrated statistics.

Two broader results follow. First, computing the omnibus in log space is required at modern GWAS sample sizes; otherwise a silent failure can occur. Second, self-contained pathway testing saturates: in a GWAS of $1.3$ million individuals, $11.2\%$ of genes are genome-wide significant, a random 50-gene set rejects with probability $0.997$, yet strong FWER control leaves $\nLipSigLDL$ of $\nLipNpw$ pathways significant. This reflects the hypothesis, not the correction, and ranking remains informative: plasma lipoprotein assembly/remodeling/clearance leads the LDL-C ordering, lipoprotein pathways are extreme for size in both LDL-C and HDL-C, and gene prioritization recovers known lipid genes better than GWAS association alone. In a sorghum panel with much denser LD and low power, the same procedure finds no significant pathways—as expected for a calibrated method—and a genotype-permutation null shows that centering gene $z$-scores calibrates each pathway while FWER remains above nominal. When few genes are associated, CATFISH’s self-contained question is natural; whether it outperforms competitive tests for weak signals remains open.
